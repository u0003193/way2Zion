    Chapter 23

    Adoption and Communion

    Thesis

    Adoption explains who we are in communion. If union with Christ is the ontological basis by which communion becomes possible, adoption is the relational and filial form that communion takes for the redeemed. Believers are not merely admitted into divine presence as tolerated servants, acquitted strangers, or neutral participants in sacred proximity. They are brought into the Son’s own relationship with the Father. Adoption is therefore not an auxiliary blessing attached to salvation after the main work has already been done. It is one of the most direct names for communion itself: filial participation in the Son’s relation to the Father by the Spirit.

    This chapter argues that adoption must be presented primarily as participation in the Son’s relationship with the Father, filial communion, and belonging. It is not enough to speak of adoption as legal transfer of status, though status is certainly included. The New Testament consistently presents adoption in deeply participatory and relational terms. Believers receive the Spirit of adoption, cry “Abba, Father,” become heirs with Christ, and are drawn into the household, love, and inheritance of the Son. The doctrine concerns identity, address, intimacy, and shared life. It names not only what God declares believers to be, but how they now live before Him.

    This chapter is essential for the larger movement of the book because communion must be more precisely defined. It is possible to speak of access to God, participation in Christ, and indwelling by the Spirit, and still need to ask: in what relation do we stand before God? Adoption gives the answer. We stand before God as sons and daughters in the Son, as those who share in Christ’s filial communion with the Father. This is why adoption belongs so integrally to the whole architecture of the book. Divine speech renews the nous, desire is transformed, humility is born, and obedience becomes worship precisely as the believer learns to live not as alienated slave or legal outsider, but as beloved child in the household of God.

    The chapter therefore answers the prompt’s key question directly: who are we in communion? We are adopted children in the Son, drawn by the Spirit into filial communion with the Father. Adoption is the relational depth of Mercy Seat fulfillment. The God who once met and spoke from above the Mercy Seat now, in Christ, brings His people near not only as pardoned worshipers but as sons and daughters. The Church becomes a filial people. Worship becomes filial worship. Obedience becomes filial obedience. Prayer becomes filial address. Communion becomes belonging.

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

    The primary biblical texts for this chapter include John 1:12–13; John 17:20–26; Romans 8:12–17, 28–39; Galatians 3:23–29; Galatians 4:1–7; Ephesians 1:3–6; 1 John 3:1–3; Hebrews 2:10–18; Hebrews 12:5–11; and Revelation 21:7. These texts are read together with the broader theology of union, participation, and indwelling already developed in previous chapters.

    John 1 declares that those who receive Christ are given authority to become children of God. John 17 reveals Christ’s desire that believers participate in the love and glory shared between Father and Son. Romans 8 and Galatians 4 provide the most sustained apostolic accounts of adoption, the Spirit of sonship, inheritance, and the cry “Abba, Father.” Ephesians 1 presents adoption as grounded in God’s eternal purpose in Christ. First John emphasizes the astonishing love of being called God’s children and the eschatological destiny attached to it. Hebrews links Christ’s priestly solidarity with the sanctified as “brothers” and presents discipline within filial relation. Revelation 21 situates sonship within final inheritance.

    Together these texts establish that adoption is not secondary to communion but one of its deepest relational expressions.

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    Part V is exploring communion and participation from multiple angles. Chapter 22 established union with Christ as the ontological basis for communion. Chapter 25 will later explore abiding in Christ as the ongoing mode by which communion is sustained. Between those realities stands a crucial relational question: what is the identity of those who participate in Christ? Or, as the prompt states it, who are we in communion? The answer is not simply “believers,” “worshipers,” or “those with access,” though all of these are true. The fuller biblical answer is that we are adopted children in the Son.

    This matters because communion with God can be described in ways that remain impersonal or underdeveloped. One might speak of mystical participation, liturgical access, sacramental communion, or doctrinal union without yet naming the familial relation into which believers are brought. The New Testament, however, repeatedly insists that salvation is filial. Christ does not simply open an anonymous nearness to deity. He brings many sons to glory. He gives the Spirit of adoption. He shares His relation to the Father with those joined to Him.

    The chapter must therefore make clear that adoption is not merely a legal metaphor. It certainly includes legal dimensions—transfer, inheritance, naming, household membership. But if the doctrine is left there, its communion-shaping force will be diminished. Scripture goes further. The adopted cry “Abba.” They are loved with the love given to the Son. They are heirs with Christ. They are disciplined as children. They await full resemblance to Him as children of God. Adoption thus shapes not only status but speech, desire, humility, prayer, and hope.

    This chapter stands at a strategically important place in the book because it translates communion into filial terms. If the whole project argues that God created humanity for communion with Himself and restores that communion in Christ, adoption tells us the character of that restored relationship. The redeemed are not merely brought near to holy presence; they are brought home. The theology of communion becomes warm, personal, and covenantally familial here without losing its depth.

    Section Summary: The chapter asks who believers are in communion and argues that adoption names their filial identity as participants in the Son’s own relation to the Father.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From speaking of communion in general terms → to identifying the redeemed as children in the Son, living before God in filial belonging.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Several inadequate accounts of adoption fail to do justice to its theological force.

    One inadequate approach reduces adoption to legal status alone. On this reading adoption means that God reclassifies believers, transferring them into a new standing and granting rights of inheritance. This is true as far as it goes, and it preserves an important forensic and covenantal dimension. But if the account stops there, the relational and participatory depth of adoption is lost. The child becomes a legal beneficiary more than a participant in the Son’s own relation to the Father.

    A second inadequate approach sentimentalizes adoption. Here adoption means primarily that God is loving and believers should feel comforted by divine fatherhood. Again, comfort and affection are real and indispensable. But unless they are grounded in Christological and pneumatological realities, they become vague. Adoption is not a generalized religious reassurance. It is participation in the Son by the Spirit.

    A third inadequate approach makes adoption functionally identical with justification or regeneration. These doctrines are inseparable in the wider economy of salvation, yet they are not interchangeable. Justification addresses righteous standing; regeneration addresses new life; adoption addresses filial belonging. If adoption is swallowed up by other doctrines, the relational specificity of communion is blurred.

    A fourth inadequate approach individualizes adoption excessively. The believer then becomes an isolated child of God with little sense of shared household, ecclesial body, or inheritance with others. But biblical adoption is deeply corporate. The many are brought into one family in the Son. Filial communion is never merely private spirituality.

    A fifth inadequate approach abstracts sonship from the Son. One may speak of divine fatherhood in a general sense without sufficiently emphasizing that believers become children only in Christ. Yet the New Testament is clear: adoption is participation in Christ’s sonship, not an independent relation established alongside Him. He is Son by nature; believers are sons and daughters by grace in Him.

    These inadequate readings all weaken the relational depth of communion. The chapter therefore seeks to recover adoption as filial participation in the Son’s relation to the Father, given by the Spirit and lived in the Church.

    Section Summary: Legal-only, sentimental, conflated, individualistic, and de-Christologized readings all fail to show adoption as filial participation in the Son’s communion with the Father.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From shrinking adoption into status or feeling → to recovering it as the relational heart of communion in Christ.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    Power to Become Children of God

    John 1:12–13 provides a decisive starting point: those who receive the Word made flesh are given authority to become children of God. This immediately ties adoption to the Incarnation. Divine sonship is not a general human possession simply by creation. It is a gift granted in relation to Christ. The Word tabernacled among us so that those who receive Him might enter a new filial standing and life.

    This is crucial because it shows that adoption belongs directly to the logic of Part IV and Part V. The Incarnation draws near, and the result is not only revelation and access but sonship shared by grace.

    The Son’s Prayer and Shared Love

    John 17 intensifies the theme by locating believers within the love between Father and Son. Christ prays that His people may be one, that they may be with Him, and that the love with which the Father has loved Him may be in them. This is one of the chapter’s most important texts because it reveals adoption not merely as external acceptance but as participation in the Son’s own filial communion. Believers are drawn into the sphere of that love.

    This is why adoption must be treated as relational participation. To be a child of God is not merely to be renamed. It is to be welcomed into the love, glory, and nearness shared between Father and Son.

    The Spirit of Adoption in Romans 8

    Romans 8 gives the clearest Pauline formulation. Believers have not received the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but the Spirit of adoption, by whom they cry, “Abba, Father.” The Spirit bears witness with their spirit that they are children of God, and if children, heirs—heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ.

    Several things must be noted. First, adoption is contrasted with slavery and fear. This is theologically decisive for the book because communion is thereby shown to be filial rather than servile. Second, adoption is mediated by the Spirit, who gives both assurance and address. Third, inheritance is christological: believers are heirs with Christ, not independently of Him. Adoption thus includes speech (“Abba”), identity, and future glory.

    This is not a thin doctrine. It shapes the believer’s relation to God at every point.

    Galatians 4 and Filial Participation

    Galatians 4 reinforces the same pattern. The Son is sent, born of a woman, born under the law, to redeem those under the law so that they might receive adoption. Then God sends the Spirit of His Son into their hearts crying, “Abba, Father.” This text is especially rich because it unites Incarnation, redemption, adoption, and the Spirit in one movement. The Son comes so that believers may become sons. The Spirit of the Son is then given so that believers may live that sonship from within.

    This directly supports the chapter’s thesis that adoption is participation in the Son’s relation to the Father. The Spirit does not produce an unrelated filial consciousness. He is the Spirit of the Son, making Christ’s own filial address the believer’s own by grace.

    Chosen for Adoption

    Ephesians 1 adds an eternal and doxological dimension. Believers are chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world and predestined for adoption through Jesus Christ according to the good pleasure of God’s will. Adoption is therefore not accidental or secondary. It belongs to the eternal purpose of God in Christ. This also means that communion is not an afterthought of redemption but its designed goal.

    “See What Kind of Love”

    First John 3 gives the chapter a contemplative and affective depth: “See what kind of love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God; and so we are.” Here adoption is not only doctrinal structure but wonder. Yet the wonder is ontologically grounded—“and so we are.” John also links sonship to eschatological transformation: when Christ appears, we shall be like Him. Adoption thus stretches from present identity to future likeness.

    Christ the Firstborn Among Many Brothers

    Hebrews 2 contributes another crucial strand. Christ is not ashamed to call the sanctified His brothers. He brings many sons to glory. This passage connects priestly solidarity, incarnation, suffering, and filial destination. The Son’s work is not only to open access generically, but to gather a family into glory. Adoption here is inseparable from Christ’s priestly mediation and from the whole communion logic of the book.

    Final Sonship

    Revelation 21:7 gives the eschatological completion: “The one who conquers will have this heritage, and I will be his God and he will be my son.” The covenant formula reaches filial consummation. Adoption begins now, but it flowers in final inheritance.

    Section Summary: Scripture presents adoption as a gift of the Father through the Son by the Spirit, making believers children, heirs, and participants in the Son’s own filial communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From understanding salvation as access without identity → to seeing the redeemed as children in the Son, given speech, love, inheritance, and belonging.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The fathers offer some of the richest resources for understanding adoption as participation in the Son.

    Irenaeus is especially important because his theology of recapitulation and divine sonship consistently ties salvation to Christ’s filial relation to the Father. Humanity is restored not by an external decree only, but by being gathered into the Son who reveals the Father and makes humans children by grace. Adoption, in this frame, is not separate from communion but its familial form.

    Athanasius’s participatory logic is equally decisive. The Son becomes what we are so that we may share, by grace, what belongs to Him by nature. This does not erase the Creator-creature distinction, but it does mean that sonship is shared through union with Christ. Adoption here becomes inseparable from deification rightly understood.

    Cyril of Alexandria strongly supports the chapter by emphasizing the Spirit of the Son. Believers cry “Abba” because they receive the filial Spirit and are incorporated into Christ. The relational intimacy of adoption is therefore pneumatological and christological, not sentimental.

    Augustine contributes significantly by interpreting adoption in connection with grace, charity, and the totus Christus. To become a child of God is to be incorporated into Christ and therefore into His body. This strengthens both the ecclesial and participatory dimensions of the doctrine.

    Maximus the Confessor gives the concept further depth by relating filial participation to transformation and likeness. Sonship is not merely named externally; it is worked out in conformity to Christ. The renewed person grows into what grace has granted.

    The Orthodox tradition more broadly often preserves the intimate connection among union, adoption, and theosis. The believer becomes child by grace in the Son and grows into filial likeness through sacramental and ascetical life. This is deeply consonant with the chapter’s aim.

    Section Summary: Patristic theology reads adoption as grace-given participation in the Son’s own relation to the Father, grounding identity, transformation, and ecclesial belonging.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From seeing adoption as a secondary legal image → to recognizing it as a central patristic account of filial communion with God.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that adoption is the relational dimension of communion because it means participation in the Son’s relationship with the Father by the Spirit. It is filial communion, belonging, inheritance, and shared household life in Christ.

    Several affirmations shape this synthesis.

    First, adoption is christological. Believers are children only in the Son. He is Son by nature; they become sons and daughters by grace through union with Him.

    Second, adoption is pneumatological. The Spirit of the Son enables filial cry, filial confidence, and filial life.

    Third, adoption is participatory. It is not exhausted by status, though status matters. It means entering into the relation of love, nearness, and inheritance proper to Christ.

    Fourth, adoption is ecclesial. The many are made one household and one family in Christ. Communion with God and communion with one another belong together.

    Fifth, adoption is transformative. Children grow into likeness, receive discipline, and are formed for glory.

    Sixth, adoption is eschatological. Present filial communion anticipates final inheritance and unveiled sonship.

    This doctrinal synthesis answers the prompt’s key question. Who are we in communion? We are the Father’s children in the Son by the Spirit. Communion is not faceless nearness. It is filial belonging.

    Section Summary: Adoption names the believer’s filial identity in Christ, showing that communion with God is participation in the Son’s relation to the Father by the Spirit.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From thinking of communion as anonymous access → to understanding it as life as children in the Father’s house.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    Adoption reshapes anthropology by revealing who redeemed human beings are. They are not merely pardoned criminals or restored creatures in a generic sense, though those truths matter. They are children in the Son. This means Christian identity is fundamentally filial. The self is re-named by relation, not by autonomous self-definition.

    This filial identity directly affects the nous. The believer learns to perceive God not merely as judge, ruler, or distant benefactor, but as Father in Christ. This does not weaken reverence; it deepens it with trust. Such perception changes prayer, obedience, and hope. The mind is renewed by learning to inhabit filial truth.

    Desire is likewise transformed. Children seek the Father’s house, the Father’s pleasure, and the Father’s likeness. Adoption therefore reorders love from anxious striving or servile fear toward filial confidence and affection. The believer obeys not to become child, but because he is child.

    Humility is also shaped here in a distinctive way. Adoption excludes pride because sonship is sheer gift. It excludes despair because the gift is secure in Christ. The believer lives not from entitlement but from astonished reception: “See what kind of love…”

    Self-offering and worship also deepen. The child’s life becomes responsive gratitude, not contractual performance. Worship becomes filial approach. The Christian can pray, obey, and suffer as son or daughter in the Son, not as one outside the house seeking admission.

    Section Summary: Adoption gives the believer a filial identity that renews perception, transforms desire, grounds humility, and reshapes worshipful obedience.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From defining the self by guilt or achievement → to receiving identity as beloved child in the Son.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church is the household of adopted children. This means ecclesiology must be read through adoption as well as union. The Church is not only Christ’s body but the Father’s family in the Son by the Spirit. Her worship is therefore familial as well as priestly, covenantal, and heavenly.

    Liturgically, adoption is enacted constantly. The Church prays “Our Father.” She hears the word as children hearing the Father’s voice in Christ. She comes to the table as heirs of the covenant meal. She receives discipline, consolation, instruction, and blessing as members of the household. This does not sentimentalize worship; it situates it. The Church approaches the Holy One through the Son with the Spirit of adoption.

    Ecclesially, adoption also intensifies mutual belonging. The many are siblings in Christ. Rivalry, pride, exclusion, and contempt therefore contradict not only ethics but identity. The Church’s communal life is to bear witness that communion with God creates a family.

    The eschatological dimension remains vital. The Church already lives as the adopted people of God and yet still awaits the full inheritance, likeness, and unveiled family life of the new creation. Thus worship is both home and longing-for-home.

    Section Summary: The Church lives and worships as the household of adopted children, approaching the Father through the Son in the Spirit and learning communal belonging as filial communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From thinking of the Church mainly as institution or assembly → to seeing her as the Father’s family gathered in the Son.

    Practical Outcomes

    First, this chapter helps pastors and teachers present salvation in more personal and relational terms without losing doctrinal depth.

    Second, it strengthens prayer by grounding Christian address to God in the Spirit of adoption rather than in vague spirituality.

    Third, it deepens ecclesiology by showing that the Church is the household of God, not merely a gathering of like-minded believers.

    Fourth, it reshapes discipleship by presenting obedience as filial rather than servile.

    Fifth, it offers strong pastoral comfort by anchoring identity in gifted sonship rather than fluctuating performance.

    Sixth, it supports the book’s larger argument by clarifying the personal and relational meaning of communion.

    Objections & Replies

    Objection 1: Does emphasizing adoption risk minimizing justification?

    Reply: No. It distinguishes without separating. Justification addresses righteous standing; adoption addresses filial belonging. Both are essential, but they are not identical.

    Objection 2: Is “participation in the Son’s relation to the Father” too strong?

    Reply: Not when carefully qualified. Believers participate by grace in what belongs to Christ by nature. The distinction remains, but the participation is real.

    Objection 3: Could filial language sentimentalize God?

    Reply: Only if severed from holiness, cross, and Spirit. Biblically, adoption intensifies reverence by grounding nearness in Christ’s costly mediation.

    Objection 4: Is adoption mainly individual or corporate?

    Reply: Both, but irreducibly corporate as well. The Father does not adopt isolated spiritual consumers, but a family in the Son.

    Conclusion

    Adoption tells us who we are in communion. We are not merely forgiven outsiders allowed to stand in God’s presence. We are not merely servants admitted to sacred proximity. We are children in the Son, brought by the Spirit into the Father’s house. This is the relational heart of communion.

    The chapter has shown that adoption is rooted in Christ, mediated by the Spirit, expressed in filial address, embodied in the Church, and directed toward inheritance. It is not legal fiction, though it includes status. It is not vague comfort, though it brings comfort. It is participation in the Son’s relation to the Father by grace. Through it the whole shape of Christian life is transformed. The believer hears as child, obeys as child, worships as child, and hopes as child.

    This is why adoption is so vital to the larger argument of the book. Communion with God must eventually answer not only where we stand, but who we are there. Adoption gives that answer. It preserves the holiness of God, the mediating necessity of Christ, and the participatory depth of the Spirit while rendering the relation personal and familial. The God who met and spoke from above the Mercy Seat now, in Christ, speaks to His people as Father and receives them as children.

    Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question is clear: in communion we are the adopted children of God in the Son. That identity is the relational form of belonging. It is the warm center of theological participation. And it is the ground from which the believer can move forward into renewed desire, deepened humility, filial obedience, and covenantal worship on the way to Zion.
