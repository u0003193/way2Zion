    Chapter 6 Excursus

    Hypostatic Attitude

    Thesis

    If Chapter 6 established that divine speech is the heart of covenant communion, this excursus asks what kind of person is capable of receiving such speech fruitfully. The question is not whether God speaks, but what personal posture is required if His word is to become transformative rather than merely audible. Divine speech can be externally heard and yet inwardly resisted, misinterpreted, trivialized, or hardened against. Scripture itself bears repeated witness to this tragedy. Therefore this excursus argues that the reception of divine speech requires what may be called a hypostatic attitude: the personal mode of receptivity, dependence, trust, attention, and fidelity by which the human person stands before God as one addressed, one taught, one judged, one loved, and one summoned into obedience.

    The phrase “hypostatic attitude” is used here not to introduce an alien abstraction, but to name a concrete theological reality. Hypostasis refers to the person in his irreducible concreteness. Attitude, in this context, does not mean mood, passing disposition, or psychological self-management. It means the deep orientation of the person—the habitual and graced manner in which one receives reality and responds to God. The issue is therefore not simply whether one agrees with divine truth in the abstract, but whether one exists before God in a posture of receptive fidelity. Such a posture includes humility, but humility must be understood rightly. It is not first a preliminary qualification by which one earns access to God. It is the fruit of encounter, the consequence of communion, and the truthful response to divine speech that reorders the self.

    This excursus therefore serves as the anthropological bridge between hearing, transformation, and obedience. Hearing does not become transformative merely because sounds have entered the ear. The word of God must be received in a personal mode shaped by dependence and trust. Such hearing requires attention, because distraction dissipates presence; fidelity, because the word must be kept; and receptivity, because divine speech is gift before it becomes demand. The argument of the chapter is that covenant hearing is always hypostatic hearing: the whole person receives the word, consents to it, is humbled by it, and is gradually formed into obedience through it.

    This discussion is especially necessary for the wider architecture of the book. The controlling thesis states that in communion God speaks, divine speech renews the nous, and the renewed nous transforms desire. But that movement presupposes more than the objective reality of speech. It presupposes a personal posture capable of being renewed by what is heard. Without such a posture, hearing remains external and transformation does not take root. With it, hearing becomes inhabitable, communion becomes formative, and obedience becomes the grateful response of a person reconstituted by divine truth.

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

    The primary biblical texts for this excursus include Exodus 3:1–6; 1 Samuel 3:1–10; Psalm 25; Psalm 131; Isaiah 6:1–8; Luke 1:26–38; Luke 8:4–15; Luke 10:38–42; Luke 24:45; John 10:1–18; John 15:1–11; Romans 12:1–2; James 1:19–25; Hebrews 3:7–15; and Revelation 3:20. These texts are read together with the governing passages for the whole project, especially Exodus 25:22, Numbers 7:89, Hebrews 10:19–22, and Hebrews 12:22–24.

    Exodus 3 and Isaiah 6 show that the human response to divine speech is marked by reverent self-displacement and readiness. First Samuel 3 presents the pattern of receptive listening in Samuel’s answer, “Speak, Lord, for your servant hears.” Luke 1 reveals in Mary the deepest covenantal receptivity: “Let it be to me according to your word.” Luke 8 shows that hearing must involve retained, persevering reception if the word is to bear fruit. James 1 insists that believers must be hearers who become doers. Hebrews warns against hearing with a hardened heart. John 10 and John 15 show that hearing is bound up with personal belonging, abiding, and obedience. Revelation 3:20 combines divine initiative and personal opening in a striking image of communion and response.

    These texts show that Scripture does not imagine hearing as neutral cognition. Divine speech is personally received or resisted. The state of the person matters. The word of God seeks not only comprehension, but habitation.

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    The previous chapter argued that God’s presence is speaking presence and that covenant life is fundamentally dialogical. That claim now presses a further question: what kind of human posture corresponds to a God who speaks? If the covenant is dialogical, then the human person cannot be understood merely as an occasional listener or a rational processor of information. The person must become receptive in a deeper sense. The issue is not only what is said, but how it is heard. Why does the same divine speech soften one heart, expose another, convert a third, and harden a fourth? Why do some hearers receive the word with fidelity while others remain externally religious but inwardly closed?

    The answer cannot be reduced to sheer moral distinction, though moral life matters. Nor can it be reduced to psychological temperament, though temperament plays its creaturely role. The deeper issue is personal orientation. The self stands before God in some posture: guarded or open, proud or yielding, distracted or attentive, controlling or dependent, suspicious or trusting. That posture profoundly affects how divine speech is received. This is what the excursus names as hypostatic attitude.

    The phrase may sound technical, but it points to a deeply scriptural reality. The Bible repeatedly presents individuals whose entire person is disclosed in their response to God’s word. Samuel listens. Pharaoh hardens. Mary receives. Israel hears but forgets. Peter confesses and is corrected. Martha is anxious and distracted; Mary sits and attends. The prophets tremble, protest, submit, or flee. In each case, divine speech is not merely content but encounter, and encounter reveals the person’s mode of being.

    This excursus is therefore necessary because hearing without a corresponding account of personhood can remain too thin. One may emphasize the objectivity of revelation and the centrality of proclamation, yet fail to explain how the human person is inwardly positioned in relation to that revelation. But the wider argument of the book requires precisely such an explanation. If divine speech renews the nous, transforms desire, and produces obedience, then some account must be given of how the person receives, keeps, and yields to the word. Hypostatic attitude supplies that bridge.

    Section Summary: The excursus asks what personal posture corresponds to divine speech and argues that hearing becomes transformative only when the person stands before God in a graced mode of receptivity, trust, and fidelity.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From treating hearing as mere reception of content → to understanding it as personal encounter shaped by the deep orientation of the self.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Several common ways of describing spiritual receptivity fail to do justice to the biblical and theological depth of the matter.

    One inadequate account reduces receptivity to psychology. In this view, openness to God’s word is interpreted mainly in terms of temperament, emotional safety, self-awareness, regulation, or interior calm. While these may influence how a person listens, they do not reach the theological center. A person may be psychologically reflective and yet spiritually resistant. Another may be emotionally turbulent yet profoundly obedient. What is decisive is not inner composure alone, but the deeper personal stance before God. Receptivity is not the same as emotional availability.

    A second inadequate account treats humility as chiefly a prerequisite that the individual must generate before God will draw near. Such a view often has a superficially pious appearance, but it subtly distorts grace. It can suggest that the creature must first make himself sufficiently low before participation may begin. Scripture certainly condemns pride and commends lowliness, but the deeper biblical pattern is that true humility often arises from encounter. Isaiah cries “Woe is me” in the temple vision. Peter says “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man” in the presence of Christ. Mary’s fiat emerges from divine address. Humility is frequently the fruit of being seen and addressed by God.

    A third inadequate account reduces obedience to willpower. Here hearing is acknowledged, but the crucial work is thought to lie in moral execution. Yet if Chapter 6 is correct, hearing itself is formative. The problem is not only that people disobey what they know, but that they fail to receive the word personally and deeply enough for it to renew the nous and reorder desire. Willpower without hypostatic receptivity produces brittle obedience, self-righteousness, or discouragement.

    A fourth inadequate account understands receptivity as passive quietism. On this model, one waits for God to act while minimizing attention, fidelity, vigilance, or active keeping of the word. But biblical hearing is not inert. Mary’s receptivity leads to costly participation. Samuel’s listening leads to prophetic vocation. James insists that hearing must become doing. Receptivity is therefore active in a responsive sense. It is not self-assertion, but it is also not inert collapse.

    A fifth inadequate account spiritualizes fidelity into private inwardness detached from ecclesial and liturgical life. But covenant hearing in Scripture occurs within a people. The posture required is personal, yet never merely private. The hearer is formed by liturgy, communal memory, proclamation, sacrament, and the shared life of the Church. Hypostatic attitude is personal, but it is ecclesially schooled.

    These reductions fail because they fragment what Scripture holds together: receptivity, dependence, trust, attention, humility, and obedience all belong to the person’s response to divine truth. The excursus must therefore develop a thicker account in which hearing becomes transformative through a deeply personal and graced stance before God.

    Section Summary: Psychological, voluntarist, quietistic, and overly privatized accounts of receptivity fail because they do not adequately connect humility, attention, dependence, and obedience to the person’s concrete standing before God.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From thin accounts of listening as feeling, effort, or passivity → to a theological account of personal receptivity shaped by grace and covenant response.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    “Speak, Lord, for Your Servant Hears”

    First Samuel 3 offers one of Scripture’s clearest portraits of receptive hearing. Samuel is called repeatedly by the Lord and, under Eli’s guidance, finally answers, “Speak, Lord, for your servant hears.” Several elements are decisive. Samuel does not seize revelation; he receives it. His hearing is attentive, obedient, and self-situating. He names himself servant. The phrase contains dependence, receptivity, and readiness in a single response. This is not mere auditory function. It is a personal stance before God.

    Samuel’s answer illustrates the thesis of this excursus. Divine speech is received fruitfully where the person adopts a mode of existence that is teachable, responsive, and yielded. The servant-hearer is not annihilated as person; rather, he becomes more fully personal by standing rightly in relation to the speaking God.

    Holy Encounter and the Birth of Humility

    Exodus 3 and Isaiah 6 reveal that humility often arises from encounter rather than precedes it. Moses removes his sandals because the place has become holy through divine presence and address. Isaiah beholds the Lord high and lifted up and only then cries, “Woe is me!” In both cases, divine holiness and divine speech expose the truth of the creature. Humility appears as the response to being addressed by the Holy One.

    This pattern matters greatly because it corrects moralistic distortions. The point is not that humility is unnecessary before God; Scripture elsewhere plainly condemns pride. The point is that true humility is not chiefly self-manufactured posture. It is awakened by truth. The person who encounters God’s speaking holiness cannot remain self-central. Divine speech reveals who God is and who the hearer is. Humility is therefore a fruit of communion and a response to divine truth.

    Mary and the Fiat of Receptive Participation

    Luke 1 presents perhaps the most luminous biblical image of hypostatic receptivity. When Gabriel announces the divine purpose, Mary asks, receives clarification, and responds: “Behold, I am the servant of the Lord; let it be to me according to your word.” Her response is not passive resignation. It is trusting, intelligent, faithful consent. Here dependence, receptivity, attention, fidelity, and humility are all present. Mary is not reduced to instrumentality; she participates personally and freely in the divine word.

    Her fiat is deeply instructive for this excursus because it shows that receptivity is not opposed to action or agency. On the contrary, it is the highest form of creaturely participation. The person becomes capable of bearing the word by receiving it. Mary stands as the great biblical witness that hearing becomes fruitful when the hypostasis says yes to God.

    The Soil of the Heart

    Luke 8 develops the same theme through the parable of the sower. The decisive issue is not the goodness of the seed but the state of the soil. Some hear and lose the word quickly; some receive it with joy but have no root; some are choked by cares, riches, and pleasures; and some hear, hold fast the word in an honest and good heart, and bear fruit with patience. This is one of the clearest biblical statements that hearing must be personal and persevering if transformation is to occur.

    The language of “holding fast” and “bearing fruit with patience” points directly toward the concerns of this excursus. Hearing requires fidelity. Attention must be sustained. The word must be kept, not merely admired. The condition of the heart matters—not in a voluntaristic sense that makes grace secondary, but in the sense that the person must receive and continue in what God gives.

    Mary and Martha: Attention as Personal Posture

    Luke 10:38–42 reinforces the role of attention. Martha is “anxious and troubled about many things,” while Mary sits at the Lord’s feet and listens to His word. Jesus does not condemn service as such, but He reveals that the deeper issue is inward posture. Anxiety fragments attention. Receptivity gathers it. Mary’s listening becomes the image of the personal attitude proper to divine speech.

    This passage is especially important in a modern context because it shows that hearing is not merely a matter of theological correctness but of existential availability. One may be busy with many good things and still fail to receive the one thing necessary. Hypostatic attitude therefore includes attention as a mode of being present to the Word.

    Be Quick to Hear, Slow to Speak

    James 1 brings hearing, humility, and obedience into explicit connection. Believers are to be “quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger,” and to “receive with meekness the implanted word, which is able to save your souls.” The word “implanted” is particularly significant. Divine speech is meant not simply to strike the ear but to take root within the person. This requires meekness—again, not as self-abasement for its own sake, but as the openness that allows the word to dwell and work.

    James then warns against being hearers only and not doers. Obedience flows from hearing where the word is received meekly. The anthropological bridge between hearing and doing is thus not willpower but the implanted word in a receptive person.

    “If You Hear His Voice”

    Hebrews 3–4 gives the negative counterpart. “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.” Hearing can be refused. The same divine speech that offers life becomes the occasion for hardening when the person closes himself. This confirms that hypostatic attitude matters deeply. Receptivity is not automatic. The covenant people must remain soft-hearted, trusting, and attentive. The opposite of this is not merely ignorance but hardness.

    Taken together, these texts show that divine speech is personally received. Scripture repeatedly portrays the hearer’s stance—servant-like, meek, attentive, trusting, patient, or hardened—as decisive in the movement from word to transformation.

    Section Summary: Biblical patterns of hearing show that God’s word becomes fruitful only where the person receives it with servant-like receptivity, attentive fidelity, meekness, and soft-hearted trust.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From hearing as external auditory event → to hearing as personal assent and yielded presence before the speaking God.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The patristic tradition provides rich resources for describing the personal posture required to receive divine speech. Although the specific phrase “hypostatic attitude” is not standard in the fathers, the reality it names is deeply consonant with their theology of personhood, obedience, humility, and noetic formation.

    Irenaeus is again instructive in his emphasis on divine pedagogy and childlike receptivity. Humanity matures by learning to receive from God. The creature’s proper posture is not self-assertive autonomy but filial dependence. This does not diminish personhood; it fulfills it. The one who receives becomes capable of true growth.

    Athanasius reinforces the same pattern christologically. The Word becomes human not simply to speak at humanity from outside, but to heal the human mode of hearing and obedience from within. In Christ, the perfectly receptive and obedient human life appears. He is not merely the teacher of receptive hearing but its incarnate form. To be conformed to Christ is therefore to learn the human attitude proper to the Father’s word.

    Maximus the Confessor is especially useful because he joins anthropology, noetics, desire, and personal movement. For Maximus, the human person is not merely defined by faculties but by the mode in which those faculties are directed. The gnomic instability of fallen life means that the person vacillates, appropriates, resists, and misuses what is given. Grace does not erase personal willing; it heals and aligns it. This offers an important analogue for the idea of hypostatic attitude. The issue is not the existence of a human person, but the mode in which the person stands toward God—whether scattered, self-enclosed, and resistant, or gathered, obedient, and receptive.

    The ascetical tradition, especially in the Desert Fathers and later Philokalic writers, also contributes insight regarding attention and meekness. True hearing requires stillness of heart, sobriety, and freedom from the tyranny of immediate reaction. Yet this should not be confused with self-generated technique. The fathers repeatedly insist that humility, prayer, and dependence upon grace are indispensable. Watchfulness and receptivity belong together.

    Orthodox theology broadly preserves a particularly helpful intuition here: obedience is personal communion before it is external compliance. The saint is not merely one who follows commands, but one whose whole person has become transparent to divine life. Such transparency includes attentiveness, trust, and humble fidelity. The Church’s hymnody concerning the Theotokos especially exemplifies this. Mary is honored not simply because she complied, but because in her whole personal stance she received the word and bore Christ. Her response is paradigmatic for covenant receptivity.

    Thus the fathers support several dimensions of this excursus. First, receptivity is personal, not merely functional. Second, humility is awakened and deepened in encounter. Third, obedience becomes authentic when it grows from a person inwardly aligned with divine speech. Fourth, Christ Himself is the measure and healing of this human attitude.

    Section Summary: Patristic theology confirms that the reception of divine speech depends on a healed personal mode of dependence, attentiveness, and obedient openness, modeled supremely in Christ and exemplified in Mary.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From generic spiritual openness → to a theologically precise vision of personal receptivity healed and patterned by Christ.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The doctrinal resolution of this excursus can now be stated clearly: the reception of divine speech requires a hypostatic attitude, that is, a personal mode of receptivity, dependence, trust, attention, and fidelity by which the human person stands before God in communion and is thereby formed for obedience. This attitude is not a natural self-generated disposition, nor a purely psychological state, nor a passive vacancy. It is a graced form of personhood awakened by encounter with God and shaped through participation in Christ by the Spirit.

    This synthesis includes several interrelated claims.

    First, divine speech is addressed to persons, not merely to rational faculties. God does not speak only to the intellect or only to moral will. He addresses the whole person. Therefore the whole person must respond. Hearing is hypostatic because it involves the concrete self in relation to God.

    Second, receptivity is constitutive of creaturely truth. Human beings are not less personal when they receive; they are more fully themselves. Dependence upon God is not a defect but the truth of creaturehood. The person who hears rightly does not lose agency; he receives it back in proper order.

    Third, trust is essential because divine speech is relational. One cannot hear the voice of the Shepherd while fundamentally refusing His goodness. Suspicion closes the heart. Faith opens it. Trust is therefore not an optional emotional supplement; it is intrinsic to covenant hearing.

    Fourth, attention is necessary because the word of God must be attended to personally if it is to become formative. Distraction is not morally neutral when it becomes a settled refusal of presence. A scattered person may hear externally yet fail inwardly to receive.

    Fifth, fidelity completes hearing. The word must be kept, remembered, inhabited, and obeyed. Receptivity without fidelity becomes sentiment. Fidelity without receptivity becomes legalism. Hypostatic attitude holds the two together.

    Sixth, humility is primarily a fruit of encounter and communion. It is not best described as a prerequisite that earns access. Rather, divine truth displaces illusion. The person who stands before the speaking God becomes truthful about God, self, and world. This truthfulness is humility. It then further deepens receptivity and obedience.

    These claims together provide the bridge demanded by the book’s argument. Hearing leads to transformation only where the person receives the word in trust and attentiveness. Transformation leads to obedience only where the person’s will and desire are reshaped in fidelity. Thus hypostatic attitude is not an optional refinement. It is the personal posture by which communion becomes formative.

    Section Summary: The excursus resolves the issue by defining hypostatic attitude as the graced personal posture in which divine speech is truly received, retained, and embodied.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From viewing formation as the automatic effect of hearing → to seeing hearing become transformative through a receptive, attentive, faithful personal stance before God.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    This excursus carries substantial anthropological implications. It reinforces the claim that the human person is not merely a bundle of faculties but a concrete and relational self whose mode of being before God matters profoundly. The same word may be heard by many, but not every person hears it in the same way. The difference is not reducible to intelligence, temperament, or discipline alone. It lies in the deeper personal posture toward God.

    This helps explain why divine speech can renew the nous. The mind is not transformed by content alone, but by content received personally in truth. Where the self is defended, proud, scattered, or suspicious, the word may remain external. Where the self becomes receptive, dependent, and trusting, the word can begin to take root. The implanted word in James is not magical. It becomes fruitful where the person receives it with meekness.

    The excursus also clarifies the relation between humility and transformation. Humility is often misunderstood either as psychological smallness or as spiritual self-denigration. But if humility is the fruit of encounter, then it is best understood as truthful self-location before God. The humble person is not the one who despises himself, but the one who no longer needs to maintain false centrality. Divine speech dec enters the ego. The person becomes free to obey because he is no longer busy preserving an illusion of autonomy.

    This posture also shapes desire. A receptive person is teachable in love. He can be drawn, corrected, and reoriented. A resistant person, by contrast, may hear commands and even admire truths while remaining inwardly unformed. Thus hypostatic attitude is one of the hidden conditions of sanctification. It does not replace grace; it is grace’s personal habitation.

    Spiritually, this discussion emphasizes dependence. The hearer cannot master divine speech as object. He must receive it as one who needs life from outside himself. Such dependence is not humiliating in the negative sense; it is liberating. It frees the person from self-sufficiency and opens him to communion. This is why trust and fidelity are so closely linked. One remains with the word only if one believes the Speaker is good.

    The modern relevance is substantial. Contemporary people are often habituated into postures opposite to hypostatic receptivity: suspicion, distraction, self-curation, defensiveness, ironic distance, and constant self-assertion. Such habits are spiritually corrosive. They make true hearing difficult. This excursus therefore offers not merely a theoretical clarification but a pastoral diagnosis. To hear God well, the person must be healed in posture.

    Section Summary: The person’s deep stance before God determines whether divine speech remains external or becomes inwardly transformative, making receptivity and humility central to sanctification.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From thinking transformation depends mainly on better information or stronger effort → to recognizing the necessity of a healed personal posture before divine truth.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The personal posture described here is not cultivated in isolation. It is formed within the Church’s liturgical, sacramental, scriptural, and communal life. The Church teaches hypostatic receptivity by teaching her members how to stand, kneel, listen, confess, answer, wait, and receive. These are not merely ritual behaviors. They are embodied pedagogies of personhood.

    The liturgy is especially important because it repeatedly enacts the proper relation between divine initiative and human response. God speaks in Scripture and proclamation. The people answer in acclamation, prayer, confession, creed, thanksgiving, and song. The structure of worship itself trains the faithful to exist as hearers. Over time, this repeated form begins to heal the self from reactive autonomy and to teach the gentle strength of receptive fidelity.

    The sacramental life likewise belongs here. In baptism, chrismation, Eucharist, absolution, and blessing, the Christian learns that divine life is received. Nothing is self-generated. The sacraments therefore school dependence. They do not bypass hearing, but intensify its covenantal texture: the God who speaks is the God who gives Himself.

    Ecclesial community is also essential because fidelity to the word is tested in shared life. One can imagine oneself receptive in private. It is harder to maintain such illusions when one must forgive, submit, bear burdens, confess sins, receive correction, and remain in communion with difficult neighbors. The Church becomes the concrete sphere where hearing becomes obedience and humility becomes visible.

    The Orthodox tradition’s attention to postures of prayer, stillness, reverence, and obedient listening offers particular insight here. Bodily and liturgical attitudes are not arbitrary. They are meant to express and form the deeper hypostatic stance of receptivity. Yet they must never be treated as self-sufficient externals. Their truth lies in their ability to serve the inward posture of trustful hearing.

    Finally, this ecclesial formation is eschatological. The Church trains persons now for the perfected communion of Zion. Those who learn to hear, receive, and keep the word are being prepared for the day when hearing becomes seeing. Hypostatic attitude is therefore not merely a present moral concern. It is the personal shape of pilgrimage toward the unveiled presence of God.

    Section Summary: The Church’s liturgical and communal life forms the person in receptive fidelity, teaching believers to become hearers whose obedience grows from shared participation in divine speech.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From imagining receptivity as private inward disposition only → to seeing it as ecclesially and liturgically formed personhood on the way to Zion.

      A rich witness to attention, humility, obedience, and the practical formation of the hearer before God.

    5. Symeon the New Theologian. The Discourses.
      Useful for the relation between encounter, humility, inner receptivity, and the personal appropriation of divine life.

      Helpful for connecting sacramental reception, personal participation, and the concrete shape of Christian life in the Church.

    Practical Outcomes

    First, this excursus teaches pastors and teachers to address not only what believers know but how they stand before the word. The question is not merely whether the truth has been stated, but whether hearers are being formed in receptivity.

    Second, it deepens preaching. Sermons should not only explain content but summon persons into a posture of hearing marked by trust, attention, and response.

    Third, it refines spiritual direction and pastoral care. Many struggles with obedience are not only problems of will but of posture—defensiveness, distraction, fear, suspicion, or self-protectiveness before God.

    Fourth, it enriches liturgical catechesis. The Church can teach bodily and verbal responses in worship as expressions of the deeper covenantal attitude proper to hearers.

    Fifth, it corrects misunderstandings of humility. Believers should be taught that humility is not self-hatred or merely a condition for divine favor, but a fruit of encountering the truth of God.

    Sixth, it offers a pastoral antidote to modern fragmentation. In a culture of constant distraction and self-curation, hypostatic receptivity becomes a form of spiritual sanity.

    Objections & Replies

    Objection 1: “Hypostatic attitude” sounds too technical and abstract for practical theology.

    Reply: The phrase is technical, but the reality is concrete. It names the deep personal posture of a creature before the speaking God. Scripture constantly describes such realities, even if it uses different vocabulary.

    Objection 2: Emphasizing posture may make transformation seem dependent on human preparation rather than grace.

    Reply: The excursus explicitly resists that. Grace is primary. The posture itself is awakened and healed by divine encounter. Yet grace does not bypass the person; it forms and summons the person into responsive participation.

    Objection 3: If humility is mainly a fruit of encounter, does that weaken the biblical call to humble oneself?

    Reply: No. It clarifies it. The call to humility remains real, but the chapter insists that authentic humility is sustained not by self-preoccupation but by truthful relation to God. One humbles oneself most truly by yielding to divine truth.

    Objection 4: Does this account overemphasize inwardness?

    Reply: It would, if isolated from Church, sacrament, worship, and obedience. But the chapter repeatedly places hypostatic attitude within liturgical, ecclesial, and embodied life. The inward posture is real precisely because it becomes outwardly faithful.

    Conclusion

    The God who speaks seeks not merely listeners, but hearers—persons who receive His word in trust, dependence, attention, and fidelity. This excursus has argued that such reception requires what may be called a hypostatic attitude: the deep personal posture by which the concrete self stands before God as one addressed and one summoned. Without this posture, divine speech may be admired, analyzed, or resisted, but it will not readily become transformative. With it, the word of God can take root, renew the nous, reshape desire, and give rise to obedience.

    This account also clarifies humility. Humility is not best understood as a spiritual toll one must pay before entering communion. It is the fruit of encounter, the consequence of divine truth, the response of the creature who has stood before the Holy One and has been re-centered by that meeting. The person becomes humble because God has spoken and thereby revealed reality. This humility then deepens receptivity and prepares the way for further obedience.

    As an excursus following the chapter on divine speech, this chapter serves as the necessary anthropological bridge between hearing, transformation, and obedience. It shows how the word moves from external proclamation into inward formation. It explains why attention matters, why trust matters, why fidelity matters, and why the person cannot remain spiritually neutral before divine address.

    In the life of the Church, this hypostatic attitude is schooled through word, sacrament, worship, prayer, and shared obedience. The believer learns to say with Samuel, “Speak, Lord, for your servant hears,” and with Mary, “Let it be to me according to your word.” These are not merely exemplary phrases from biblical history. They are the personal form of covenant hearing. And where that form is learned, communion becomes more habitable, transformation more stable, and obedience more truthful. Such persons are being prepared already for Zion, where the hearer shall at last behold the One whose voice has formed him all along.
