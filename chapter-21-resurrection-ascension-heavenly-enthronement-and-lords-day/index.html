    Chapter 21

    Resurrection, Ascension, Heavenly Enthronement, and Lord’s Day

    Thesis

    The risen and ascended Christ continues to bring His people into communion with God because His work is not only accomplished in the past but actively mediated in the present. The resurrection is the beginning of new creation. The ascension is the priestly entrance of the Son into the heavenly sanctuary. His enthronement is the ongoing reign and mediation by which He governs, gathers, and intercedes for His people. The Lord’s Day is the Church’s weekly participation in resurrection reality, and heavenly worship is not merely future expectation but present covenantal access through the Spirit. Christ’s finished work is therefore not static completion left behind in history. It is living completion, present efficacy, and continuing mediation.

    This chapter argues that resurrection, ascension, enthronement, and Lord’s Day belong together as one coherent extension of Christ’s Mercy Seat fulfillment. The Incarnation drew near. The Eucharist instituted anamnetic participation. The torn veil opened access. The christological synthesis of Chapter 20 showed that Christ fulfills Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, temple, and Mercy Seat. Chapter 21 now asks how that fulfilled work is presently operative. How does the ascended Christ continue to bring His people into communion with God? The answer is that He does so by living, reigning, mediating, and gathering them into His heavenly life.

    This chapter must therefore show more than the fact of resurrection or the doctrinal correctness of ascension. It must demonstrate their teleology. Resurrection matters because in it the humanity of Christ is raised into incorruptible new creation. Ascension matters because the incarnate Son enters the heavenly holy place as priest and forerunner. Enthronement matters because the risen Lord reigns from the Father’s right hand in active mediation. The Lord’s Day matters because it is not merely commemorative scheduling, but the Church’s recurring entrance into resurrection-shaped worship under the living Christ. Heavenly worship matters because the Church’s liturgy and communion are anchored in the present reign of the ascended Lord.

    The chapter therefore answers the prompt’s key question directly: how does the ascended Christ continue to bring His people into communion with God? He does so by remaining the living mediator of access, by interceding in the heavenly sanctuary, by pouring out the Spirit, by ruling His Church, by speaking through His word, and by gathering His people into participation in His resurrection life and heavenly worship. In Him, finished work and ongoing participation are not opposites. They belong together. Christ has accomplished everything necessary for communion, and as the risen and ascended one He actively brings His people into the benefits and reality of that communion now.

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

    The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Luke 24:36–53; Acts 1:1–11; Acts 2:1–36; Ephesians 1:15–23; Colossians 3:1–4; Hebrews 1:1–4; Hebrews 4:14–16; Hebrews 6:19–20; Hebrews 7:23–28; Hebrews 8:1–2; Hebrews 9:24; Hebrews 10:19–25; Revelation 1:9–20; Revelation 4–5; and Revelation 21–22. These are read together with Psalm 110, Psalm 118:22–24, and the wider sanctuary and Zion theology already developed in the book.

    Luke 24 and Acts 1 establish the resurrection and ascension as bodily, historical, and covenantally significant events. Acts 2 interprets Christ’s exaltation in relation to Pentecost and the gift of the Spirit. Ephesians 1 presents Christ exalted above all rule and power and given as head to the Church. Hebrews interprets ascension as priestly entrance into the heavenly sanctuary and ongoing mediation. Revelation 1, 4, and 5 display the risen Lord in heavenly glory and worship, while the Lord’s Day appears as the temporal context in which John receives his apocalyptic vision. Revelation 21–22 gives the final horizon of consummated communion.

    These texts together show that the ascended Christ is not absent from the Church but actively mediating access, participation, and heavenly worship.

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    Part IV of the book has been tracing how Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat reality in progressively unfolding ways. Chapter 17 showed that in the Incarnation the Word tabernacled among us, making communion present in principle. Chapter 18 argued that anamnesis is the Church’s covenantal participation in Christ’s accomplished work. Chapter 19 demonstrated that the torn veil means opened access into God’s presence. Chapter 20 gathered the old covenant institutions into one christological synthesis. The present chapter now addresses an indispensable further question: if Christ has accomplished everything necessary for communion, how does His accomplished work continue to be effective for the Church now?

    This question matters because Christian theology is always vulnerable to two opposite distortions. One is to emphasize Christ’s finished work in such a way that His present priestly and royal activity becomes practically muted. The other is to speak so strongly of present spiritual experience that the completed and once-for-all character of His historical work is weakened. Scripture resists both. Christ’s work is finished, and Christ is alive. He is seated, and He is active. He has offered Himself once for all, and He always lives to intercede. He has ascended bodily into heaven, and He remains the living mediator of His people’s communion with God.

    The chapter must therefore bridge finished work and ongoing participation. This is exactly the concern named in the prompt. The resurrection, ascension, enthronement, and Lord’s Day are not separate topics added after the cross. They explain how the once-for-all accomplishment of Christ becomes the living environment of the Church. The resurrection inaugurates new creation life. The ascension locates the incarnate Son in the heavenly sanctuary. Enthronement identifies Him as the reigning and speaking Lord. The Lord’s Day becomes the Church’s temporal participation in that reality, while heavenly worship becomes the unseen but real liturgical sphere into which the Church is drawn.

    The issue, then, is not whether Christ is alive in a generic devotional sense, but how the risen and ascended Christ continues actively to bring His people into communion. The chapter argues that He does so through His heavenly priesthood, His sovereign reign, His gift of the Spirit, and His gathering of the Church into resurrection-shaped worship. Without this chapter, the communion theology of the book might remain too focused on historical accomplishment. With it, the reader sees how that accomplishment is presently lived and shared.

    Section Summary: The chapter asks how Christ’s accomplished work remains actively effective for the Church and argues that resurrection, ascension, enthronement, and Lord’s Day together explain His living mediation of communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From thinking of Christ’s work as finished-and-past only → to seeing it as finished, living, and presently mediating communion.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Several inadequate approaches obscure the theological force of resurrection, ascension, and enthronement.

    One inadequate approach treats the resurrection primarily as proof that Jesus is right or that death is defeated, without integrating it into new creation and communion. Those claims are true, but partial. The resurrection is not mere validation. It is the beginning of the humanity that can now live beyond death in incorruptible fellowship with God.

    A second inadequate approach regards the ascension mainly as Jesus’ departure. On this reading ascension means absence, and heavenly session becomes little more than heavenly waiting. But Hebrews, Acts, and Ephesians do not allow such passivity. The ascended Christ is active: interceding, reigning, pouring out the Spirit, and bringing His people near.

    A third inadequate approach limits enthronement to royal sovereignty in abstraction from priesthood and communion. Christ reigns, certainly, but if this reign is treated only politically or cosmically, its sanctuary dimension may be obscured. Yet the one seated at the right hand is also the one who entered as priest and forerunner. His enthronement is not detached dominion; it is priestly kingship for the sake of access and communion.

    A fourth inadequate approach treats the Lord’s Day merely as a commemorative marker of resurrection. Commemoration is certainly involved, but if that is all, the ecclesial and participatory depth of the day is lost. Revelation 1 and the Church’s liturgical tradition suggest that the Lord’s Day is not empty chronology but the appointed temporal sphere of gathered resurrection life and hearing.

    A fifth inadequate approach confines heavenly worship to future eschatology. Revelation then becomes mainly a preview of the end, while the Church’s present worship remains merely earthly. But Hebrews 12 and Revelation together imply something stronger: the Church already participates in heavenly Zion and worship. The communion is anticipatory but real.

    These distortions fail because they fragment what Scripture holds together: risen life, priestly presence, royal reign, Spirit-gift, liturgical gathering, and heavenly worship all serve one reality—ongoing communion in the ascended Christ.

    Section Summary: Reductive readings of resurrection, ascension, enthronement, Lord’s Day, and heavenly worship fail when they isolate proof, absence, sovereignty, commemoration, or futurity from communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From partial post-Easter doctrines → to a unified theology of Christ’s living mediation and the Church’s present participation.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    Resurrection as the Beginning of New Creation

    The resurrection is not merely a reversal of death. It is the beginning of the new creation in the humanity of Christ. The risen Lord is not resuscitated into old mortality but raised into transformed life, incorruptible and death-defeating. Luke 24, John 20, and Paul’s resurrection theology all insist on the bodily reality of this event. This matters deeply for communion theology because the humanity that was assumed in the Incarnation and offered in sacrifice is now glorified and alive before the Father.

    In Christ’s resurrection, access is no longer only an abstract possibility. A human life now exists beyond death and judgment in perfect communion with God. The second Adam stands where the first failed. The exile from Eden is not merely countered by forgiveness; it is answered by a human being restored and exalted in life before God. New creation has begun.

    Ascension as Priestly Entrance

    Acts 1 presents the ascension as the bodily exaltation of Christ into heaven. Hebrews interprets that movement theologically. The ascended Christ is not simply relocated. He enters the heavenly sanctuary as high priest, forerunner, and mediator. Hebrews 6 calls Him the forerunner who has entered within the veil. Hebrews 9 says He appears in the presence of God for us. Hebrews 8 identifies Him as minister in the true tent set up by the Lord.

    This is the central sanctuary claim of the chapter. Ascension is priestly entrance. The incarnate, crucified, risen Son now stands in the Father’s presence as the living mediator of communion. The Day of Atonement pattern is not only fulfilled in historical sacrifice, but in ongoing heavenly priesthood. Christ’s ascension therefore answers the prompt’s demand that the chapter show priestly entrance into the heavenly sanctuary.

    Enthronement as Ongoing Reign and Mediation

    Ephesians 1 and Hebrews 1 present Christ seated at the right hand of God, far above all rule and authority. Psalm 110 lies behind much of this language: the enthroned Lord who also remains priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. This conjunction is crucial. Christ’s heavenly reign is not isolated from His mediatorial role. He rules as the crucified and risen one who has opened access and now governs the Church in communion.

    This means enthronement serves the present reality of the Church. Christ is not passively enthroned. He is living, active, speaking, interceding, and pouring out gifts. His authority is exercised for the gathering and sanctification of His people. Enthronement is therefore not only cosmic kingship but communion-preserving mediation.

    Pentecost and the Gift of Participatory Nearness

    Acts 2 must be read as inseparable from resurrection and ascension. Peter explicitly interprets Pentecost as the result of Christ’s exaltation to the right hand of God. The Spirit is poured out by the ascended Christ. This is vital for the whole book’s logic. The exalted Christ does not leave His people without participatory means of communion. He sends the Spirit so that His people may be incorporated into His life, hear His word, and live in the reality of His opened access.

    Thus Acts 1–2 together answer the chapter’s governing concern. The risen Christ ascends and then, from heaven, mediates participation through the Spirit. The Church’s communion is therefore pneumatic because it is ascension-grounded.

    The Lord’s Day as Participation in Resurrection Reality

    Revelation 1 identifies John as “in the Spirit on the Lord’s Day.” This is a small phrase with large theological significance. The Lord’s Day is not merely a Christianized calendar convention. It is the temporal sign of resurrection lordship. It is the day on which the Church is gathered under the risen Christ and, in Revelation’s case, caught up into heavenly vision and hearing.

    This means the Lord’s Day is not merely remembrance of a past event. It is participation in resurrection reality. It is the recurring liturgical time in which the Church lives from the victory, life, and reign of the ascended Christ. The day itself becomes pedagogical and participatory, much as earlier liturgical times did under the old covenant, now fulfilled in resurrection form.

    Heavenly Worship as Present Reality

    Revelation 4–5 presents heavenly worship centered on the enthroned God and the Lamb. Hebrews 12 says believers have come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God, to festal assembly. Taken together, these texts imply that heavenly worship is not only future spectacle. It is a present reality into which the Church is drawn. The ascended Christ does not merely rule over worship; He gathers His people into it.

    This is essential for the chapter’s claim that communion is ongoing. The Church worships on earth, but not as though cut off from heaven. In Christ, earthly liturgy participates in heavenly worship. The risen and ascended Lord remains the mediator of that participation.

    Finished Work and Ongoing Participation

    The whole biblical witness holds together Christ’s finished work and the Church’s ongoing participation. Hebrews is especially careful here. Christ offered one sacrifice for sins forever and sat down. Yet the same epistle emphasizes that He always lives to intercede and that the Church draws near now through Him. The finished work is not made irrelevant by ongoing mediation; rather, the ongoing mediation is the living exercise of the finished work’s efficacy.

    This is the key to the chapter’s central question. The ascended Christ continues to bring His people into communion with God by living in the presence of God for them and by drawing them into His own risen, priestly, heavenly life.

    Section Summary: Scripture presents the risen Christ as the beginning of new creation, the ascended priest in the heavenly sanctuary, the enthroned mediator, the giver of the Spirit, the Lord of the Lord’s Day, and the center of present heavenly worship.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From seeing resurrection and ascension as past doctrines only → to understanding them as the living basis of the Church’s present communion with God.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The fathers often treat resurrection, ascension, and heavenly enthronement as inseparable dimensions of Christ’s continuing work for the Church.

    Athanasius is again indispensable because he sees the resurrection not merely as proof but as the overthrow of death in the humanity Christ assumed. This supports the chapter’s claim that resurrection is new creation in principle. Human life is not simply excused; it is renewed and glorified in Christ.

    Cyril of Alexandria is especially helpful for holding together Incarnation, ascension, and participation. The one who ascends is the incarnate Son. Therefore His heavenly session is not abandonment of the flesh but the exaltation of humanity in Him. This is crucial to the doctrine of communion. Humanity’s access to God is not abstract but personal and embodied in Christ.

    John Chrysostom’s preaching on Hebrews consistently emphasizes Christ’s priestly session and the confidence it gives believers. He is particularly useful for showing that the ascended Christ remains active in intercession and access. The Church’s boldness is not psychological optimism but priestly confidence grounded in a living mediator.

    Gregory of Nyssa’s ascent theology resonates deeply with heavenly sanctuary language. In Christ, ascent is no longer only human striving but participatory movement in the one who has gone before. This helps prevent ascension from being conceived as mere departure. It is the opening of the human path into God.

    Maximus the Confessor offers a profound synthesis. The risen and ascended Christ is the one in whom cosmic divisions are overcome and the Church is gathered into heavenly liturgy. His thought strongly supports the idea that heavenly worship is not remote futurity only, but present participatory reality in Christ.

    Orthodox liturgical tradition also reinforces the chapter’s claims. Ascension and Lord’s Day are celebrated not as commemorations of divine absence but as feasts of exalted presence, priestly mediation, and heavenly participation. The Church’s worship lives under the conviction that Christ is enthroned and active now.

    Section Summary: Patristic theology confirms that the risen and ascended Christ remains the living mediator whose exalted humanity, priesthood, and reign gather the Church into present participation in divine life.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From treating resurrection and ascension as completed episodes → to seeing them as the living modes of Christ’s ongoing communion-bringing work.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that the ascended Christ continues to bring His people into communion with God by uniting finished work and living mediation. Resurrection inaugurates new creation in His humanity. Ascension is priestly entrance into the heavenly sanctuary. Enthronement is the ongoing reign and intercession of the mediator. The Lord’s Day is the Church’s repeated participation in resurrection lordship. Heavenly worship is the present sphere into which the Church is drawn through Him.

    Several affirmations structure this synthesis.

    First, Christ’s resurrection is not ancillary to communion. It is the beginning of the restored human life that can live in God’s presence beyond death.

    Second, Christ’s ascension is sanctuary theology fulfilled. He enters within the veil as high priest and forerunner, bringing humanity into God’s presence in Himself.

    Third, Christ’s enthronement is mediatorial reign. The one seated at the right hand is the same one who offered Himself, intercedes, pours out the Spirit, and rules the Church for her communion with the Father.

    Fourth, Pentecost is the ascended Christ’s gift of participatory communion. Through the Spirit, what Christ has accomplished becomes the Church’s present life.

    Fifth, the Lord’s Day is not bare memorial but the temporal form of resurrection participation. The Church gathers under the risen Lord in anticipation and enjoyment of new creation life.

    Sixth, heavenly worship is already present reality for the Church in Christ. Earthly liturgy is thus neither self-contained nor merely historical. It is participatory, eschatological, and heavenward.

    This synthesis answers the prompt’s key question. How does the ascended Christ continue to bring His people into communion with God? He does so by remaining the living priest, king, and mediator whose risen humanity stands before the Father and whose Spirit gathers the Church into His access, worship, and life.

    Section Summary: The chapter resolves its central question by showing that Christ’s resurrection, ascension, reign, and Spirit-gift actively mediate the Church’s present communion with God.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From seeing Christ’s work as complete but static → to understanding it as complete and continually participatory through the living ascended Lord.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    This chapter deepens the book’s anthropology by showing that human destiny is not merely to be forgiven and improved, but to be raised, drawn upward, and seated in communion with God in Christ. The resurrection and ascension reveal what humanity is for. In the exalted Christ we see not only who God is for us, but what redeemed human life is becoming.

    This is especially significant for the renewal of the nous. The believer’s mind is renewed not only by ethical instruction, but by participation in resurrection reality and heavenly orientation. Colossians’ exhortation to seek the things above presupposes an ascended Christ in whom believers’ lives are hidden. The mind learns to perceive from the perspective of communion rather than from within exile and death.

    Desire is likewise reshaped. The ascension teaches the heart where its true life and treasure are. The Church is not left with an earthly closed horizon. Christ’s exaltation redirects longing toward the life of God and the heavenly Zion already opened in Him. The Lord’s Day reinforces this reorientation weekly.

    Humility is also strengthened. The believer’s communion with God is not self-generated ascent. It is sharing in the ascent of Christ. The human being is brought near because Another has gone before. This undercuts both despair and pride. It produces grateful participation.

    Spiritually, the chapter encourages Christians to understand worship, prayer, obedience, and sacramental life as participation in the living Christ rather than as isolated human acts. The Christian life is not mere remembrance of a distant Savior. It is present relation to the risen and reigning Lord.

    Section Summary: The risen and ascended Christ reveals humanity’s true destiny and reorients mind, desire, humility, and worship toward shared participation in heavenly life.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From conceiving redemption as earthbound moral repair → to seeing it as participation in the exalted humanity and living mediation of Christ.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church lives now under the reign of the risen and ascended Christ. This gives her worship, identity, and mission their specific form. She is not simply a community remembering Jesus fondly. She is the body of the one who is seated at the right hand of the Father, lives forever, and ministers in the heavenly sanctuary. Her worship is therefore resurrectional, ascensional, and heavenly in orientation.

    The Lord’s Day is the privileged temporal expression of this reality. It is the day of the risen Lord and therefore the weekly gathering point of the Church’s participation in new creation. When the Church gathers on the Lord’s Day, she does not merely mark elapsed time since Easter. She enters again into the reality of Christ’s resurrection lordship, hears His word, approaches His table, and lives from His opened access.

    The Church’s liturgy is also heavenly participation. Revelation’s throne-room worship and Hebrews’ language of Mount Zion make clear that ecclesial worship is not isolated from the heavenly assembly. The Church is gathered into a reality already alive in Christ. This means her worship is not a human attempt to rise to God unaided, but a sharing in the worship of the ascended Son.

    Ecclesially, this chapter also clarifies the Church’s dependence on Christ’s ongoing mediation. Her unity, holiness, sacramental life, proclamation, and endurance all rest on the fact that Christ remains active now. The ascension does not create vacancy at the center of ecclesial life; it secures the truest form of presence.

    Finally, the Church’s hope is strengthened. The one who has gone before will bring His people where He is. The communion already begun in word, sacrament, Spirit, and worship will one day become unveiled sight. Thus the Lord’s Day and heavenly worship are not only present realities but anticipations of the final Zion.

    Section Summary: The Church is a resurrectional and ascensional people whose Lord’s Day worship and heavenly orientation arise from Christ’s present priestly reign and mediation.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From treating church life as remembrance of absent Christ → to living as the gathered people of the present, risen, ascended, and reigning Lord.

    Patristic Witness (Top 6, SBL)

    1. Athanasius. On the Incarnation.
      Essential for resurrection as the victory of life and the restoration of humanity in Christ.

    2. Cyril of Alexandria. Commentary on John and related christological writings.
      Valuable for the exalted humanity of Christ and the continuity between Incarnation and ascension.

    3. John Chrysostom. Homilies on Hebrews.
      A major witness to Christ’s ongoing priestly mediation and the Church’s bold access.

    4. Gregory of Nyssa. The Life of Moses and selected homilies.
      Helpful for ascent, transformation, and movement into God through Christ.

    5. Maximus the Confessor. Mystagogy and Ambigua.
      Important for the Church’s participation in heavenly liturgy and the exalted Christ’s cosmic mediation.

    6. Nicholas Cabasilas. The Life in Christ.
      Rich for the Church’s present sacramental participation in the living Christ.

    Practical Outcomes

    First, this chapter helps pastors and teachers explain why resurrection and ascension are central to communion, not optional appendices to the cross.

    Second, it gives the Lord’s Day a much richer theological significance as the Church’s weekly participation in resurrection reality.

    Third, it strengthens liturgical theology by grounding worship in Christ’s present heavenly mediation.

    Fourth, it deepens spirituality by teaching believers to live in conscious participation with the ascended Christ rather than in relation only to a remembered past.

    Fifth, it supports ecclesiology by showing that the Church’s unity and worship depend on Christ’s living reign and priesthood.

    Sixth, it strengthens eschatological hope by presenting heavenly worship as already real and final communion as already opened in Christ.

    Objections & Replies

    Objection 1: Does this chapter risk minimizing the finality of Christ’s work by emphasizing His ongoing mediation?

    Reply: No. The chapter insists that His ongoing mediation is the living efficacy of His finished work, not a supplement to its insufficiency.

    Objection 2: Is the Lord’s Day really more than weekly commemoration?

    Reply: Yes. Commemoration is included, but Revelation 1, apostolic practice, and the theology of resurrection lordship indicate that the day is participation in the reality of the risen Christ.

    Objection 3: Does heavenly worship as present reality blur the distinction between now and the end?

    Reply: No. The chapter maintains inaugurated participation and future consummation together. The Church already shares in heavenly Zion, but not yet in unveiled fullness.

    Objection 4: If Christ is ascended, how can He still be present to the Church?

    Reply: He is present precisely as the ascended Lord—by His Spirit, through His word, in sacramental participation, and in His ongoing priestly mediation before the Father.

    Useful for the new creation significance of the resurrection and its implications for Christian hope and worship.

    Conclusion

    The risen and ascended Christ continues to bring His people into communion with God because He is not merely the one who once accomplished redemption. He is the one who now lives in the presence of the Father for them. Resurrection is the beginning of new creation in His humanity. Ascension is His priestly entrance into the heavenly sanctuary. Enthronement is His ongoing reign and mediation. Pentecost is the outpouring of the Spirit by which the Church is drawn into His life. The Lord’s Day is the recurring temporal participation in this resurrection reality. Heavenly worship is the present sphere into which the Church is gathered.

    This means that finished work and ongoing participation are not rival theological emphases. They belong together. Christ has finished all that was necessary for access, and as the living mediator He now brings His people into the benefits and reality of that access. He is both the accomplished Savior and the present High Priest. Without the first, communion would remain impossible. Without the second, communion would remain external. In Him the Church has both.

    The chapter therefore completes a crucial movement in Part IV. Christ came near in the Incarnation. He gave His self-offering and instituted anamnetic participation. He tore the veil and opened access. He fulfilled Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, temple, and Mercy Seat in one integrated work. Now we see how that work remains alive for the Church: the risen and ascended Lord continues to gather His people into the life He has opened.

    This has profound implications for the whole book. Communion with God is not merely future, nor merely forensic, nor merely symbolic. It is presently mediated through the living Christ. In Him divine presence, divine speech, participation, and heavenly worship are all active realities. The Church’s task is not to create communion, but to receive, inhabit, and be transformed by it.

    Thus the answer to the chapter’s central question may be stated plainly: the ascended Christ continues to bring His people into communion with God by remaining the living priest, reigning Lord, and Spirit-giver whose risen humanity stands in the heavenly sanctuary and whose life the Church now shares in worship, word, sacrament, and hope. In Him the way to Zion is no longer only sought. It is already opened, inhabited, and leading the people of God toward the day when hearing becomes seeing.
