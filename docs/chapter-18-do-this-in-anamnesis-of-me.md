# Chapter 18  
## Do This in Anamnesis of Me

### Thesis

The Lord’s Supper is the Eucharistic fulfillment of covenant remembrance because anamnesis in Scripture is not mere intellectual recollection but covenantal re-presentation and participatory remembrance before God. In the Eucharist, the Church does not simply think back to Christ’s accomplished work as though salvation were absent and only mentally retrievable. Rather, she is gathered into sacramental participation in that once-for-all work, receiving its communion-giving reality in the present through the command and gift of Christ. Anamnesis is therefore a means of communion. It is how the Church remembers in a covenantal mode that is liturgical, participatory, and access-opening rather than merely symbolic, intellectual, or repetitious.

This chapter argues that the Eucharist must be understood within the line running from Exodus 12 through Luke 22 and 1 Corinthians 10–11 to Hebrews. Passover already taught Israel that remembrance is covenantal and liturgical. The past act of redemption was made present to later generations not by repetition of the original event in a crude sense, but by a divinely instituted memorial that incorporated them into its meaning and claims. When Jesus, in the setting of Passover, says, “Do this in anamnesis of me,” He is not reducing His self-offering to mental recollection. He is fulfilling Passover remembrance in Himself and giving the Church the covenantal means by which His accomplished work is participated in, proclaimed, and received.

This is crucial to the book’s central movement from redemption to access to communion. If Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat reality, then His self-offering must not be construed as a past act whose saving significance remains external to the Church except by cognition. The Church must be brought into participation. The Eucharist is one of the principal means by which this occurs. There the people of God remember in the biblical sense: they stand within the covenantal memorial instituted by Christ, proclaim His death, receive communion in His body and blood, and are formed as a worshiping people before God.

This chapter therefore answers the prompt’s key question directly: how does the Church participate in Christ’s accomplished work? She participates by anamnesis that is covenantal, ecclesial, liturgical, and sacramental. The Lord’s Supper is not symbolism only, not intellectual remembrance only, and not ritual repetition of a once-for-all sacrifice. It is the divinely given mode in which the once-for-all sacrifice is remembered covenantally and received as communion. Christ’s accomplished work is not repeated; it is made present to His people in the form of liturgical participation grounded in His command and promise. In that sense, anamnesis is one of the Church’s central practices of living within fulfilled Mercy Seat reality.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 12:1–28; Exodus 13:3–10; Deuteronomy 16:1–8; Luke 22:14–20; 1 Corinthians 10:14–22; 1 Corinthians 11:23–32; Hebrews 9:11–15, 24–28; Hebrews 10:19–25; Psalm 111; and Revelation 19:6–9. These are read in continuity with the book’s broader scriptural center in Exodus 25:17–22; Numbers 7:89; Luke 24:45; Hebrews 12:22–24; and Revelation 21–22.

Exodus 12 and 13 establish Passover as both redemptive event and covenant memorial. The people are to remember, tell, and enact what the Lord has done for them. Deuteronomy 16 further shows how feast and remembrance belong to covenant identity. Luke 22 places Jesus’ words of institution in Passover context and gives the explicit command to do this in anamnesis of Him. First Corinthians 10–11 shows that the Lord’s Supper is communion, proclamation, discernment, and covenant seriousness, not bare symbolism. Hebrews provides the decisive theological guardrails by insisting on the once-for-all character of Christ’s offering while also emphasizing opened access and gathered worship. Revelation 19 contributes the eschatological horizon of the marriage supper, while Revelation 21–22 shows the consummated communion toward which Eucharistic remembrance points.

These texts together establish that anamnesis is covenantal remembrance ordered toward communion and participation.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

Part IV is tracing how Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat reality. Chapter 17 established that the Incarnation is God’s decisive act of drawing near, making Christ the true tabernacle, temple, Bethel, and Emmanuel. The question now becomes: how does the Church participate in this accomplished reality? If communion is not to remain only objective and external, if Christ’s once-for-all work is not to be merely admired from a distance, then there must be divinely given modes by which the people of God are incorporated into it. The Eucharist is one of the central answers to that question.

The difficulty lies in how “remembrance” is commonly understood. In many modern settings remembrance is assumed to mean mental recollection. One remembers something absent by calling it to mind. Applied to the Lord’s Supper, this can reduce the Eucharist to a reflective act in which participants think about Christ’s death. Such thinking may be reverent and sincere, but if this is the whole meaning of anamnesis, the biblical and liturgical depth of the Supper is significantly diminished. A meal instituted in Passover context, bound to covenantal language, and interpreted by Paul in terms of koinonia cannot be adequately explained as symbolic recall alone.

The chapter must therefore clarify what biblical anamnesis means. It does not mean repeating Christ’s sacrifice, as though the once-for-allness of the cross were compromised. Nor does it mean mere recollection, as though the Church remained outside the event and only thought about it. Rather, it means covenantal remembrance within a divinely given liturgical act that makes the saving event determinative and participable in the present. The Church remembers in obedience to Christ’s command and in the mode Christ Himself has instituted.

This matters deeply for the whole book’s argument. If Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat, opens access, and grants participation in communion with God, then the Church’s worship must include forms in which this participation is enacted and received. The Eucharist belongs precisely here. It is one of the Church’s principal modes of entering liturgically into redemption → access → communion. Thus the issue is not only sacramental theology but the grammar of communion itself.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks how the Church participates in Christ’s accomplished work and argues that Eucharistic anamnesis must be understood as covenantal and participatory remembrance, not mere mental recall.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From thinking of remembrance as internal recollection → to understanding anamnesis as liturgical participation in Christ’s accomplished covenantal work.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate approaches fail to do justice to biblical anamnesis and the Lord’s Supper.

One inadequate approach reduces the Supper to symbolism only. The bread and cup signify Christ, perhaps even powerfully, but no real participation is thought to occur. Yet 1 Corinthians 10 speaks of communion in the body and blood of Christ, not symbolism alone. A purely symbolic reading cannot easily explain Paul’s language of participation, discernment, judgment, and ecclesial seriousness.

A second inadequate approach reduces remembrance to intellectual recollection. On this view the Supper is primarily an exercise in memory. The worshiper thinks about the cross and thereby honors Christ. But this is too thin for the biblical data. Passover remembrance was never only internal thought. It was covenantal reenactment under divine command, communal, liturgical, and identity-shaping. Jesus’ use of anamnesis in Passover context must therefore be read through that richer pattern.

A third inadequate approach understands the Lord’s Supper as ritual repetition in a way that threatens the once-for-allness of Christ’s sacrifice. Hebrews is a necessary corrective here. Christ offered Himself once for all and entered the heavenly sanctuary not repeatedly but definitively. Therefore Eucharistic anamnesis cannot mean a new sacrifice added to His completed work as though it were insufficient. Any account of sacramental participation must remain governed by the finality and uniqueness of the cross.

A fourth inadequate approach separates Eucharistic practice from covenant renewal. The meal is then treated as individual devotion or spiritual nourishment without sufficient sense of communal belonging, proclamation, and ecclesial identity. But Paul clearly links the one bread to the one body, and the words of institution are covenantal. The Eucharist is not private spirituality. It is the meal of the covenant people.

A fifth inadequate approach isolates the Supper from the larger sanctuary and Mercy Seat logic of Scripture. In such readings the Eucharist becomes an isolated ordinance rather than a fulfillment of Passover, sanctuary access, sacrificial remembrance, and divine communion. The chapter resists this by situating the meal within the whole biblical movement toward restored presence.

These inadequate readings fail because they detach remembrance from covenant, communion, or Christ’s once-for-all priestly work. The chapter therefore seeks a theological account that preserves all three: final sacrifice, present participation, and covenantal remembrance.

**Section Summary:** Symbolic-only, intellectualist, repetitive-sacrificial, privatized, and de-contextualized accounts of the Lord’s Supper all fail to capture the biblical meaning of anamnesis.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From shrinking the Supper into symbol or thought alone → to recovering it as covenantal, participatory remembrance governed by Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### Passover as Covenantal Remembrance

Exodus 12 and 13 provide the indispensable background for understanding anamnesis. Passover is not merely an event that happened once in Egypt and was later remembered sentimentally. It is instituted as an ongoing memorial feast. Israel is commanded to keep it, explain it to the next generation, and inhabit it as the defining remembrance of the Lord’s redemptive act. The participants are not physically present in Egypt, yet the act of remembrance is more than historical lecture. It is liturgical participation in the identity created by the original redemption.

This is crucial because it establishes the biblical pattern of remembrance as covenantal re-presentation. The event remains once-for-all in historical occurrence, yet its saving significance is brought forward into the present through instituted memorial. The people are claimed again by what God did then. Passover remembrance is therefore not mere recollection. It is identity-forming, communal, and liturgical.

### “Do This in Anamnesis of Me”

Luke 22 places Jesus’ words of institution directly in Passover context. He takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it, and says, “This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in anamnesis of me.” The cup is likewise identified with the new covenant in His blood. Several things must be said at once.

First, Jesus is clearly reconfiguring Passover around His own self-offering. He is not merely attaching Himself to an existing memorial; He is fulfilling and redirecting it. Second, the remembrance He commands must be read in continuity with Passover’s covenantal memorial logic. Third, the meal is explicitly covenantal: “This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.” Therefore remembrance here is not detached from covenant renewal and participation. It is the meal of the fulfilled covenant people.

Most importantly, Christ gives the Church not merely an explanatory doctrine but a practice. He commands an ongoing liturgical act. This already indicates that participation in His accomplished work will not be reduced to mental reflection alone.

### Communion in 1 Corinthians 10

Paul’s language in 1 Corinthians 10 is decisive: “The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a participation [koinonia] in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a participation in the body of Christ?” Here the meal is explicitly participatory. Paul does not ask whether the elements symbolize Christ only. He speaks of communion with Christ and, because there is one bread, the formation of one body.

This is crucial for the chapter’s argument. The Supper is covenantal remembrance that effects communion. It is not repetition of the sacrifice, but it is real participation in the one sacrifice’s communion-giving reality. Paul’s logic is not merely illustrative; it is sacramental and ecclesial. Christ’s accomplished work is not absent from the meal in every sense except conceptual memory. The people commune in what He has accomplished.

### Proclamation and Discernment in 1 Corinthians 11

In 1 Corinthians 11, Paul adds another layer. “As often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.” The meal is not only remembrance and participation; it is proclamation. Yet even proclamation here is not bare speech. It is liturgical enactment. The death of Christ is proclaimed sacramentally by eating and drinking as He commanded.

The warning about discerning the body and the consequences of unworthy participation also show that more than symbolism is at stake. A merely mental memorial could hardly bear such theological and ecclesial weight. The Supper concerns the real body of Christ, the real unity of the Church, and the real holiness of participation.

### Hebrews and the Once-for-All Sacrifice

Hebrews supplies essential theological boundaries. Christ has entered once for all into the holy places by His own blood and has offered one sacrifice for sins forever. Any interpretation of the Eucharist must preserve this once-for-allness. The Church does not re-sacrifice Christ. She does not supplement His work. She does not improve upon Calvary by liturgical repetition.

Yet Hebrews also emphasizes present access: believers draw near, enter with confidence, and gather in worship. This means the once-for-all sacrifice is not merely complete in itself; it is also present in its effects as the ground of communion. The Eucharist can therefore be understood as anamnesis precisely because Christ’s act is once-for-all and covenantally present to the Church as the basis of access and assembly. The meal does not add to the sacrifice; it sacramentally participates in its communion-opening reality.

### Eschatological Banquet

Finally, the Eucharist must be understood eschatologically. Jesus speaks of not drinking again until the kingdom comes. Paul says the Church proclaims His death until He comes. Revelation 19 gestures toward the marriage supper of the Lamb. Anamnesis is therefore not only backward-looking. It is also forward-leaning. The Church remembers in hope, participating now in a meal that anticipates consummated communion.

Thus the scriptural claim is clear: biblical anamnesis is covenantal re-presentation, Eucharistic participation, proclamation, and anticipation. It is a means of communion with Christ’s accomplished work.

**Section Summary:** Scripture presents anamnesis as covenantal remembrance shaped by Passover, fulfilled by Christ, enacted in communion, guarded by His once-for-all sacrifice, and oriented toward eschatological banquet.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From thinking the Supper merely points to Christ’s work → to seeing it as the Church’s commanded participation in that work through covenantal remembrance.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers consistently resist reducing the Eucharist to bare symbol or inward recollection, while also maintaining Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice in a way appropriate to the Church’s worship.

Irenaeus is especially important because he sees the Eucharist as participation in the life of Christ and as the offering of creation received back from God and returned in thanksgiving. This helps place anamnesis in a participatory, ecclesial, and incarnational frame. For Irenaeus, the Eucharist is not merely pedagogical symbolism; it is part of the Church’s real communion in Christ.

Cyril of Jerusalem, in his catechetical writings, is similarly clear that the Eucharistic mystery is not exhausted by appearance or by mental remembrance. The Church receives what Christ gives and thereby participates in His body and blood. Such teaching does not deny remembrance but deepens it into sacramental reality.

John Chrysostom is valuable because he repeatedly emphasizes both the once-for-allness of Christ’s offering and the Church’s real Eucharistic participation. His homiletic and liturgical instincts help prevent both reduction to mere symbol and misunderstanding as a new sacrifice separate from the cross. The Church remembers in a way that participates in the one offering.

Maximus the Confessor broadens the picture by placing Eucharistic life within the Church’s whole movement of participation, unification, and ascent to God. The liturgy becomes the communal form in which Christ’s accomplished work is received and embodied. This strongly supports the chapter’s claim that anamnesis is a means of communion.

Nicholas Cabasilas is also deeply relevant. For him, the mysteries bring believers into actual participation in Christ’s life, death, and resurrection. Remembrance in the Church’s sacramental life is therefore not detached cognition but entry into Christ’s life-giving work.

The broader Orthodox tradition of anamnesis and epiclesis also confirms that the Eucharist is not mere recollection. The Church remembers before God, invokes the Spirit, and receives Christ’s gift in covenantal communion. This liturgical consciousness preserves the biblical depth of remembrance as participatory and ecclesial.

**Section Summary:** Patristic and Orthodox theology affirm that Eucharistic remembrance is not bare mental recall but sacramental participation in Christ’s once-for-all, communion-giving work.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From reducing remembrance to thought or sign alone → to seeing it as liturgical and sacramental participation in the accomplished work of Christ.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that anamnesis is the Church’s covenantal, liturgical, and participatory remembrance of Christ’s accomplished work, through which the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ is neither repeated nor merely thought about, but received as the present ground of communion with God and one another.

This synthesis includes several affirmations.

First, anamnesis is biblical remembrance, not mere recollection. It stands in continuity with Passover memorial and therefore has covenantal, communal, and liturgical depth.

Second, the Lord’s Supper is the fulfillment of Passover. Christ re-centers remembrance around His own body given and blood of the new covenant. The old exodus meal becomes the new covenant meal of accomplished redemption.

Third, remembrance is covenantal re-presentation. This does not mean a new saving event replaces the old one, but that the one event remains effective and determinative in the present through the memorial Christ instituted.

Fourth, the Eucharist is participation in Christ. Paul’s language of koinonia requires more than symbolism only. The Church communes in the body and blood of Christ and is formed as one body through that communion.

Fifth, the once-for-allness of Christ’s sacrifice is preserved. Hebrews rules out any crude notion of repeated sacrifice. Anamnesis depends on the finality of Christ’s work; it does not challenge it.

Sixth, anamnesis is a means of communion. It serves the movement redemption → access → communion by bringing the Church into liturgical participation in Christ’s accomplished access-opening work.

This doctrinal synthesis explains why the chapter belongs within Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat. The Church’s Eucharistic life is one of the principal ways the fulfilled Mercy Seat reality is inhabited. Christ’s work is accomplished once, access is opened once, but that access is repeatedly entered and received in the Church’s worship.

**Section Summary:** The chapter resolves its central question by defining anamnesis as covenantal, participatory remembrance through which the Church communes in Christ’s once-for-all work.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing the Eucharist as either mere memory or repeated sacrifice → to understanding it as participatory remembrance grounded in final sacrifice and ordered toward communion.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter deepens the book’s anthropology by showing that human beings are not formed merely by ideas but by liturgical remembrance. What a people remembers covenantally, it inhabits; and what it inhabits, it becomes. The Eucharist therefore addresses not only belief but desire, identity, and embodied participation.

Anamnesis shapes the nous because it gathers perception around Christ’s accomplished work. The Church does not merely hear that Christ died; she enacts and receives the form of that remembrance in worship. This repeated participation trains the mind to see reality through redemption, covenant, sacrifice, thanksgiving, and hope. The Supper becomes a school of perception.

It also shapes desire. The table retrains the heart away from self-sufficiency and toward received communion. The faithful learn to hunger for Christ, to desire participation rather than possession, and to seek unity in His body rather than private spiritual autonomy. This is especially important for the book’s larger argument, since transformed desire must be given concrete liturgical means of formation.

Humility likewise emerges here. No one comes to the table as self-sufficient possessor of grace. The Supper is received, not achieved. It recalls a death we did not accomplish and a covenant we did not establish. The worshiper is therefore humbled and nourished simultaneously. He learns dependence in the very act of communion.

Self-offering and obedience are also strengthened by anamnesis. Since the Eucharist remembers the body given and blood poured out, it forms the Church in the logic of self-giving love. The remembrance of Christ’s offering becomes the matrix for the people’s own living sacrifice. Thus anamnesis does not end in contemplation alone. It forms a people for obedient communion.

**Section Summary:** Eucharistic anamnesis forms the mind, desire, humility, and self-offering of the Church by making Christ’s covenantal self-gift the center of her repeated remembrance.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From imagining remembrance as mentally passive → to seeing it as one of the Church’s deepest means of spiritual and anthropological formation.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church is the community that obeys Christ’s command, “Do this in anamnesis of me,” and in doing so becomes a Eucharistic people. This means the Lord’s Supper cannot be treated as peripheral. It belongs near the center of the Church’s liturgical and ecclesial life because it is one of the primary means by which Christ’s accomplished work is proclaimed, participated in, and received.

Ecclesially, the Supper binds the Church together. Paul’s one-bread/one-body logic makes this plain. The Eucharist is not only relation between individual believer and Christ. It is corporate participation that constitutes and reveals the Church as one communion. Anamnesis is therefore inseparable from ecclesiology.

Liturgically, the Church remembers before God. She gives thanks, proclaims, invokes, receives, and anticipates. This is why reducing the Supper to internal recollection distorts the shape of worship. Anamnesis belongs within the full liturgical life of the Church because it is covenantal remembrance, not bare thought.

The eschatological dimension also matters greatly. The Eucharist looks backward to Christ’s death, upward to His heavenly priesthood, inward to present communion, and forward to the banquet of the kingdom. The Church therefore remembers as pilgrims. The table is not only memory of the cross but foretaste of Zion fulfilled.

This locates Chapter 18 properly within the broader sequence of Chapters 17–21. The incarnate Christ has come near. In the Eucharist, the Church participates in that nearness as fulfilled remembrance. The veil will be treated in the next chapter as access opened. But even here the Church already lives from that access in liturgical form.

**Section Summary:** The Church becomes herself in Eucharistic anamnesis, as a covenant people formed by communion in Christ’s once-for-all work and oriented toward the banquet of the kingdom.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating the Lord’s Supper as secondary observance → to understanding it as a central ecclesial means of participating in Christ’s accomplished communion.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Doesn’t this account make the Eucharist too strong and threaten the finality of the cross?

**Reply:** No. The chapter explicitly grounds anamnesis in the once-for-allness of Christ’s offering. The Supper does not repeat Calvary; it participates in Calvary’s abiding covenantal efficacy.

### Objection 2: Is “re-presentation” too close to “repetition”?

**Reply:** Only if misunderstood. Re-presentation here means covenantal memorial presence grounded in divine institution, not a new sacrifice or second saving event.

### Objection 3: Could Paul’s “participation” language still be symbolic?

**Reply:** Symbolism is certainly involved, but Paul’s language of koinonia, discernment, judgment, and ecclesial unity strongly suggests something more than bare symbol. The signs are participatory signs.

### Objection 4: Why insist on Passover background so strongly?

**Reply:** Because Jesus Himself institutes the Supper in that context, and Passover supplies the biblical grammar of covenantal remembrance necessary to understand anamnesis.

## Conclusion

The Church participates in Christ’s accomplished work through covenantal anamnesis. This is the chapter’s central claim and the answer to its governing question. The Lord’s Supper is not a bare symbol, not a mental exercise, and not a ritual repetition of what Christ has already completed. It is the liturgically enacted remembrance by which the Church receives and proclaims the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ as the present ground of access, communion, and covenant life.

Passover already taught Israel that remembrance could be liturgical, communal, and identity-forming. Christ takes up that grammar and fulfills it in Himself. “Do this in anamnesis of me” means that His people are not left to admire redemption from afar. They are brought into it sacramentally, covenantally, and ecclesially. Paul’s teaching confirms this by speaking of communion in the body and blood of Christ and by linking one bread with one body. Hebrews preserves the finality of Christ’s sacrifice while opening the way for the Church’s bold access and worship. Thus the whole biblical pattern coheres.

This chapter therefore advances the book’s larger theological movement. Christ has drawn near in the Incarnation. In the Eucharist, the Church participates in that nearness as remembered and received covenantal reality. Divine presence, divine speech, participation, and worship all converge at the table. There the Church is formed as a people of communion. There desire is retrained, humility deepened, self-offering learned, and hope stirred toward the coming banquet.

The significance of this for the whole book is substantial. If the controlling thesis is that redemption is ordered toward communion, then the Lord’s Supper must be understood as one of the Church’s chief means of inhabiting that communion. It is not enough to say that Christ died. The Church must remember as He commanded. And in that remembrance—biblical, covenantal, participatory remembrance—she is drawn again and again into the life opened by His death and resurrection.

Thus anamnesis functions exactly as the chapter insists it should: as a means of communion. The Church does not merely recall Christ; she receives Him as the one who has accomplished access and gives participation in His accomplished work. In doing so, she becomes ever more deeply the people of the fulfilled Mercy Seat: gathered, nourished, reconciled, and turned toward the day when remembrance gives way to sight and the Eucharistic foretaste flowers into the marriage supper of the Lamb.
