# Chapter 17  
## The Word Tabernacled Among Us

### Thesis

The Incarnation is God’s decisive act of drawing near to humanity. In Jesus Christ, God does not merely send a message from a distance, nor provide a new ritual mechanism by which humanity might approach Him, nor simply reveal information about divine mercy. He comes Himself. The Word becomes flesh and tabernacles among us. The Incarnation is therefore already communion in principle: the personal, historical, embodied arrival of God in the midst of humanity in order to restore access, presence, hearing, participation, and worship. Christ is the true tabernacle, the true temple, the true Bethel, the true Emmanuel, and the definitive meeting place between God and humanity.

This chapter argues that the Incarnation must be understood through the sanctuary logic already developed in the book. Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 disclosed God’s desire to meet with and speak with His people from above the Mercy Seat. Eden revealed humanity’s original vocation to dwell before God. Zion disclosed the destination of ordered communion. Exile showed the tragedy of presence lost and speech disrupted. The Incarnation now appears as God’s climactic answer to that whole story. In Christ, divine presence is no longer merely localized in a sanctuary made by hands. The dwelling of God comes personally among humanity. What temple, tabernacle, and ark anticipated, the Son fulfills in His own person.

This means the chapter must show more than that Jesus is divine or that He is the bearer of revelation, though both are essential. It must show that Christ Himself is the place where God and humanity meet. He fulfills temple theology, not by abolishing the logic of sacred dwelling, but by taking it into Himself. He fulfills Bethel, the “house of God,” because in Him heaven and earth are joined. He fulfills Emmanuel because God is with us not merely by favor or symbol but in embodied, personal nearness. He fulfills the tabernacle because in Him the glory dwells bodily and from Him divine speech goes forth.

The chapter therefore answers the prompt’s key question directly: how does God dwell among His people in Christ? He dwells among them by becoming man without ceasing to be God, by entering history as the true meeting place, and by making communion not merely promised but embodied in principle. Redemption → access → communion is already present in germ at the Incarnation itself. Forgiveness alone cannot explain John 1. The Word tabernacling among us is the beginning of restored presence, restored speech, and restored participation. In Christ, the distance created by sin is not merely addressed from above. It is invaded by grace from within.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include John 1:1–18; John 1:43–51; John 2:13–22; Colossians 1:15–20; Colossians 2:9; Hebrews 1:1–4; Hebrews 10:19–22; Exodus 25:17–22; Exodus 29:42–46; Genesis 28:10–22; Psalm 84; Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:22–23; and Revelation 21:3, 22–23. These are read together with the wider biblical pattern already established in the book from Eden, sanctuary, Zion, and prophetic hope.

John 1 is central because it declares both the deity of the Word and the fact that the Word became flesh and dwelt—or more literally, tabernacled—among us. John 1:51 evokes Bethel and the opened heaven over the Son of Man. John 2 identifies Christ’s body as the true temple. Colossians 1 and 2 present Christ as the one in whom all fullness dwells bodily and through whom reconciliation occurs. Hebrews 1 identifies the Son as the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of His being, while Hebrews 10 shows the access opened through His flesh. Genesis 28 provides the Bethel background, and Revelation 21 gives the consummation in which God’s dwelling with humanity is fully unveiled.

Together these texts establish that the Incarnation is not incidental to communion but its decisive historical inauguration.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

Part IV marks the true christological center of the book. Everything prior has prepared for it. Part I established humanity’s creation for communion, hearing, worship, and covenantal love. Part II showed that redemption, sanctuary, sacrifice, priesthood, and divine speech all exist in service of communion. Part III traced the loss of communion through Eden, exile, departure of glory, and the longing for Zion. Now the book turns to the decisive question: how does God restore what has been lost? The answer is not first a system, rite, or message. It is a person. The Word becomes flesh.

This chapter asks what it means to say that the Incarnation is itself already communion in principle. That phrase requires careful attention. It does not mean that the full effects of redemption are automatically enjoyed simply because the Son has assumed flesh. Nor does it collapse the cross, resurrection, ascension, and gift of the Spirit into one undifferentiated moment. Rather, it means that in the Incarnation the decisive act of divine nearness has occurred. God has drawn near in a way that gathers all prior sanctuary logic into personal form. The distance between Creator and creature, aggravated and disordered by sin, is now entered by the Creator Himself in mercy.

The issue is therefore not merely one of christological identity, though that identity is indispensable. The chapter must not only say who Christ is, but what His coming means in relation to temple, Bethel, sanctuary, Mercy Seat, and Zion. The question is not whether God is near in Christ in a generic sense, but how the particular biblical patterns of dwelling, meeting, and speech are fulfilled in Him. If previous chapters have shown that God’s purpose is to meet with and speak with His people, then the Incarnation must be read as the climactic mode of that meeting and speech.

This chapter must also answer a temptation present in many theological accounts: to make the work of Christ begin only at the cross, as though the Incarnation were merely prerequisite mechanism. But John, Colossians, and Hebrews do not allow such flattening. The tabernacling of the Word is already revelatory, sanctifying, communion-making presence. The cross will complete and open the way into what the Incarnation inaugurates, but the nearness of God in the flesh is itself a decisive redemptive act.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks how the Incarnation itself advances the restoration of communion and argues that in Christ God’s decisive act of drawing near gathers sanctuary, temple, and Bethel patterns into personal form.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating the Incarnation as mere prelude to redemption → to seeing it as the historical inauguration of restored divine-human communion.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate approaches obscure the full theological significance of the Incarnation for the book’s central claim.

One inadequate approach reduces the Incarnation to metaphysical necessity for the atonement. On this account the Son becomes human primarily so that He may later die. There is truth in this: the cross requires the Incarnate One. Yet if the Incarnation is treated merely as a mechanism, its own revelatory and communion-making significance is diminished. John 1 does not present the Word made flesh as inert precondition. It presents Him as glory, grace, truth, and dwelling.

A second inadequate approach treats Jesus mainly as moral example or teacher. Such readings rightly note His humanity, teachings, and exemplary life, but they cannot account for temple fulfillment, Emmanuel theology, or the language of divine fullness dwelling bodily. If Christ is only exemplary man, He cannot be the true meeting place between God and humanity.

A third inadequate approach severs Incarnation from sanctuary and temple theology. Christ then becomes the answer to guilt but not the fulfillment of God’s dwelling. Yet Scripture explicitly resists this. John says the Word tabernacled among us. John 2 says His body is the temple. Colossians says the fullness dwells bodily in Him. The sanctuary patterns are not optional interpretive overlays; they are woven into the apostolic witness.

A fourth inadequate approach speaks of divine presence in Christ too vaguely. “God is with us” becomes a generalized statement of comfort or closeness. But biblical theology demands greater precision. How is God with us? In Christ He is with us as tabernacled glory, opened Bethel, true temple, speaking Word, reconciling presence, and the locus of access to the Father. Without this precision, the theological force of Emmanuel is blunted.

A fifth inadequate approach opposes the old covenant sanctuary too sharply to the Incarnation, as though Christ renders temple categories irrelevant instead of fulfilling them. But fulfillment is not abolition into meaninglessness. The logic of temple, Mercy Seat, and divine dwelling becomes more concrete in Christ, not less. He is not merely an alternative to sacred dwelling; He is its truth.

These inadequate readings fail because they isolate one aspect of Christ from the wider biblical story of divine presence. The chapter must therefore recover the Incarnation as the concentrated fulfillment of sanctuary communion.

**Section Summary:** Mechanistic, exemplary, de-sanctuarized, vague, and anti-fulfillment readings all fail to show how the Incarnation gathers God’s dwelling purpose into Christ Himself.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From reading Jesus mainly as means, model, or message → to understanding Him as the true tabernacle and personal meeting place of God and humanity.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### “The Word Became Flesh and Tabernacled Among Us”

John 1:14 is the axial text for the chapter: “And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory.” The verb often translated “dwelt” more literally means “tabernacled.” This is not a decorative metaphor. John is intentionally identifying Jesus with the logic of sanctuary dwelling. The glory once associated with tabernacle and temple now appears in the flesh of the incarnate Son. The divine presence has become personal, historical, embodied, and accessible in a new and climactic way.

This is decisive for the whole book’s argument. In Exodus 25:22 God promises meeting and speech from above the Mercy Seat. In John 1, that meeting and speech are no longer confined to architectural sacred space. The Word Himself comes among humanity. Divine speech is now not only uttered by God, but embodied in God the Son. Communion has moved from typological concentration in sanctuary to personal concentration in Christ.

### “No One Has Ever Seen God . . . He Has Made Him Known”

John 1:18 deepens the matter: the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, has made Him known. The Incarnation is not merely God’s nearness but God’s self-exegesis. The Son reveals the Father by being from the Father and with the Father in perfect communion. This is crucial because the book’s controlling sequence places divine speech at the heart of communion. In Christ, that sequence reaches astonishing intensity: the one who is God’s Word draws near bodily and makes the Father known from within His own filial relation.

This means the Incarnation is already hearing-restoring in principle. Humanity, darkened and exiled, is no longer dependent merely on distant signs or fragmented prophetic anticipation. The Son Himself is present as the revealer. This does not negate the need for cross, resurrection, or Pentecost; it establishes the decisive new situation in which communion is no longer merely promised from afar.

### Christ as Bethel Fulfilled

John 1:51 invokes Genesis 28 in a startling way. Jesus tells Nathanael that he will see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man. The allusion to Jacob’s vision at Bethel is unmistakable. Bethel, the “house of God,” was the place where heaven and earth touched in revelatory encounter. By applying that imagery to Himself, Jesus declares that He is the true Bethel. The ladder, the access, the place of divine-human traffic, is now His own person.

This is profoundly important for the book because Chapter 1 already traced the throne-ladder and Bethel logic. The present chapter now brings that theme to christological fulfillment. Christ is not merely standing at Bethel. He is Bethel fulfilled. Heaven and earth meet in Him. God dwells among His people in Him. Access is no longer simply symbolic; it is personal.

### “Destroy This Temple, and in Three Days I Will Raise It Up”

John 2:19–21 makes the temple claim explicit. Jesus identifies His body as the true temple. The old temple is not dismissed as irrelevant. Rather, it finds its purpose gathered into Him. Temple theology—presence, sacrifice, access, glory, worship—now converges in the flesh of Christ. The place where God dwells among humanity is no longer finally a building in Jerusalem but the body of the Son.

This is essential to the prompt’s demand that the chapter show Christ as the fulfillment of temple theology. If Christ is the true temple, then He is the meeting place between God and humanity not metaphorically alone but ontologically and personally. Divine presence does not merely visit Him; it dwells in Him bodily.

### Colossians: Fullness Dwelling Bodily

Colossians 1:19 and 2:9 reinforce the same reality in dogmatic form: in Christ all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell, and in Him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily. This language is astonishing in its density. It confirms that the Incarnation is not simply divine assistance to humanity or human elevation by association. It is the bodily dwelling of divine fullness. If temple and tabernacle were places of divine dwelling, then Christ fulfills them by being the one in whom that dwelling is complete and personal.

Colossians also links this dwelling to reconciliation. Through Him God reconciles all things. This supports the book’s broader movement from redemption to access to communion. The fullness dwells bodily in Christ so that estranged creation may be restored to peace and participation.

### Hebrews: The Son as Radiance and Access

Hebrews 1 presents the Son as the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of His being. The sanctuary’s glory now shines personally in Him. Hebrews 10 then shows that access is opened through His flesh. This is especially important because it links Incarnation and access in a way that serves the chapter’s thesis. The flesh of Christ is not obstacle to communion; it is the very mode by which communion is opened.

The argument of Hebrews prevents us from separating dwelling from atonement or Incarnation from access. The Son becomes what we are and offers Himself so that entry into the heavenly sanctuary may be granted. Thus the Incarnation is already communion in principle, and the cross and priestly work render that communion accessible in fullness.

### Emmanuel and New Creation

Matthew’s citation of Isaiah 7:14—“God with us”—must also be heard here. Emmanuel is not generic divine solidarity. It is the naming of the Incarnation as restored dwelling. And Revelation 21 finally reveals the consummation toward which this all points: “Behold, the dwelling place of God is with humanity.” The Incarnation is therefore both fulfillment and foretaste. In Christ, God dwells among us decisively. In the new creation, that dwelling becomes all-encompassing and unveiled.

**Section Summary:** John, Colossians, Hebrews, and related texts present Christ as the true tabernacle, Bethel, temple, and Emmanuel, in whom divine fullness dwells bodily and communion with God is inaugurated in principle.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing the Incarnation as divine nearness in general → to seeing it as the definitive sanctuary fulfillment in which God Himself becomes the meeting place with humanity.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers of the Church are especially strong on the themes central to this chapter because the Incarnation lies at the heart of their theology.

Athanasius is indispensable. For him, the Word becomes flesh not merely to teach or to be seen, but to heal, restore, and deify humanity. The Incarnation is already redemptive because in it the Word enters the very condition of fallen humanity to renew it from within. This aligns directly with the chapter’s claim that the Incarnation is already communion in principle. God draws near not symbolically but personally and ontologically.

Irenaeus likewise sees in Christ the recapitulation of humanity and of the whole economy of God. What Adam failed to be, Christ fulfills. What Israel anticipated in types and covenants, Christ gathers into Himself. Such a vision supports the claim that sanctuary, Bethel, and temple theology are not discarded but concentrated in the incarnate Son.

Cyril of Alexandria is especially important for temple Christology. He repeatedly insists that the Word’s assumption of flesh means that divine presence and human nature are joined without confusion in the one Christ. The body of Christ is not a temporary instrument external to the Word’s identity. It is the flesh of the one who is Himself God’s self-revelation. This makes the temple language of John 2 especially luminous.

Maximus the Confessor contributes a cosmic dimension. For Maximus, the Incarnation is the mystery in which the divisions of creation are overcome in the Logos. Heaven and earth, God and humanity, visible and invisible are reconciled in Him. This strongly reinforces the Bethel and temple themes of the chapter. Christ is the true junction of realms, the living place of divine-human communion.

The Orthodox liturgical tradition also deepens these themes. The feast of the Nativity, the hymns of Theophany, and the broader cycle of worship all repeatedly confess the Incarnation as the advent of divine presence among humanity. The Church does not celebrate simply the birth of a holy child but the coming of God to dwell with and restore His people. In that sense, liturgy preserves the same instincts the chapter is articulating.

The patristic witness therefore confirms several core claims: the Incarnation is inherently redemptive, Christ fulfills temple and dwelling theology, and divine-human communion is restored in principle by the Word’s assumption of flesh.

**Section Summary:** Patristic theology powerfully affirms that the Incarnation itself is a saving act of divine nearness in which temple, sanctuary, and restored humanity are fulfilled in Christ.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating Incarnation mainly as doctrinal prerequisite → to understanding it as the personal and restorative arrival of God’s dwelling among humanity.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that the Incarnation is God’s decisive act of drawing near because in Jesus Christ the fullness of deity dwells bodily, making Him the true tabernacle, the true temple, the true Bethel, and the true Emmanuel. In Him the sanctuary logic of Scripture reaches personal fulfillment, and communion is inaugurated in principle.

This synthesis includes several claims.

First, Christ is the true tabernacle. The Word tabernacles among us, which means divine glory and presence are no longer merely concentrated in sacred architecture but in the person of the Son.

Second, Christ is the true meeting place. What the Mercy Seat anticipated, Christ embodies. God and humanity meet in Him without confusion or division.

Third, Christ is the fulfillment of temple theology. His body is the temple because God’s dwelling is bodily present in Him. The temple’s truth is therefore personal rather than merely structural.

Fourth, Christ fulfills Bethel. He is the place of open heaven, the living house of God, the true union of heaven and earth. In Him ascent and access are no longer merely visionary but embodied.

Fifth, the Incarnation is already communion in principle. God’s drawing near in Christ is itself redemptive presence. It does not yet exhaust the whole economy of salvation, but it decisively establishes the new reality in which communion is restored from God’s side.

Sixth, the Incarnation serves the book’s central movement: redemption → access → communion. It must not be collapsed into forgiveness alone. The Son comes not merely to cancel debt but to dwell, reveal, reconcile, and gather humanity into divine life.

This doctrinal synthesis helps explain why the Incarnation is indispensable to every subsequent chapter in this section. Veil torn, Eucharistic anamnesis, heavenly enthronement, and participation all depend on the fact that God has already come near in Christ. The sanctuary is fulfilled not in abstraction, but in a person.

**Section Summary:** The chapter resolves its central question by presenting Christ as the bodily dwelling of divine fullness and the personal fulfillment of tabernacle, temple, Bethel, and Emmanuel.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From saying merely that God sent Christ → to confessing that in Christ God Himself dwelt among humanity as the true place of communion.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

The Incarnation clarifies humanity by revealing both its dignity and its need. Human nature is not discarded by God but assumed. This means the human vocation to communion is not abandoned as impossible. It is taken up and restored in Christ. The very fact that the Word becomes flesh testifies that humanity was made for union with God’s life in a way that, though ruptured by sin, remains recoverable in grace.

This has major implications for the book’s anthropology. The human being is not merely a sinner requiring acquittal, though he is that. He is also a being whose nature is capable of communion because it was made for it and because in Christ it is assumed without destruction. The Incarnation therefore confirms and deepens the claims made in Chapters 4 and 5: humanity’s telos is communion, and salvation restores that telos from within human life itself.

The chapter also bears on the renewal of the nous. If the Son is the true revelation of the Father and the bodily dwelling of divine fullness, then to behold Christ is already to have one’s perception reoriented. Divine speech now comes through a face, a voice, a body, a life. The noetic restoration later developed in the book depends in part on this incarnational concreteness. The mind is not renewed by abstraction alone, but by encounter with the incarnate Word.

Desire is likewise transformed by the Incarnation. The beauty and goodness of God are no longer hidden behind inaccessible holiness or symbolic mediation alone; they appear in the humanity of Christ. He becomes the true object of sanctified desire, drawing love away from false goods and toward God made present.

Humility also takes on renewed depth. The Incarnation humbles human pride by revealing that salvation comes not from human ascent but from divine descent. Yet it also dignifies humanity by showing that God has not despised the flesh. Thus humility becomes neither self-hatred nor self-exaltation, but truthful reception of grace.

Spiritually, the chapter teaches that communion with God is now irreversibly christological. One does not approach the Father in abstraction from the Son. One comes through the one in whom God has already come near. This gives Christian spirituality its concrete center.

**Section Summary:** The Incarnation reveals humanity as made for communion, renews perception through the incarnate revelation of God, redirects desire, and grounds humility in divine condescension and human dignity.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From conceiving salvation as external adjustment to human life → to seeing human nature itself restored and redirected in the incarnate Christ.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church exists as the community gathered around and into the incarnate Christ, who is the true tabernacle and meeting place of God and humanity. This gives ecclesial and liturgical life a deeply incarnational structure. The Church does not simply remember a teacher from the past. She participates in the life of the one in whom God dwells bodily and through whom access to the Father is opened.

This means the Church’s worship is not merely symbolic recollection of divine nearness. It is participation in the presence of the incarnate, crucified, risen, and ascended Lord. Word and sacrament take on their full force here. The proclaimed Gospel is the address of the one who tabernacled among us. The Eucharistic table is communion in the one whose body is the true temple. The Church gathers as those already brought into Christ’s own access to the Father.

Ecclesially, the Incarnation also establishes the Church as a temple people. Because Christ is the true temple and believers are united to Him, the Church becomes His body and thereby shares in His temple reality. This does not erase the distinction between Christ and His people, but it means that ecclesiology is fundamentally participation in the incarnate Lord.

The liturgical implications are equally strong. Christian worship is bodily because the Word became flesh. It is communal because God came among a people. It is sacramental because divine presence is not anti-material. It is heavenward because the Son has united heaven and earth in Himself. Thus the Church’s worship is the historical continuation, by participation, of the communion inaugurated in principle at the Incarnation.

Finally, the Church’s life remains eschatological. The Incarnation has begun what Revelation will unveil completely. God dwells among humanity in Christ now; God will dwell openly with humanity in the new creation then. The Church lives between these poles as the people of Emmanuel, already tasting restored presence and awaiting its unveiled consummation.

**Section Summary:** The Church’s worship and identity are grounded in the Incarnation because Christ is the true tabernacle whose bodily presence, word, and sacramental life gather believers into communion with God.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing church life as remembrance of absent Christ → to understanding it as participation in the incarnate Lord who remains the meeting place of God and humanity.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps prevent reduction of salvation to forgiveness alone by showing that God’s redemptive purpose includes embodied nearness and restored communion.

Second, it deepens preaching on Christmas and Incarnation by presenting the Word made flesh as sanctuary fulfillment, not merely inspirational wonder.

Third, it enriches sacramental theology by grounding Eucharistic and ecclesial life in Christ as the true temple and tabernacle.

Fourth, it strengthens Christian anthropology by showing that human nature is assumed, dignified, and restored in Christ.

Fifth, it shapes spiritual formation by centering communion with God in the person of the incarnate Son rather than in abstraction or technique.

Sixth, it provides the necessary foundation for the following chapters on anamnesis, veil, access, and heavenly enthronement.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Doesn’t emphasizing the Incarnation as communion in principle downplay the necessity of the cross?

**Reply:** No. The chapter explicitly distinguishes principle from consummation. The Incarnation inaugurates divine nearness personally; the cross, resurrection, ascension, and gift of the Spirit accomplish and open that communion fully. The chapter honors the whole economy by not reducing one moment to another.

### Objection 2: Is the temple/tabernacle language in John 1 and John 2 too metaphorical to bear this weight?

**Reply:** The language is certainly richly theological, but it is not thin metaphor. John intentionally uses sanctuary language to interpret Christ’s person as the locus of divine dwelling and glory.

### Objection 3: Does this make Christ merely a “place” rather than a person?

**Reply:** Not at all. The whole point is that sanctuary and meeting-place categories are fulfilled personally. Christ is not impersonal sacred space. He is the living Son in whom all these realities are embodied.

### Objection 4: If Christ is the true temple, does the Church still need liturgical forms?

**Reply:** Yes, because fulfillment in Christ does not abolish embodied participation. The Church’s liturgical life becomes the communal and sacramental way of abiding in the incarnate Lord’s opened communion.

## Conclusion

The Word tabernacled among us. With that confession the whole sanctuary logic of Scripture is drawn into Christ. The God who once met and spoke from above the Mercy Seat now draws near in person. The dwelling once concentrated in tent and temple is now embodied in the Son. Bethel is fulfilled because heaven and earth are open over Him. Temple is fulfilled because His body is the true sanctuary. Emmanuel is fulfilled because God is with us not as distant benefactor but as incarnate Lord.

This is why the Incarnation must be seen as already communion in principle. God has crossed the distance from His side. He has not abandoned humanity to upward striving, nor contented Himself with symbolic nearness. He has come. In Christ, divine presence is historical, tangible, personal, and revelatory. Divine speech is no longer only inscribed on tablets or mediated through prophetic utterance; it is embodied in the eternal Word made flesh.

This does not lessen the importance of the cross, resurrection, ascension, or Pentecost. Rather, it gives them their proper context. The one who dies, rises, ascends, and pours out the Spirit is already the one in whom God has drawn near. The whole work of Christ unfolds from the Incarnation’s astonishing claim: the fullness of God dwells bodily among humanity. Thus redemption must never be reduced to forgiveness alone. It is the restoration of communion through the one who is Himself the meeting place of God and man.

For the Church, this means that worship, hearing, sacrament, and participation are all irreducibly christological. She comes to God only in and through the incarnate Son. Yet in coming to Him she finds not merely an intermediary but the very dwelling of God. The Church’s life is therefore life around Emmanuel, life in the true temple, life under the opened heaven of the Son of Man.

The chapter’s answer to its central question can thus be stated plainly. How does God dwell among His people in Christ? He dwells among them by becoming one of them without ceasing to be Himself, by taking flesh as the true tabernacle of glory, and by making His own person the definitive place of access, communion, and divine speech. Everything before prepared for this. Everything after unfolds from it. In Christ, God has drawn near decisively, and communion is no longer only hoped for in promise. It has appeared among us in the flesh.
