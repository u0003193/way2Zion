# Chapter 28  
## Divine Speech in Communion

### Thesis

Communion leads to hearing, and hearing renews the nous. This is not an incidental devotional observation but a central biblical pattern. God does not merely grant His people access so that they may remain silent observers in sacred space. He brings them into communion so that He may meet with them and speak with them. Divine speech is therefore not an optional supplement to communion; it is one of communion’s central acts. In the heavenly liturgy, before the throne of God and the Lamb, the Church is not only present. She is addressed. And this address renews her perception, reorders her understanding, and begins the transformation that leads toward humility, self-offering, and worshipful obedience.

This chapter argues that communion → hearing → renewed nous is a central biblical pattern and one of the most important original claims of the book. The pattern is already visible in Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89, where meeting and speech are inseparably joined at the Mercy Seat. It is intensified in the heavenly throne-room participation of Hebrews 12 and Revelation 4–5, where communion with God in Christ opens into a speaking reality. Divine speech there is not merely propositional information delivered into a vacuum. It is covenantal address within participation. God speaks where He gathers His people near.

This makes Chapter 28 one of the theological spine chapters of the whole project. Everything before prepares for it. Chapter 11 established the centrality of meeting and speech. Chapters 26 and 27 showed that Christian worship is participation in heavenly liturgy and throne-room communion. Now Chapter 28 must make explicit the next step: in communion, God speaks. The hearing Church is formed because the worshiping Church is addressed. This is the hinge by which sanctuary theology becomes anthropology and spiritual formation. The pattern does not remain external. It enters the person.

The chapter therefore answers its central question directly: does it successfully establish communion → hearing → renewed nous as a central biblical pattern? The answer this chapter argues is yes, because divine speech in Scripture belongs to the sphere of divine presence and divine meeting. It is not a detached data transfer. It is the speech of the enthroned God to the people He has brought near. The nous is renewed not merely by study in the abstract, but by hearing within communion. The Word of God, received in the liturgy of heaven and earth joined in Christ, becomes the power by which perception is healed, reality is seen rightly, and the person is prepared for humility.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 25:17–22; Numbers 7:89; Deuteronomy 4:10–13; 1 Kings 19:9–18; Psalm 95; Isaiah 6:1–10; Hebrews 1:1–4; Hebrews 3:7–4:16; Hebrews 10:19–25; Hebrews 12:18–29; Revelation 1:10–20; Revelation 2–3; Revelation 4–5; and Revelation 8. These are read together with Luke 24:45, Romans 12:1–2, and the broader liturgical-throne-room logic established in the preceding chapters.

Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 remain foundational, establishing that the place of meeting is the place of speech. Deuteronomy 4 and Psalm 95 emphasize Israel as a people constituted by hearing the voice of God. Isaiah 6 shows that participation in heavenly worship leads to divine address and prophetic mission. Hebrews 1 presents the Son as God’s climactic speech; Hebrews 3–4 warns and exhorts the Church to hear that speech “today”; Hebrews 12 ties heavenly Zion and divine voice together. Revelation 1–5 and 8 show the enthroned Christ speaking within the heavenly liturgical context, especially to His churches in Revelation 2–3.

Together these texts establish that divine speech is mediated in communion and that hearing is the essential bridge from participation to transformation.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

Part VI is the book’s great hinge, and Chapter 28 stands at its center. Chapter 26 argued that Christian worship is participation in heavenly liturgy. Chapter 27 specified that this participation is throne-room participation, the heavenly fulfillment of the Mercy Seat’s meeting-and-speech pattern. The question now becomes immediate and decisive: what happens there? If the Church participates in heavenly worship and stands in communion before the throne, what is the nature of that communion? The answer of Scripture is that God speaks. Divine speech is the act by which communion becomes formative.

This question matters because much Christian reflection on worship and spiritual life tends to separate presence from speech or to treat speech as mere instruction detached from liturgical communion. Worship may be imagined as affective encounter, and Scripture reading or preaching as the didactic portion that follows it. But the biblical pattern is deeper. Where God gathers His people, He addresses them. Meeting and speech belong together. The hearing Church is therefore not a church that adds attention to its worship; it is a church that recognizes that worship itself is the sphere of divine address.

The prompt rightly frames the chapter with exceptional rigor. This chapter is one of the book’s theological spine chapters because the originality and coherence of the whole project depend on it. If communion does not lead to hearing, then the later sequence of renewed nous, transformed desire, and humility loses its primary biblical basis. But if it does, then the whole movement from sanctuary to anthropology becomes intelligible. The Church is transformed because she hears within participation.

The issue must also be distinguished from generic doctrines of revelation. Of course God speaks in Scripture and through prophets and apostles. But the concern here is not revelation in abstraction. It is liturgical and communion-shaped hearing. The point is that divine speech belongs to access, to worship, and to the presence of God. This chapter therefore stands at the meeting point of biblical theology, liturgy, ecclesiology, and spiritual anthropology.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks what happens in heavenly participation and answers that the worshiping Church is addressed by God there, making hearing the central bridge from communion to transformation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From separating worship and word too sharply → to seeing divine speech as the defining act within communion itself.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate accounts obscure the force of the communion → hearing → renewed nous pattern.

One inadequate account reduces divine speech to information transfer. God speaks, and His people receive propositions or commands. These may be true and important, but if this is all that speech means, then its covenantal and liturgical depth is lost. In Scripture, God’s speech does not merely inform; it addresses, summons, judges, reveals, and reconstitutes. It occurs in relation to presence.

A second inadequate account treats worship as primarily non-verbal or affective encounter. Divine presence is then emphasized, but speech is muted or subordinated. Yet Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 do not allow this split. The God who meets is the God who speaks. Presence without address is not the biblical pattern of communion.

A third inadequate account isolates hearing from communion. The word of God is then encountered mainly in private study, theological discourse, or moral instruction apart from the liturgical and heavenly context of worship. Such practices matter greatly, but if hearing is detached from communion, theologically it becomes disembedded. The book’s argument is not that God only speaks in corporate worship, but that the pattern of divine speech is fundamentally communion-shaped.

A fourth inadequate account understands renewed perception mainly as human reflection upon speech rather than divine address acting within communion. This makes transformation more self-driven than grace-driven. The biblical witness points the other way. The voice of God is itself powerful, discerning, searching, and life-giving.

A fifth inadequate account postpones divine speech in a decisive sense to the past canon alone, such that worship today becomes merely a reading of prior speech without present address. This is dangerously thin. The Church indeed hears the canonical Scriptures, but in and through them the risen Christ speaks now, especially in the liturgical assembly gathered before Him.

These approaches fail because they separate what Scripture joins: worship, presence, word, and transformation. The chapter therefore seeks to recover divine speech as the central act of communion that renews the person from within.

**Section Summary:** Informational, affective-only, de-liturgized, self-reflective, and past-only accounts of divine speech all fail to capture its present, communion-shaped, transformative character.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating hearing as detached cognition or private reflection → to understanding it as divine address within worshiping communion.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### The Mercy Seat Pattern: Meeting and Speech

Exodus 25:22 provides the controlling biblical pattern: “There I will meet with you, and from above the mercy seat . . . I will speak with you.” Numbers 7:89 shows this promise enacted: Moses hears the voice speaking from above the Mercy Seat between the cherubim. The theological significance of these texts has already been established in Chapter 11, but here their implications are drawn forward. The place of communion is the place of hearing. God does not merely admit His servant into proximity. He speaks.

This pattern is foundational because it ties together worship, access, presence, and revelation. Speech is not an unrelated second stage. It is internal to communion. The people are drawn near so that they may hear God.

### Israel as a Hearing People

Deuteronomy 4 and Psalm 95 both underscore that the covenant people is constituted by hearing. At Horeb the people are gathered to hear the words of God. Psalm 95 warns, “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts.” This is not simply moral exhortation. It assumes that life with God turns on hearing His voice. The people of God are hearers before they are performers.

This is especially relevant for the book’s central pattern. Hearing precedes renewal and obedience. The people do not first renovate themselves into fitness for divine speech. They are addressed, and then their hearts are exposed and formed.

### Isaiah 6: Presence, Purification, Speech

Isaiah 6 offers a crucial paradigm. The prophet sees the Lord enthroned, hears the seraphic worship, is convicted of uncleanness, is purified, and then hears the divine commission. This sequence is deeply aligned with the argument of Part VI. Heavenly participation and presence lead to divine speech, and divine speech sends and shapes the hearer.

Isaiah 6 is important not because it is identical to Christian corporate liturgy in every respect, but because it displays a biblical grammar: throne-room presence is not mute; it is the context of hearing. The address of God in the place of worship becomes the beginning of transformation and mission.

### Hebrews: The Speaking Son and the Hearing Church

Hebrews makes the theology of divine speech particularly strong. Hebrews 1 begins by declaring that God has spoken definitively in His Son. Speech is now christological in its fullness. Hebrews 3–4 then presses the liturgical and existential urgency of hearing that speech “today,” as the Spirit says. The Church is warned not to harden her heart. Hebrews 4:12–13 presents the word of God as living and active, discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart. This is speech not merely heard but penetrating and judging.

Then Hebrews 12 joins this directly to heavenly Zion. The people have come to Mount Zion, and the writer warns, “See that you do not refuse him who is speaking.” The sequence is unmistakable: heavenly participation and divine speech belong together. This is one of the strongest biblical supports for the chapter’s thesis.

### Revelation: The Enthroned Christ Speaks

Revelation 1–5 and especially Revelation 2–3 make the point with extraordinary force. John is “in the Spirit on the Lord’s Day,” caught into heavenly-liturgical vision, and there hears the voice of the risen Christ. The throne-room setting is not silent spectacle. It is the sphere from which Christ addresses His churches. Each letter ends, “He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches.”

This is decisive. The Church’s relation to throne-room participation is not observational only. The ascended Christ speaks from within that heavenly reality to His gathered people. Revelation 8 strengthens the same dynamic by locating the prayers of the saints within heavenly worship and then depicting response proceeding from that sphere.

### Divine Speech as Transformative

Across these texts, divine speech is not neutral. It judges, warns, comforts, commissions, and renews. It reveals God, exposes the heart, and reconstitutes the people. The hearing of God’s speech in communion therefore becomes the decisive bridge to renewed perception. The book’s thesis that divine speech renews the nous is not a speculative psychology. It is the next step of a thoroughly biblical pattern.

### Communion → Hearing → Renewed Nous

The scriptural claim may now be stated in its full shape. God brings His people near; in that nearness He speaks; through that speech the person is confronted, illumined, and renewed. Worship is therefore not one thing, word another, and transformation a third only loosely connected effect. They belong together organically within the divine economy of communion.

**Section Summary:** Scripture consistently presents the place of divine communion—from the Mercy Seat to Zion to the throne room—as the place of divine speech, and that speech acts powerfully to expose, renew, and form God’s people.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating divine speech as external message only → to seeing it as the active, renewing address of God within the place of communion.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers consistently understand divine speech as transformative and as belonging within ecclesial communion rather than outside it.

Origen, when handled with discernment, is especially strong on the relation between Scripture and spiritual hearing. He sees the word of God as living address that must be received in the Church and that works transformation in the hearer. Though his allegorical tendencies must be carefully governed, his instinct that divine speech is formative rather than merely informative is deeply useful here.

Athanasius helps by insisting that in the Son God has spoken His own self-disclosure. The Word is not only message but the living one in whom revelation is personal and saving. This strengthens the chapter’s christological foundation: divine speech is ultimately the speech of the Son and is received in communion with Him.

Cyril of Alexandria contributes significantly through his emphasis on Christ’s presence in the Church and the Spirit’s role in teaching and illumining believers. Hearing is not left to bare human interpretation. It is ecclesial, christological, and pneumatic.

Maximus the Confessor is especially important for the later link to the nous. For Maximus, the human person is renewed as the Logos heals distorted perception and reorders the soul. Divine speech is therefore noetically transformative. It illumines reality and enables the person to perceive rightly.

The Orthodox liturgical tradition likewise assumes that worship is the place of divine address. The readings, prayers, hymns, and proclamations of the Church are not merely commemorative texts. They are the living sphere in which the Spirit speaks through the word to the gathered people. This makes liturgical hearing central to ecclesial life.

Thus the patristic tradition broadly confirms the chapter’s claim: the Church hears God in worship, and such hearing is transformative. Divine speech in communion is a living patristic instinct as well as a biblical one.

**Section Summary:** Patristic theology confirms that divine speech is living, christological, ecclesial, and transformative, renewing the hearer within the Church’s worshiping communion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From reading Scripture as static content only → to hearing it as the living address of God that renews perception within the Church.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that communion → hearing → renewed nous is a central biblical pattern because God’s purpose in bringing His people near is not only their presence before Him but their transformation by His address. The place of communion is the place of speech, and divine speech renews human perception.

Several affirmations give this synthesis clarity.

First, divine speech is intrinsic to communion. God does not merely receive worship; He addresses worshipers.

Second, this speech is christological. God has spoken climactically in the Son, and the risen Christ continues to speak to His Church.

Third, this speech is liturgical and heavenly in context. It belongs to the throne-room reality into which the Church is admitted.

Fourth, this speech is transformative. It penetrates, judges, illumines, and renews the inner person.

Fifth, hearing precedes renewed nous. The mind is not renewed by autonomous reflection alone but by receiving the divine address in worshiping communion.

Sixth, this sequence grounds what follows in the book. Renewed perception leads to transformed desire, humility, self-offering, obedience, and worship.

This doctrinal synthesis makes Chapter 28 the hinge it must be. The whole book’s interior sequence turns on the truth that divine speech is not an abstract category but a lived event within communion.

**Section Summary:** The chapter resolves its central question by showing that God’s meeting with His people culminates in divine speech, and that such speech renews the nous as the beginning of transformation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From regarding worship and renewal as loosely related → to understanding divine speech in communion as the hinge of spiritual transformation.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter has unusually significant anthropological consequences because it identifies hearing, rather than self-assertion, as central to human restoration. Human beings were created for communion and therefore for receptive participation in the speaking God. Sin distorts not only desire and behavior, but hearing itself. To be renewed is to become capable again of truthful hearing.

This means the nous is fundamentally responsive. It is healed not first by self-construction but by divine address. The modern tendency to imagine transformation as arising from internal resolution, therapeutic insight, or moral effort is deeply challenged here. Renewal begins when God speaks and the person hears.

This also has immediate implications for desire. What the person hears shapes what the person loves. False speech in Genesis 3 deformed desire; true speech in communion begins to heal it. The whole later argument of the book depends on this. One cannot explain transformed desire without first explaining renewed hearing.

Humility also begins to emerge here. The hearer is not master of reality. He stands under a word he did not author. Divine speech relativizes pride, exposes illusion, and calls the person into truth. Thus hearing God is already the beginning of humility, even before the chapter dedicated to humility arrives.

Spiritually, the chapter encourages believers to take hearing with utmost seriousness. Worship is not merely expressive activity. It is the sphere where God addresses His people. Attention, receptivity, silence, obedience, and discernment become crucial precisely because hearing is the path of renewal.

**Section Summary:** Human restoration begins in truthful hearing, because the nous is renewed by divine address and desire, humility, and obedience grow from what the person receives from God.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From imagining change begins with human effort → to seeing that transformation begins when the worshiping person hears God rightly.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church is, in the deepest sense, a hearing Church. Her identity is not exhausted by sacramental participation, heavenly access, or communal praise, though all these are essential. She is the people to whom the risen Christ speaks. This means that the liturgical assembly is not only gathered to offer worship but also to receive address.

Word and worship therefore cannot be separated. The reading of Scripture, the proclamation of the Gospel, the preaching of Christ, the sung psalms and hymns, and the responses of the people all belong to one liturgical reality of divine speech within communion. The Church is not only speaking to God; God is speaking to the Church.

This also clarifies ecclesial formation. The Church becomes what she hears. Her holiness, discernment, desire, and obedience are shaped not first by organizational strategy but by faithful hearing in the sphere of worship. The Church’s deformities are often deformations of hearing before they are anything else.

The liturgical and heavenly dimensions remain inseparable here. The Church hears not as a secular lecture audience, but as a throne-room people gathered in Christ. Divine speech comes within the already-and-not-yet of heavenly participation. Thus the Church’s hearing is reverent, expectant, communal, and formative.

**Section Summary:** The Church is the people addressed by God in worship, and her liturgical life is the communal sphere in which divine speech forms her identity and communion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating church proclamation as mere instruction → to understanding it as divine address within heavenly participation.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps pastors and teachers recover the deep unity of worship and word.

Second, it offers a strong biblical basis for treating proclamation as divine address rather than merely religious instruction.

Third, it strengthens liturgical theology by showing that heavenly participation naturally leads to hearing.

Fourth, it clarifies spiritual formation by locating the renewal of the mind in divine speech rather than self-generated effort.

Fifth, it provides the crucial conceptual bridge to Chapter 29 on the renewed nous.

Sixth, it helps the Church recover reverent attentiveness in worship as essential to communion.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Does this overstate the immediacy of divine speech in the Church?

**Reply:** No, if carefully qualified. The chapter does not deny the mediation of Scripture, proclamation, and liturgy. It insists that through these means the living God actually addresses His people.

### Objection 2: Could this lead to subjectivism about hearing God?

**Reply:** Not if the ecclesial, scriptural, and liturgical context is preserved. The speech in view is not private impression detached from the Church, but divine address mediated through Christ and the apostolic word in worship.

### Objection 3: Is hearing really prior to renewed nous?

**Reply:** In the logic of this book and the biblical pattern traced here, yes. The mind is renewed because it is addressed truthfully by God in communion.

### Objection 4: What about silent contemplation—does this chapter marginalize it?

**Reply:** No. Silence may be one mode of receptive hearing. The chapter simply insists that contemplation itself is situated under and within divine address, not apart from it.

## Conclusion

Communion leads to hearing, and hearing renews the nous. That is the central claim of this chapter and one of the central claims of the whole book. God does not bring His people near merely so that they may stand in reverent silence before inaccessible glory. He brings them near so that He may meet with them and speak with them. The Mercy Seat already revealed this. The heavenly liturgy and throne room fulfill it. The hearing Church lives from it.

This means that divine speech is not secondary to communion but one of its essential acts. In worship the Church is not merely active toward God; she is receptive before God. The throne room is the place of meeting and speech. The Lamb mediates access there. The Spirit makes hearing possible there. And from there the word of God goes forth to expose, renew, and transform.

The chapter also secures the whole sequence that follows. If hearing is real, then renewed nous becomes theologically intelligible. If the mind is renewed by divine address, then transformed desire and humility follow not as arbitrary ethical requirements but as fruits of truthful communion. The whole architecture of the book depends on this hinge.

Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question is clear: yes, Scripture does establish communion → hearing → renewed nous as a central biblical pattern. And because it does, the Church must recover worship as the place where heaven’s liturgy becomes earth’s hearing, where presence becomes address, and where the human person begins truly to see reality as it is before God.
