# Chapter 11  
## “There I Will Meet with You and Speak with You”

### Thesis

Exodus 25:17–22 and Numbers 7:89 disclose one of the deepest centers of the entire biblical story: God redeems, orders worship, establishes priesthood, and gives the sanctuary so that He may meet with His people and speak with them. These texts reveal not a peripheral liturgical detail but the ultimate purpose of redemption itself. Sacrifice serves access. Access serves meeting. Meeting serves speech. Speech serves communion. In these verses the whole movement of the book comes into concentrated theological form. If the controlling thesis of this project is that God created humanity for communion with Himself, that sin disrupted communion, and that Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat and opens access to the heavenly Holy of Holies so that believers may participate in communion with God, then Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 stand as among the most decisive scriptural witnesses to that logic.

This chapter argues that “There I will meet with you and speak with you” expresses God’s ultimate purpose in redemption. God does not merely forgive in order to clear a record. He does not merely sacrifice in order to resolve guilt. He does not merely ordain priesthood in order to maintain ritual order. He does not merely establish the tabernacle in order to localize holiness. He does all these things so that communion may become possible: God and humanity in covenantal nearness, at peace, in holy fellowship, with divine speech renewing the people from within. The goal is not sacrifice as such, but access; not access as such, but meeting; not meeting as such, but communion through divine address.

This chapter therefore serves as a theological center of the book. Many preceding chapters prepare for it, and many subsequent chapters flow from it. Chapter 7 established that covenant, presence, and liturgy are one fabric. Chapter 8 showed that Passover is redemption for communion. Chapter 9 demonstrated that Sinai forms a people by divine speech. Chapter 10 explained why the Ten Words lie beneath the Mercy Seat. Now Chapter 11 gathers all of this into the single declaration that God meets and speaks with His people from above the Mercy Seat. Here we are given the divine “why” of sanctuary, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, and worship. They exist because God desires access to His people—not access in the sense of domination, but access in the sense of reconciled communion, covenantal presence, and speaking fellowship.

For that reason, this chapter must be read not as one chapter among many but as one of the book’s axial claims. It names the center from which the book’s entire theological movement unfolds: communion → hearing → renewed nous → transformed desire → humility → self-offering → obedience → worship → Zion. God meets. God speaks. Therefore humanity may hear, be renewed, and become a people fit for His presence. The sanctuary’s deepest truth is not distance but reconciled nearness. The Mercy Seat’s deepest truth is not only covering, but meeting and speech. And the gospel’s deepest truth is that in Christ this purpose reaches fulfillment.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary texts for this chapter are Exodus 25:17–22 and Numbers 7:89, read in close relation to Exodus 24:3–8; Exodus 29:42–46; Leviticus 16; Psalm 27; Psalm 63; Psalm 84; John 1:14–18; John 10:1–18; Hebrews 4:14–16; Hebrews 8–10; Hebrews 12:18–24; and Revelation 21–22. These are also held together with the book’s governing passages, especially Luke 24:45 and Romans 12:1–2.

Exodus 25:17–22 establishes the Mercy Seat atop the ark and culminates in God’s declaration: “There I will meet with you, and from above the mercy seat . . . I will speak with you.” Numbers 7:89 narrates the fulfillment of this promise as Moses hears the voice speaking to him from above the Mercy Seat between the cherubim. Exodus 29 already explains that the purpose of the tent of meeting is that God may meet, speak, sanctify, and dwell among His people. Leviticus 16 provides the Day of Atonement logic by which access to the holy place becomes possible. Hebrews interprets the sanctuary, priesthood, sacrifice, and access in Christological fulfillment. Revelation shows that the final state of redemption is the dwelling of God with humanity, where His servants worship Him and see His face.

Taken together, these texts reveal that meeting and speech are not incidental outcomes of redemption but its very goal.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

This chapter stands at one of the highest points in the argument of the book. The preceding chapters have gradually built the theological structure necessary to understand it. Humanity was created for communion. Christ is the hermeneutical and mediating center of Scripture. The Spirit forms and renews the person. Human beings are created for perception, hearing, and worship. Divine speech stands at the heart of covenant communion. Covenant, presence, and liturgy are one fabric. Passover reveals redemption for communion. Sinai shows that God’s people are formed by hearing. The Ten Words beneath the Mercy Seat show that divine command belongs within divine presence. Now the question becomes inescapable: toward what center do all these realities move?

The answer lies in God’s own declaration: “There I will meet with you and speak with you.” This is not a mere description of ritual location. It is a statement of divine intent. The key issue before the chapter is therefore theological, not merely exegetical: why does God desire access to His people? What does it mean that the Holy One orders sacrifice, priesthood, sanctuary, and covenant around a place of meeting and speech? Why is the climax of the tabernacle instructions not simply correct ritual performance, but the promise of divine-human encounter?

This question is crucial because many accounts of redemption stop too soon. They explain why sacrifice is needed, why atonement matters, why priesthood mediates, or why holiness requires boundaries. All of these concerns are vital. But unless they are gathered under the larger telos of meeting and speech, they remain incomplete. The biblical text itself presses beyond them. God wants to meet His people. God wants to speak with them. The architecture of the sanctuary exists because communion, not merely acquittal, is the end.

The chapter must therefore demonstrate that Exodus 25:17–22 and Numbers 7:89 are not interesting details hidden within ceremonial legislation. They function as a center of gravity for the sanctuary logic of the Pentateuch and, indeed, for the entire biblical story. The pattern sacrifice → access → meeting → speech → communion must be made explicit. When this pattern is seen, the whole shape of the book becomes coherent. Everything before prepares for it; everything after unfolds from it.

**Section Summary:** This chapter identifies Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 as a theological center of the book and asks why sacrifice, sanctuary, and priesthood culminate in the promise of divine meeting and speech.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing sanctuary and atonement as ends in themselves → to recognizing them as ordered toward God’s desire to meet and speak with His people.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate ways of reading the sanctuary obscure the centrality of meeting and speech.

One inadequate reading reduces the tabernacle primarily to ritual management. On this view the sanctuary is chiefly a system for maintaining purity, organizing sacrifice, and safeguarding divine holiness. These concerns are undeniably present. But if they are treated as the main point, the sanctuary becomes a religious machine. What is lost is the divine purpose expressed in Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89. Ritual exists, but it exists for meeting and speech.

A second inadequate reading treats sacrifice as the final goal. Many theological accounts rightly stress atonement, blood, and reconciliation. Yet if the logic stops at sacrifice, it has not yet reached the sanctuary’s purpose. The Day of Atonement, for example, does not exist because blood is intrinsically the end. Blood serves access. The cleansing of the holy place, the mediation of the priest, and the removal of impurity are all ordered so that God and His people may dwell together. Sacrifice is essential, but it is not ultimate.

A third inadequate reading construes priesthood mainly as hierarchical sacred office. Such a reading can either exalt priesthood as ritual power or reduce it to institutional mediation. But the biblical question is more teleological: why does priesthood exist? The answer is not simply “to officiate sacrifice.” It is to make holy approach possible, to mediate access, and thus to serve the divine desire to meet and speak with His people.

A fourth inadequate reading spiritualizes communion and minimizes speech. Here God’s presence is affirmed, but in such a way that speech becomes secondary, perhaps even incidental. Yet Exodus 25:22 explicitly binds the two together. God does not say simply, “There I will be present,” but “There I will meet with you, and . . . I will speak with you.” Presence and address are inseparable. Communion is dialogical.

A fifth inadequate reading severs the Old Testament sanctuary from its fulfillment in Christ, treating these texts as merely obsolete cultic structures. Yet Hebrews insists that the sanctuary pattern is not meaningless but fulfilled. Christ opens the true holy place precisely because the old sanctuary spoke truly in shadow form about access, meeting, and divine address. If one discards the old pattern rather than understanding its fulfillment, one truncates the gospel’s own imagery and depth.

All of these inadequate readings fail because they stop at means and miss the end. The chapter must therefore recover the sanctuary’s teleological center: sacrifice serves access, access serves meeting, meeting serves speech, and speech serves communion.

**Section Summary:** Readings that absolutize ritual, sacrifice, priesthood, mute presence, or discontinuity with Christ all fail because they miss the sanctuary’s true purpose in meeting and speech.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From focusing on sanctuary mechanisms alone → to seeing their teleological movement toward divine-human encounter and address.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### The Mercy Seat and the Promise of Meeting

Exodus 25:17–22 brings the tabernacle instructions to a point of extraordinary concentration. The ark is described, the Mercy Seat is fashioned, the cherubim overshadow it, and then God declares: “There I will meet with you.” The force of this statement should not be weakened. The sanctuary does not merely symbolize a general religious truth. It names a place of divine initiative. God appoints the locus where He will meet with His people. The entire architecture of holiness is drawn toward this promise.

This means that the sanctuary is fundamentally relational. Holy furniture, sacred space, and ritual order are not final realities in themselves. They are ordered toward encounter. The Holy One makes a place where He and His people may meet without the annihilation that sin would otherwise entail. The Mercy Seat is therefore not simply a cultic surface but a theological center: the place where holiness and mercy, throne and approach, distance and nearness converge.

### “And I Will Speak with You”

The promise does not end with meeting. God adds, “and from above the mercy seat . . . I will speak with you.” Here the theological center of the book comes into view. Meeting and speech belong together. Communion is not mute proximity. God’s desire is not merely that His people exist nearby, but that they receive His address. Divine speech within communion is thus part of the goal of redemption, not an accessory benefit.

This is decisive for the book’s whole argument. If God speaks in communion, then hearing becomes foundational to transformation. The renewed nous, reordered desire, and obedient worship described later in the book all presuppose this divine speech. The Mercy Seat is therefore not only a place of atoning possibility; it is a place of formative address. The God who meets is the God who speaks.

### Numbers 7:89: The Promise Enacted

Numbers 7:89 narrates the fulfillment of Exodus 25:22 with sober precision: Moses enters the tent of meeting to speak with the Lord, and he hears the voice speaking to him from above the Mercy Seat between the cherubim. This verse is easily overlooked, but it is one of the most luminous in the Pentateuch. It shows that the sanctuary’s promise is not theoretical. God actually meets and speaks from that place. The tabernacle is not an unrealized symbol but a functioning site of covenant communion.

This verse also emphasizes the continuity between sanctuary architecture and lived encounter. The speech from above the Mercy Seat confirms that the whole system of sacrifice, priesthood, and holiness was never simply about management of impurity. It was ordered toward speech. The voice is the climax. The people of God are being brought into a world where divine presence becomes divine address.

### Exodus 29: Why God Dwells Among His People

Exodus 29:42–46 provides the broader theological frame. There God says that the tent of meeting is the place where He will meet with Israel, speak to them, sanctify them, dwell among them, and be their God. These verses make unmistakably clear that the sanctuary has a teleological center: not ritual correctness for its own sake, but divine-human dwelling and speech. Sanctification itself is ordered toward this relation. The holy God makes holy space, holy mediation, and holy people for the sake of communion.

This also answers the prompt’s question about why God established the tabernacle, priesthood, and worship. The tabernacle exists because God desires to dwell among His people. Priesthood exists because holy mediation is necessary for that dwelling. Worship exists because a people in God’s presence must respond in praise, sacrifice, thanksgiving, and obedience. All of this converges in meeting and speech.

### Leviticus 16: Sacrifice for Access

Leviticus 16 clarifies the sacrificial logic underlying the Mercy Seat. On the Day of Atonement, the holy place is cleansed, blood is carried within the veil, and the priest approaches the most holy reality of Israel’s liturgical world. The purpose is not blood for blood’s sake. The purpose is access for a sinful people whose uncleanness would otherwise make divine dwelling impossible. The sacrificial system is therefore teleological. It serves the place where God meets and speaks.

This helps articulate the pattern at the center of the chapter: sacrifice → access → meeting → speech → communion. The order is vital. Sacrifice is necessary because access is necessary. Access is necessary because meeting is the goal. Meeting matters because speech is the form of covenant communion. Communion is the end because God created humanity for Himself.

### Christological Fulfillment in John and Hebrews

John’s Gospel and Hebrews gather this sanctuary logic into Christ. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us. In Christ, God does not merely speak from above the Mercy Seat; He speaks in and through the incarnate Son. John 10 reveals the shepherd whose sheep hear His voice. Hebrews 4, 8–10, and 12 deepen the sanctuary pattern. Through Christ’s priesthood and sacrifice believers gain access to the true holy place and are warned not to refuse Him who is speaking. Thus the centrality of meeting and speech is not left behind in the old covenant but fulfilled and intensified in Christ.

This is why Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 can serve as a controlling center for the entire biblical story. They name in concentrated form what Christ fulfills fully: through sacrifice, access is opened; in access, God meets His people; in meeting, He speaks; and in speech, communion becomes formative and life-giving.

### Revelation and the Eschatological End

Revelation 21–22 reveals the consummation of the same movement. The dwelling of God is with humanity. His servants worship Him. They see His face. The movement from sanctuary to city, from hearing to seeing, does not abolish the logic of meeting and speech; it consummates it. The God who desired access to His people in Exodus is the God who will dwell with them openly at the end.

Thus the scriptural witness is united: redemption, sanctuary, sacrifice, priesthood, worship, speech, and eschatology all converge on the divine desire to meet and speak with His people.

**Section Summary:** Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 reveal that the sanctuary’s true goal is divine meeting and speech, a pattern rooted in sacrifice and access, fulfilled in Christ, and consummated in Revelation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing the Mercy Seat as mainly cultic object → to recognizing it as the theological center where redemption’s purpose in communion is most clearly declared.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers of the Church, though often not centering these exact verses in the same way as this book, nevertheless provide a theological framework that strongly confirms their significance.

Irenaeus is foundational because he reads salvation history as God’s gradual accustoming of humanity to His presence. God dwells with humanity, speaks to humanity, and brings humanity into fuller and fuller participation. This broad vision illuminates Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89. The sanctuary is one stage in the divine pedagogy by which God prepares a people for communion. Meeting and speech are thus not incidental but central to God’s historical purpose.

Origen, when read with discernment, helps make visible the inward and christological meaning of sanctuary language. He understands the holy place and divine speech as realities that must culminate in Christ and in the transformation of the hearer. While his allegorical instincts sometimes move too quickly, he rightly senses that these texts concern more than external ritual. They concern God’s communicative presence.

Athanasius strengthens the Christological side of the argument. If the incarnate Word is the definitive self-disclosure of God, then the sanctuary’s logic of speech from the place of meeting finds its fulfillment in Him. What was once spoken from above the Mercy Seat is now embodied in the Son. God’s purpose has always been communicative communion, and Christ is its fullest realization.

Gregory of Nyssa’s treatment of Moses emphasizes divine encounter as transformative ascent. Moses does not merely receive information from God; he is drawn into increasing intimacy, mystery, and participation. This helps frame Numbers 7:89 not as a bare report of prophetic hearing but as an instance of the wider human vocation to be formed in relation to the speaking God.

Maximus the Confessor provides an especially fruitful conceptual framework. For Maximus, the Logos is both the ground and goal of creation, and human life is healed and unified by participation in Him. Divine speech is therefore not accidental to communion; it is one of the principal forms in which communion occurs. The person is renewed by the address of God, and the cosmos finds coherence in the divine Logos. This deeply supports the chapter’s claim that speech is central to redemption’s end.

Orthodox liturgical theology also bears witness to the unity of meeting and speech. The Church’s worship is full of petitions for peace, wisdom, hearing, illumination, and communion, because the faithful understand themselves to be meeting God in order to hear and respond. The sanctifying presence of God is never conceived as mute. The liturgy continues the sanctuary logic in fulfilled form.

The patristic witness therefore strengthens the chapter on three fronts: it confirms that salvation is participatory rather than merely forensic, that divine presence is communicative rather than silent, and that Christ fulfills the sanctuary as the true locus of meeting and speech.

**Section Summary:** Patristic theology confirms that God’s historical purpose is to dwell with, address, and transform humanity, and that Christ fulfills the sanctuary’s logic of meeting and speech.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating these texts as isolated cultic promises → to seeing them within the fathers’ broader vision of participatory and communicative communion.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter may be stated with directness: Exodus 25:17–22 and Numbers 7:89 express God’s ultimate purpose in redemption because they reveal that sacrifice, priesthood, sanctuary, and worship exist so that God may gain holy access to His people for the sake of meeting and speaking with them in covenant communion.

Several doctrinal affirmations emerge from this.

First, redemption is teleological. God redeems not merely to remove bondage or guilt but to restore divine-human fellowship. Passover and Exodus are therefore subordinate to a larger purpose: the dwelling and speech of God among His people.

Second, sacrifice serves access. It is not the end. The blood of atonement matters because sinful humanity cannot otherwise enter or survive holy presence. Sacrifice opens the possibility of communion.

Third, access serves meeting. There is no value in holy approach as abstraction. God does not desire a cleansed empty space; He desires reconciled nearness with His people.

Fourth, meeting serves speech. Communion with God is dialogical. He addresses His people. His speech forms them, reveals His will, renews their perception, and binds them to Himself.

Fifth, speech serves communion. God speaks not only to command but to communicate Himself, to create covenant identity, to renew the nous, to transform desire, and to form a worshiping people. This is why divine speech is so central to the book’s controlling thesis.

Sixth, Christ fulfills the whole pattern. He is sacrifice, access, place of meeting, incarnate Word, and mediator of communion. In Him the purpose expressed at the Mercy Seat reaches fullness. God meets humanity and speaks definitively in the Son, and through His priestly work believers are brought near to the heavenly sanctuary.

This doctrinal synthesis explains why this chapter stands so near the center of the book. It names the core logic from which the later chapters on divine speech, renewed nous, humility, worship, and Zion all unfold. The chapter’s central formula—sacrifice → access → meeting → speech → communion—summarizes not only the sanctuary but the gospel.

**Section Summary:** The chapter resolves its central question by showing that the entire sanctuary economy is ordered toward divine-human meeting and speech, fulfilled climactically in Christ.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From stopping at atonement, priesthood, or access → to seeing their true end in God’s desire to meet and speak with His people.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter carries immense anthropological weight because it shows what kind of creature the human being truly is. Humanity is not merely a moral agent needing correction or a guilty party needing acquittal. Humanity is a being made for encounter and address. To be human is to be created for communion with the living God, and therefore to be created to hear Him. If God’s purpose in redemption culminates in meeting and speech, then the deepest truth about human nature is receptive participation in divine life.

This means that spiritual formation must be understood primarily in terms of what happens when God meets and speaks. The renewed nous is not the result of abstract discipline alone. It arises because the person is brought into the sphere of divine address. Speech from God reorders perception. The person begins to see reality differently because he has heard it differently. Desire then changes because what is now seen as true begins also to appear as good.

The chapter also clarifies humility. Humility is not first a human technique for qualifying oneself for access. It is the fruit of meeting and hearing. When God meets a person and speaks truth, illusions about the self are undone. One becomes truthful because one has stood in the presence of the Holy One who speaks. Thus humility grows from encounter, just as the prompt requested.

Likewise, obedience becomes intelligible as response rather than self-generated morality. If divine speech is central to communion, then obedience is what happens when the person receives and answers the word of God. This protects against moralism. The Christian obeys not in order to generate communion, but because communion has become a living reality through meeting and speech.

This also shapes desire in a positive sense. The human heart is formed by what it hears from the One it loves. Divine speech does not merely regulate desire from outside. It educates and redirects desire from within. The believer begins to hunger for God, His holiness, His truth, His Zion, because He has been addressed by God Himself.

Spiritually, then, the chapter helps explain why worship, Scripture, sacrament, and contemplative attention are so essential. They are not religious add-ons. They are the living environment in which the people of God continue to meet and hear the Lord.

**Section Summary:** Meeting and speech reveal that the human person is created for encounter and address, and that humility, obedience, and transformed desire arise as fruits of that divine-human relation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing salvation as chiefly moral or juridical repair → to understanding it as the restoration of the person to hearing communion with God.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church lives from the fulfilled reality of what Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 promised. In Christ, the people of God are brought near to the true holy place. The Church therefore exists as the assembly where God meets and speaks with His people through the Spirit in word and sacrament. The ecclesial and liturgical implications are profound.

First, worship exists because God desires communion. The Church does not gather to perform religious actions for their own sake. She gathers because the God who redeemed her continues to meet her. Liturgy is the communal habitation of that meeting.

Second, proclamation is central because God’s presence is speaking presence. Scripture is read, preached, chanted, confessed, and sung because the Church understands herself as a hearing people standing before the living God. The liturgy of the word is thus not preparatory padding. It is one of the principal forms of divine-human meeting.

Third, sacrament intensifies rather than replaces this logic. The God who speaks also gives Himself. The Church’s Eucharistic life makes visible the pattern at the heart of this chapter: access, meeting, communion. Word and table belong together because God meets and speaks.

Fourth, ecclesial identity is sustained by this meeting-and-speech. The Church is not simply an institution preserving religious memory. She is continually formed by divine address. Her unity, holiness, mission, and hope all depend on the fact that she remains the people whom God meets and to whom He speaks.

Fifth, the Church’s worship is eschatological. Hebrews 12 and Revelation show that the assembly already participates in heavenly Zion. The liturgy is therefore not a retreat from history but an anticipation of the final dwelling of God with humanity. The Church hears now in faith what she shall one day behold in sight. The goal is still communion, and the path is still meeting and speech.

Thus the Church stands as the new-covenant realization of the sanctuary’s deepest purpose. She exists because in Christ the Mercy Seat’s logic has been fulfilled. God desires access to His people, not to consume them but to dwell with, form, and glorify them. The Church is where that mystery is already tasted.

**Section Summary:** The Church fulfills the sanctuary’s purpose by living as the assembly where God meets and speaks with His people in word, sacrament, worship, and anticipatory participation in heavenly Zion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating church worship as religious routine → to understanding it as the fulfilled sphere of divine meeting and speech in Christ.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter reshapes preaching of salvation. Christians should be taught not only what Christ saves them from, but that He saves them for divine meeting and speech.

Second, it deepens teaching on worship. Liturgy can be explained as the communal form of God’s desire to meet and address His people, rather than merely as inherited ceremony.

Third, it strengthens sacramental catechesis. Word and sacrament belong together because the God who meets also speaks and gives Himself.

Fourth, it corrects moralism. Obedience should be preached as the fruit of divine-human encounter rather than as the mechanism for manufacturing intimacy with God.

Fifth, it enriches spiritual formation. Prayer, Scripture, silence, and liturgical attentiveness can be framed as practices of standing before the God who meets and speaks.

Sixth, it clarifies the whole architecture of the book. Readers can now see that many later themes—divine speech, renewed nous, humility, worship, Zion—flow from this theological center.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Isn’t it too much to make Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 a controlling center of the entire biblical story?

**Reply:** Not if the texts are read canonically. They concentrate in a few words the purpose already implied in redemption, covenant, priesthood, sanctuary, and divine indwelling, and they are fulfilled explicitly in Christ and the Church.

### Objection 2: Doesn’t this chapter risk minimizing sacrifice by subordinating it to meeting and speech?

**Reply:** No. It gives sacrifice its true dignity by placing it in relation to its purpose. Sacrifice matters supremely because without it access, meeting, and communion would remain impossible for sinful humanity.

### Objection 3: Could “meeting and speech” simply describe prophetic revelation rather than the broader purpose of redemption?

**Reply:** Numbers 7:89 certainly involves prophetic hearing, but it does so within the sanctuary architecture of covenant presence. The narrative itself places this speech at the center of the tabernacle’s purpose, not at its edges.

### Objection 4: Does this make God’s desire for access sound needy?

**Reply:** No. God’s desire is gracious, not needy. He seeks access to His people because He wills their good, their communion, and their participation in His life. Divine desire here is the expression of holy generosity.

## Conclusion

“There I will meet with you and speak with you.” These words disclose the divine purpose at the heart of redemption. God desires access to His people not merely to command from afar, not merely to display holiness, and not merely to regulate worship, but to bring sinful humanity into reconciled communion where meeting and speech become possible. Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 therefore do not sit at the margins of biblical theology. They stand near its center.

The whole sanctuary economy is ordered toward this end. Sacrifice serves access. Access serves meeting. Meeting serves speech. Speech serves communion. The tabernacle exists because God intends to dwell among His people. Priesthood exists because holy approach requires mediation. Worship exists because a meeting people must answer in praise, thanksgiving, confession, and obedience. The law lies beneath the Mercy Seat because command belongs within mercy and presence. Everything converges here.

This also reveals why the chapter is so important for the whole book. Later chapters on divine speech, renewed nous, desire, humility, and worship all depend on this center. One cannot understand why divine speech renews the person unless one first understands that God’s purpose in redemption includes speaking in communion. One cannot understand why humility is a fruit of encounter unless one first sees that God actually meets and addresses His people. One cannot understand why worship matters unless one sees that it is the communal form of living before the God who meets and speaks.

In Christ, the truth of these verses reaches fullness. He is the fulfilled Mercy Seat, the true place of meeting, the incarnate Word, the priest who opens access, and the one through whom believers are brought into the heavenly sanctuary. In Him God meets with His people and speaks with them in final and living form. The Church now exists from that reality, hearing in faith what she shall one day behold in sight.

Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question is simple and inexhaustible: God desires access to His people because He desires communion with them. He desires to dwell with them, to form them by His truth, to renew them by His speech, and to bring them at last into the Zion where hearing becomes seeing. Everything before prepares for this. Everything after unfolds from it. And so the words spoken over the ark remain among the most luminous in all Scripture: “There I will meet with you, and I will speak with you.”
