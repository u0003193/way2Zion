# Chapter 21 Excursus  
## Already and Not Yet

### Thesis

The life of communion opened in Christ is already real and not yet complete. The Church already has access to the Father through the Son in the Spirit. She already participates in heavenly Zion, already hears the voice of the risen Lord, already receives the life of the age to come in sacramental and ecclesial form, and already belongs to the new creation inaugurated in Christ’s resurrection. Yet she does not yet see face to face. She does not yet dwell in the unveiled fullness of Revelation 21–22. She does not yet experience communion without weakness, distraction, sin, death, or liturgical mediation. The Christian life is therefore lived within the tension of inaugurated communion: possessed truly, enjoyed partially, and awaited fully.

This excursus argues that the already-and-not-yet is not a secondary eschatological refinement but a necessary theological category for the entire book’s argument. Without it, one will either overrealize communion—as though the Church already possessed the final form of divine-human fellowship—or underrealize it—as though access and participation were merely future hopes without substantial present reality. Both distortions must be rejected. The whole biblical movement from Eden to sanctuary, from sanctuary to Christ, and from Christ to new Jerusalem teaches that what is promised can be truly present before it is finally consummated. The Incarnation, Eucharist, torn veil, ascension, and Lord’s Day all witness to this.

This excursus is especially necessary after Chapter 21 because resurrection, ascension, heavenly enthronement, and Lord’s Day theology can be misunderstood in two opposite directions. One might conclude that because Christ is risen, ascended, enthroned, and present in heavenly worship, the Church already possesses the final state in full. Or one might conclude that because the final state has not yet come, present communion is mostly symbolic or aspirational. The scriptural witness supports neither conclusion. The Church already participates in the ascended Christ and in heavenly worship, but she does so under the conditions of pilgrimage, sacrament, hope, and partial sight.

This means the already-and-not-yet belongs directly to the book’s central movement. Communion is already opened. Hearing is already granted. The renewed nous is already being formed. Desire is already being healed. Humility, self-offering, obedience, and covenantal worship are already real. Yet all remain unfinished, contested, and directed toward final transfiguration. The people of God are therefore a pilgrim communion: truly brought near, still awaiting fullness; truly indwelt, still longing for unveiled presence; truly gathered into Zion, still asking the way toward its consummation. The already-and-not-yet does not weaken the theology of communion. It makes it truthful.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this excursus include Luke 17:20–21; John 4:23–24; John 14:1–3, 16–23; Romans 8:18–25; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 1 Corinthians 13:8–12; 2 Corinthians 3:17–18; Ephesians 2:4–22; Colossians 3:1–4; Hebrews 10:19–25; Hebrews 12:22–24; and Revelation 21–22. These are read alongside the wider biblical patterns of Exodus 25:17–22, Numbers 7:89, Acts 1–2, and the resurrection-ascension themes of Chapter 21.

Luke 17 and John 4 show that the kingdom and worship have already taken a new form in Christ. John 14 promises indwelling presence while still orienting disciples toward future dwelling. Romans 8 and 1 Corinthians 13 insist that real participation and real groaning coexist. Ephesians 2 and Hebrews 12 declare that believers already have access and have come to heavenly Zion. Revelation 21–22 shows that this participation is not yet the final unveiled state. Together these texts make clear that the life of communion is inaugurated now and consummated later.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

The book’s governing claim is that God created humanity for communion with Himself, that sin disrupted communion, and that Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat, opens access, and grants participation in communion with God. Yet once this claim is taken seriously, a pressing question emerges: in what sense do believers possess this communion now, and in what sense do they still await it? This excursus answers that question by clarifying the already-and-not-yet structure of redemptive participation.

The need for this clarification arises from the very success of the previous chapters. If one has properly seen that Christ is the true tabernacle, that the Eucharist is participatory anamnesis, that the veil is torn, that He fulfills Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, temple, and Mercy Seat, and that the ascended Christ now gathers the Church into heavenly worship, one may be tempted toward overrealization. Why not say simply that the Church already has all of communion now? Yet the persistence of suffering, sin, weakness, death, liturgical mediation, and hope itself suggests otherwise. Scripture also repeatedly directs the people of God toward a future consummation: seeing face to face, the marriage supper, the removal of the curse, and the dwelling of God without temple or veil.

At the same time, if one emphasizes the future too strongly, another distortion arises. Present access can begin to look thin or merely symbolic. Worship becomes memory of what is not yet real. Participation becomes psychological aspiration. The sacraments become gestures toward absence rather than gifts of presence. Such underrealization is equally incompatible with Scripture. Hebrews does not say believers will one day come to Zion only; it says they have come. Paul does not say the cup will one day be communion only; he says it is communion.

The chapter’s question is therefore not whether communion is present or future, but how it is both. The already-and-not-yet offers the proper framework. In Christ, communion is real, substantial, and participatory now. In Christ, communion is also unfinished, anticipatory, and directed toward unveiled fullness. This tension defines the Church’s life between ascension and parousia.

**Section Summary:** The excursus asks how believers can already possess communion while still awaiting its fullness, and argues that this tension is essential to a truthful theology of redemptive participation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From forcing a choice between present communion and future hope → to understanding Christian life as inaugurated but incomplete participation in God.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several common approaches distort the balance required here.

One inadequate approach is overrealized eschatology. This can take different forms. Sometimes it appears sacramentally, as though the Church’s present worship were already indistinguishable from consummated glory. Sometimes it appears spiritually, as though believers could or should already possess unbroken intimacy, complete transformation, or fully healed desire. But this fails to account for the New Testament’s persistent language of hope, waiting, groaning, partial sight, and unfinished holiness. It neglects the remaining weight of mortality and pilgrimage.

A second inadequate approach is underrealized eschatology. This reading acknowledges future fulfillment but thins present participation. Communion becomes mostly aspirational. Access becomes mainly legal status without experiential or liturgical depth. The Eucharist becomes memory without participation. Worship becomes earthly symbolism waiting for heaven to become real. But such a reading cannot account for Hebrews 12, 1 Corinthians 10, or the language of indwelling in John and Paul.

A third inadequate approach separates ecclesial experience from eschatology. In such accounts, “already and not yet” is treated as a technical doctrine about kingdom chronology rather than as the living grammar of worship, sanctification, and spiritual formation. Yet the tension is deeply practical. It shapes how the Church prays, hopes, approaches the sacraments, understands suffering, and interprets her partial communion.

A fourth inadequate approach makes the future merely quantitative, as though what is already present now were simply “more of the same” later. But Scripture suggests that the not-yet is not only increase but unveiling, transfiguration, and consummation. The Church now participates truly, but then she shall see fully. Sacrament gives way to sight, faith to unveiled glory, pilgrimage to home.

A fifth inadequate approach resolves the tension by collapsing the Church either into heaven or into history. The former neglects frailty, sin, and longing; the latter neglects access, indwelling, and heavenly participation. The New Testament insists on both: believers are seated with Christ and still groan for redemption of their bodies.

These distortions fail because they refuse the paradoxical but necessary structure of New Testament communion. The excursus therefore seeks to articulate that structure in a way coherent with the whole book.

**Section Summary:** Overrealized, underrealized, overly technical, merely quantitative, and collapsed readings all fail to honor the Church’s real present participation and real continuing hope.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From simplifying the tension of Christian life → to receiving the already-and-not-yet as the proper grammar of communion.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### Already: Access Granted

Hebrews 10:19–25 and 12:22–24 are among the strongest texts for the “already” dimension. Believers have confidence now to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus. They are summoned now to draw near. They have come now to Mount Zion, to the city of the living God, to Jesus the mediator, and to the sprinkled blood. This language is not merely promissory. It is present and participatory. The Church already stands within the reality Christ has opened.

Ephesians 2 reinforces this. Through Christ both Jew and Gentile have access in one Spirit to the Father. Access is not postponed until the eschaton. It is the present gift of the reconciled people of God. This is crucial for the book’s communion theology. The Church already lives from opened access.

### Already: Communion Given

Paul’s Eucharistic language in 1 Corinthians 10 makes the same point through sacramental life. The cup is communion in the blood of Christ, and the bread is communion in His body. The Church is not waiting for communion to become real in some wholly future sense. She receives it already in liturgical participation. Likewise, John 14 speaks of the Father and the Son making their dwelling with believers by the Spirit. Present indwelling is real.

The resurrection and ascension themes of Chapter 21 also support this. The Spirit is poured out now. The Lord’s Day exists now. Heavenly worship is entered now in faith and liturgical participation. The “already” is therefore robust and must not be weakened.

### Not Yet: Sight Awaited

Yet Scripture is equally clear that the final form of communion has not arrived. Romans 8 declares that creation groans and that believers themselves groan as they await the redemption of their bodies. What is hoped for is not yet seen. First Corinthians 13 says that now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Revelation 21–22 portrays a state in which there is no curse, no temple, no death, and no need of mediating light because God and the Lamb are immediately present.

This future is not rhetorical flourish. It means the Church’s present communion, though real, is incomplete. The veil is torn, but faith remains. The Eucharist nourishes, but it is not yet the marriage supper in unveiled fullness. The Spirit indwells, but the body still awaits resurrection glory. The Church is already in Zion and still on pilgrimage to Zion’s consummation.

### The Indwelling Tension in John

John 14 provides one of the richest scriptural expressions of the tension. Jesus promises that He and the Father will dwell with believers in the Spirit. Yet in the same discourse He also speaks of going to prepare a place and of coming again. Presence is therefore both given and awaited. The disciples are not abandoned as orphans, but neither are they yet in the fully prepared dwelling. This is the already-and-not-yet of communion in christological and relational form.

### Worship in Spirit and Truth

John 4 adds another dimension. The hour is coming and is now here when true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth. Here again, future and present interpenetrate. The eschatological hour has broken into the present in Christ. Worship is already transfigured. Yet its full horizon still stretches forward into the complete manifestation of what Christ has begun.

### Participation and Transformation

Second Corinthians 3:17–18 also reveals this structure. Believers already behold the glory of the Lord and are already being transformed from glory to glory. Yet transformation is progressive. The communion is real but not complete. This text is especially valuable for the book’s later emphasis on renewed nous and transformed desire. The already-and-not-yet is not static. It is formative and pilgrim.

### Revelation as Consummation

Revelation 21–22 must not be flattened into continuity without consummation. The dwelling of God with humanity, the removal of every curse, and the beatific vision indicate that the not-yet remains indispensable. The Church cannot simply claim to have everything now. Yet the book’s whole logic also prevents deferral of communion into futurity alone. The Lamb is already in the midst of heavenly worship. The saints already sing. The paradox must be preserved.

**Section Summary:** Scripture holds together a robust present access and communion with a still-future unveiled fullness, teaching that the Church already participates in what she does not yet fully possess.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating present participation or future consummation in isolation → to embracing both as the biblical shape of life in Christ.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers frequently inhabit this tension even when they do not name it with later systematic vocabulary.

Irenaeus is especially helpful because his theology of recapitulation and growth resists both triumphal completion and thin postponement. Humanity is restored truly in Christ and yet still growing toward incorruption, vision, and fullness. This supports the claim that communion is already restored in principle and not yet consummated in experience.

Athanasius, too, holds together victory and ongoing struggle. Christ has destroyed death, yet believers still await the full manifestation of resurrection life. Divine life has entered humanity truly, but its final transfiguration of all things lies ahead. This is precisely the balance needed here.

Gregory of Nyssa’s theology of endless ascent offers a particularly rich way of thinking about the not-yet without diminishing the already. The soul truly participates in God now, yet God’s infinity means that participation is always dynamic and deepening. While this must be coordinated carefully with eschatological finality, it offers a valuable idiom for partial real participation without closure.

Maximus the Confessor strongly supports the excursus because his liturgical and cosmic vision assumes present participation in heavenly realities while also orienting everything toward eschatological fulfillment. The Church does not merely wait; she already participates. Yet her participation is not yet the full unveiling of all things in Christ.

The Orthodox liturgical tradition embodies this tension with unusual clarity. The Church proclaims that the kingdom is blessed now, enters heavenly worship now, receives the life of the age to come now, and yet continually prays, “Thy kingdom come.” The liturgy is perhaps one of the Church’s clearest lived testimonies to the already-and-not-yet.

**Section Summary:** Patristic and liturgical theology affirm that divine life is truly participated in now while still stretching toward future consummation and unveiled glory.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating participation as either complete or deferred → to inhabiting the fathers’ dynamic balance of real communion and pilgrim fulfillment.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this excursus is that the Church’s communion with God is inaugurated and real because Christ has already accomplished redemption, opened access, ascended into the heavenly sanctuary, and poured out the Spirit; yet this communion remains incomplete because the final unveiling of divine presence, resurrected bodily glory, and perfected worship awaits the consummation.

This synthesis includes several claims.

First, the “already” is objective and participatory. It is grounded in Christ’s finished work and present mediation, not in human religious feeling.

Second, the “already” is ecclesial and sacramental. The Church actually participates now in heavenly Zion, Eucharistic communion, and Spirit-indwelling life.

Third, the “not yet” is equally real. Sin persists, death remains, sight is partial, worship is mediated, and the full dwelling of God with humanity is still awaited.

Fourth, the “not yet” does not cancel the “already.” Rather, it gives it a pilgrim and hopeful character.

Fifth, the already-and-not-yet is formative. It shapes the Church into a people of faith, hope, patience, repentance, thanksgiving, and desire.

Sixth, the whole book’s movement depends on this structure. Communion is already given enough for hearing, renewal, desire-transformation, humility, obedience, and worship truly to begin; it is not yet complete enough that these realities cease to require growth, struggle, and hope.

**Section Summary:** The Church truly participates now in Christ’s communion-opening work, yet awaits its final unveiled fullness, and this tension shapes the whole Christian life.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From thinking Christian communion must be either fully present or fully future → to understanding it as inaugurated participation moving toward consummation.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This excursus helps explain the Christian experience of tension. Believers truly know God and yet long for more. They truly worship and yet remain distracted. They truly hear and yet remain partially deaf. They truly commune and yet still suffer loneliness, death, temptation, and weakness. This is not proof that communion is unreal; it is the anthropological shape of life between resurrection inauguration and new creation consummation.

The already-and-not-yet thus protects the believer from despair and illusion alike. Despair says, “Because fullness is absent, nothing is really given.” Illusion says, “Because something is given, nothing more need be awaited.” The biblical pattern rejects both. Something decisive has been given in Christ. Much still remains to be unveiled.

This has direct bearing on the renewed nous. The mind is being renewed truly, but not instantly or perfectly. Desire is being healed, but not without struggle. Humility is growing, but not without failure. Self-offering becomes possible, but not without resistance. Worship is real, but still under signs and sacraments. The already-and-not-yet therefore gives the book’s transformation sequence its experiential plausibility.

Spiritually, this also teaches patience. The Christian life is not stagnation, but neither is it immediate consummation. It is participatory pilgrimage. One already belongs to Zion and still asks the way to Zion. One already tastes the heavenly gift and still longs for the wedding feast. One already hears the Shepherd and still yearns for the day when hearing becomes seeing.

**Section Summary:** The already-and-not-yet explains why Christians truly participate in communion while still experiencing struggle, growth, longing, and partiality.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From misreading spiritual tension as failure or triumph → to recognizing it as the normal shape of pilgrim communion in Christ.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church’s liturgical life is one of the clearest places where the already-and-not-yet is enacted. In worship the Church blesses the kingdom already, hears the voice of Christ already, communes in His body and blood already, and approaches the Father already. Yet she also prays for the coming kingdom, proclaims the Lord’s death until He comes, and awaits the new Jerusalem. Liturgy is thus the school in which the Church learns to live truthfully between gift and consummation.

This means the Church must neither trivialize her present access nor absolutize her present experience. She must worship with boldness because Christ has opened the way, and with longing because the way leads still further into glory. Her sacraments are not empty signs of a distant hope, nor are they the elimination of all eschatological waiting. They are anticipatory participation.

Ecclesially, the already-and-not-yet also shapes mission and patience. The Church is already the people of God and not yet perfected. She already shares in heavenly worship and still ministers amid earthly sorrow. She is already temple-body and still groans with creation. This keeps ecclesiology from triumphalism and from despair alike.

Thus the excursus belongs fittingly after Chapter 21. The ascended Christ really gathers the Church into communion now, but the very fact that He is the forerunner means that the Church follows where He has gone and awaits full arrival. The Lord’s Day is one recurring rehearsal of that tension: resurrection joy already, consummated sight not yet.

**Section Summary:** The Church’s worship, sacramental life, and mission are shaped by the already-and-not-yet as anticipatory participation in the communion Christ has opened but not yet fully unveiled.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From flattening ecclesial life into either present possession or future absence → to seeing the Church as the pilgrim people of already-given and not-yet-complete communion.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this excursus helps prevent triumphalism by reminding believers that communion is real but not yet consummated.

Second, it prevents reductionism by affirming that present worship and sacraments are genuinely participatory.

Third, it gives a truthful framework for spiritual struggle, growth, and hope.

Fourth, it clarifies the Church’s liturgical life as anticipatory participation rather than bare symbolism.

Fifth, it strengthens eschatological preaching by holding together present access and future unveiling.

Sixth, it provides an interpretive key for the rest of the book’s transformation themes, explaining why real change coexists with incompletion.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Doesn’t the already-and-not-yet weaken assurance by emphasizing incompletion?

**Reply:** No. It strengthens assurance by grounding present communion in Christ’s accomplished work while honestly acknowledging that final consummation is still awaited.

### Objection 2: Could this tension become an excuse for spiritual stagnation?

**Reply:** Not biblically. The not-yet is not passivity but hope-filled movement. The Church is already being transformed and therefore summoned to active faith, worship, and obedience.

### Objection 3: If believers have already come to Zion, why still speak of pilgrimage?

**Reply:** Because Hebrews itself joins present access with future hope. The Church is truly there in principle and participation, and still journeys toward unveiled fullness.

### Objection 4: Does this diminish the Eucharist by saying fullness is not yet present?

**Reply:** No. It honors the Eucharist as true participation while refusing to collapse sacramental presence into eschatological completion.

## Conclusion

The life of communion opened by Christ is already and not yet. It is already because the Son has come, died, risen, ascended, entered the heavenly sanctuary, poured out the Spirit, and gathered His Church into access, worship, and participation. It is not yet because the Church still waits for resurrection consummation, unveiled vision, perfected worship, and the new Jerusalem in which the dwelling of God with humanity will be complete.

This tension is not a problem to solve but a truth to inhabit. It explains why the Church can say with Hebrews that she has come to Mount Zion, and with Revelation that she still awaits the holy city coming down from heaven. It explains why the Eucharist is true communion and still a foretaste. It explains why believers are truly renewed and still groan. It explains why worship is already heavenly participation and still pilgrim liturgy under signs.

For the larger argument of the book, this excursus is indispensable. Without it, communion theology may become either triumphalist or thin. With it, the whole movement of the book becomes experientially credible: God speaks in communion now; the nous is renewed now; desire is transformed now; humility, self-offering, obedience, and worship truly begin now. Yet all these remain ordered toward final consummation when hearing becomes seeing.

Thus the already-and-not-yet is the proper grammar of the Church’s life under the ascended Christ. She is already near and still journeying. Already filled and still hungry. Already gathered and still longing. Already at the table and still waiting for the feast. And because this is true, her worship, hope, patience, and desire are all kept in right proportion before the living God.
