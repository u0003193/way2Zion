# Chapter 7  
## Covenant · Presence · Liturgy as One Fabric

### Thesis

Covenant, divine presence, and liturgy are not three adjacent themes within the biblical story. They are one fabric. Covenant is the ordered relation by which God binds a people to Himself. Presence is the reality toward which covenant moves and within which covenant lives. Liturgy is the enacted form of that covenantal presence as received, guarded, celebrated, and inhabited by the people of God. None of these can be rightly understood in isolation. Covenant without presence collapses into legal arrangement. Presence without covenant becomes formless immediacy. Liturgy without either becomes religious performance. But when held together, they reveal the deep logic of Scripture: God redeems a people in order to dwell among them, speak with them, receive their worship, and form them into a communion-bearing community.

This chapter therefore asks a central question for Part II of the book: why are covenant and liturgy necessary if God’s goal is communion? The answer is that communion with the Holy One is not abstract fusion, inward sentiment, or unmediated spontaneity. It is a holy, ordered, responsive, and communal participation in the life of God. Covenant names the divine initiative and relation; presence names the reality of God-with-us; liturgy names the communal grammar by which that relation is enacted and sustained. The sanctuary, the feasts, the sacrifices, the priesthood, the psalms, and the assembly of Israel are not arbitrary additions to a simpler religion of inward devotion. They are the pedagogical, covenantal, and liturgical forms through which a sinful people are taught how to dwell before the living God.

This claim is crucial to the architecture of the book because Part II is moving toward Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 as the theological center: “There I will meet with you and speak with you.” That center can only be properly appreciated if the preceding chapters have shown why covenant and liturgy belong intrinsically to the life of divine presence. The tabernacle and temple are not ornamental. The rites of Leviticus are not detachable ceremonial appendices. The psalms are not merely private devotion. They belong to the one reality of covenantal presence. God is forming a people able to live before Him in holiness, hearing, offering, obedience, and joy. Worship is therefore the covenantal form of communion.

This chapter also extends the logic of the earlier chapters. If God created humanity for communion, if Christ opens access to the Holy of Holies, if the Spirit renews the person, if covenantal love is the telos, and if divine speech is the heart of communion, then the question necessarily arises: what kind of communal, embodied, historical pattern corresponds to such realities? The answer is liturgy in the richest biblical sense. Liturgy is not merely ceremony. It is the embodied life of a covenant people dwelling in and responding to divine presence. In that sense, covenant, presence, and liturgy are one fabric because communion itself requires form.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 19–25; Exodus 29:42–46; Leviticus 1–10; Leviticus 16; Psalm 24; Psalm 27; Psalm 63; Psalm 84; Psalm 95; Psalm 100; Hebrews 8–10; Hebrews 12:18–29; and Revelation 4–5; 7; 21–22. These are read together with the governing passages of the entire book, especially Exodus 25:22; Numbers 7:89; Romans 12:1–2; and Hebrews 10:19–22.

Exodus 19–25 establishes the covenant at Sinai, the divine descent, and the pattern of sanctuary and presence. Exodus 29 explicitly states the purpose of God’s dwelling: “I will dwell among the people of Israel and will be their God.” Leviticus shows how the holy God makes ordered approach possible through sacrifice, priesthood, and ritual forms. The Psalms reveal the sanctuary not as dead machinery but as the place of longing, praise, hearing, and glad approach. Hebrews rereads covenant, sacrifice, sanctuary, and access christologically, showing their fulfillment in Christ without denying their coherence in the old covenant. Revelation portrays heavenly worship and the final dwelling of God with humanity, proving that liturgy and presence belong not merely to Israel’s provisional arrangements but to the consummating life of God’s people.

Taken together, these texts demonstrate that covenant is ordered toward presence, presence is enacted in liturgical life, and liturgy is the communal form of holy communion.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

Part I of this book argued that humanity was created for communion, that Christ is the hermeneutical and mediating center of Scripture, that the Spirit renews and forms the person, that the human being is created for perception and worship, that covenantal love is the telos of existence, and that divine speech lies at the heart of communion. We now enter Part II, where the logic of redemption for communion must be traced through the sanctuary, sacrifice, priesthood, and liturgical life of Israel. Before turning to specific institutions and texts, however, a more basic synthesis is necessary. One must first understand why covenant, divine presence, and liturgy belong together in the first place.

This question matters because many Christians instinctively separate what Scripture holds together. Covenant is often treated as legal framework. Presence is treated as mystical feeling. Liturgy is treated as either ritual formalism or denominational preference. The result is a fragmented theology. One might speak of salvation in covenantal terms without much attention to worship. Or one might speak of worship without seeing its covenantal structure. Or one might speak of divine presence in individualistic terms detached from communal order. In each case, the biblical pattern is thinned.

The chapter therefore asks: why are covenant and liturgy necessary if God’s goal is communion? If the purpose of redemption is fellowship with God, why not immediate access without ordered forms, mediating rites, or communal grammar? Why tabernacle? Why altar? Why priests? Why feasts? Why psalms? Why sacrifice? Why temple? Why the ordered assembly of a people around sacred time, sacred space, sacred speech, and sacred action?

The answer must begin with God’s holiness and humanity’s creaturely and fallen condition. Communion with the living God is not mere closeness. It is holy fellowship. It therefore requires divinely given order. Covenant is the form of that relation because God binds Himself to a people in promise, command, and presence. Liturgy is the shape of that relation because embodied creatures need pedagogies of approach, remembrance, offering, and response. Presence is the end because God’s purpose is not merely obedience as such but dwelling—God with His people and His people before Him.

The chapter thus stands as a threshold to the sanctuary theology that follows. It prepares the way for Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 by showing that those verses do not hover in isolation. They belong to a fully integrated biblical world in which covenant, presence, and worship are one reality lived in different dimensions.

**Section Summary:** The chapter frames the theological need to hold covenant, divine presence, and liturgy together, showing that the sanctuary world of Scripture cannot be understood if these are treated as separate themes.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From fragmented treatments of covenant, worship, and presence → to an integrated biblical vision in which all three belong to one fabric of communion.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate approaches distort the relation among covenant, presence, and liturgy.

One common reduction treats covenant primarily as legal contract. In this model the covenant is chiefly a set of stipulations and consequences: blessings for obedience, curses for disobedience, and a juridical framework for divine-human relations. There is, of course, truth in this. Biblical covenants include oaths, laws, conditions, and sanctions. But if these features are isolated from divine presence, covenant becomes contractual rather than communal. Scripture’s covenantal language is far richer. “I will be your God, and you shall be my people” is not merely legal status. It names belonging, nearness, and shared life. Covenant exists for communion.

A second inadequate approach treats divine presence as formless immediacy. In some modern spiritualities, presence is understood chiefly as inward awareness, emotional nearness, or mystical atmosphere. This is often attractive because it seems to bypass complex structures and ritual forms. Yet the biblical witness consistently resists such simplification. God’s presence is holy, covenantal, and ordered. He dwells in the midst of His people, but not as undifferentiated religious experience. He gives patterns, boundaries, mediations, and times of approach. Presence without covenant and liturgy becomes a projection of human spirituality.

A third inadequate approach treats liturgy as expendable ceremony. On this view the rites of Israel and the formal worship of the Church are secondary packaging around the “real” substance of faith. What matters is inward sincerity, moral commitment, or doctrinal conviction; liturgy is at best an aid and at worst an obstacle. But this does not accord with Scripture. God Himself gives the tabernacle pattern, appoints feasts, orders sacrifice, sanctifies priesthood, commands song, and gathers a people around shared enactments of His presence. Liturgy is not accidental. It is covenant enacted.

A fourth inadequate approach overcorrects in the other direction by absolutizing liturgy. In such cases ritual form becomes self-justifying, almost as though the mere maintenance of sacred action were the end. Yet worship exists for communion, not communion for worship. Liturgical form without divine presence and covenantal belonging degenerates into performance. This is why Scripture repeatedly denounces empty sacrifices and hollow feasts when the heart and the communal life are disordered.

A fifth inadequate approach spiritualizes presence and worship in ways that detach them from Christ. The old covenant is then treated as a merely external system, while the new covenant is imagined as inward spirituality without structure. But Hebrews does not support such a contrast. It shows fulfillment, intensification, and opening of access in Christ, not the abolition of covenantal and liturgical logic. The heavenly liturgy of Revelation confirms this. Worship and presence remain constitutive of the people of God, but now in Christological fulfillment.

These inadequate approaches fail because they isolate one dimension of biblical theology from the others. The chapter aims instead to recover the scriptural claim that covenant, presence, and liturgy form a single fabric ordered to communion.

**Section Summary:** Contractual, mystical, anti-liturgical, hyper-liturgical, and over-spiritualized readings all fail because they detach covenant, presence, and worship from their biblical unity.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From narrowing one element of the biblical pattern into the whole → to seeing covenant, presence, and liturgy as mutually interpretive realities.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### Sinai and the Birth of a Liturgical People

Exodus 19–24 establishes that Israel is not merely delivered from Egypt but brought to God. Redemption is therefore already ordered toward covenant and presence. “You have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to myself” (Exod. 19:4). The covenant begins with divine initiative and nearness. Yet this nearness is immediately accompanied by boundaries, consecration, speech, sacrifice, and assembly. The people wash their garments, gather at the mountain, hear the voice, receive the words, and enter covenant through blood.

This is profoundly important. Communion does not bypass form. God brings the people near, but He brings them near in ordered holiness. Sinai is thus already liturgical in the broad biblical sense. Time is sanctified, bodies are prepared, the people are assembled, the word is proclaimed, sacrifice is offered, and the covenant is sealed. Worship is not an optional layer added after covenant. It is one of the very modes in which covenantal communion is enacted.

### “I Will Dwell Among Them”

Exodus 25–29 makes the purpose of the sanctuary explicit. God commands that a sanctuary be made “that I may dwell in their midst” (Exod. 25:8). The logic is then intensified in Exodus 29:42–46, where the tent of meeting is identified as the place where God meets His people, speaks to them, sanctifies them, and dwells among them. Several realities converge here: meeting, speech, sanctification, covenant identity, and presence. None stands alone.

This is the essential point. The tabernacle is not primarily a piece of religious architecture, but the concrete form of God’s covenantal indwelling among a sinful people. The rituals attached to it are not arbitrary ceremonial burdens. They are the grammar of holy approach. Divine presence is so central that the entire cultic order of Israel is organized around it. Covenant exists so that God may dwell among His people. Liturgy exists because dwelling with a holy God requires ordered response.

This prepares directly for Exodus 25:22. The Mercy Seat becomes intelligible only within this larger logic. It is the place where covenant, presence, and liturgical mediation converge into meeting and speech.

### Leviticus: Holy Approach as Communal Grammar

Leviticus is often misread as a dense catalogue of obsolete ritual regulations. Yet read in the light of communion, it appears as pedagogy. The offerings, priestly consecrations, purifications, calendar, and holy distinctions all exist so that Israel may live before the Lord without being consumed. Leviticus 1–10 establishes patterns of approach and mediation; Leviticus 16 climaxes this logic in the Day of Atonement; the holiness code then extends the reality of divine presence into communal and ethical life.

Several points deserve emphasis. First, worship in Leviticus is never merely expressive. It is covenantally given. Second, holiness is not an abstract moral ideal but the condition of living presence. Third, liturgical action shapes communal identity. The people become holy as they learn how to approach, receive, distinguish, and remember. The sanctuary and the life of the camp are joined because the God who dwells among them claims the whole life of the people.

Thus liturgy is not opposed to communion but is one of its necessary forms for a fallen people. Worship is the covenantal form of communion because it is the holy enactment of nearness.

### The Psalms: Presence Desired, Worship Lived

The Psalms demonstrate that Israel did not experience this covenantal-liturigcal world as empty machinery. The sanctuary is the place of longing, delight, fear, hearing, thanksgiving, and joy. Psalm 24 asks who may ascend the hill of the Lord. Psalm 27 seeks to dwell in the house of the Lord and behold His beauty. Psalm 63 thirsts for God. Psalm 84 faints for the courts of the Lord. Psalm 95 summons the assembly to hear God’s voice in worship. Psalm 100 binds praise, thanksgiving, and belonging together.

The Psalms are therefore essential to the argument because they show the inner meaning of liturgy. Worship is not merely the external side of covenant. It is the communal expression of desire for God. Presence is not only theological proposition; it is the object of longing. The psalmic world confirms that covenant exists for communion and that liturgy is the form through which that communion is celebrated, remembered, and implored.

### Hebrews: Fulfillment Without Abolition

Hebrews 8–10 and 12 are decisive because they interpret the sanctuary and covenant patterns christologically without dissolving their coherence. Christ is the high priest of the true tent, the mediator of a better covenant, and the one whose blood opens the holy place. The old rites are not mocked as irrelevant formalities. They are treated as shadows and anticipations whose fulfillment lies in Christ. The logic of access, sacrifice, priesthood, holiness, and worship remains, but it is intensified and fulfilled.

Hebrews 10:19–22 explicitly joins access, cleansing, and drawing near. Hebrews 12:22–24 then identifies Christian approach with Mount Zion, heavenly Jerusalem, festal assembly, and Jesus the mediator. The new covenant therefore does not abandon the fabric of covenant, presence, and liturgy. It opens it from within and lifts it into its christological and heavenly fullness. The Church still approaches. She still worships. She still gathers. But she does so through the fulfilled mediation of Christ.

### Revelation: Heavenly Worship and Final Dwelling

Revelation 4–5, 7, and 21–22 provide the eschatological horizon. The throne is central. Worship surrounds it. The Lamb stands at the center of the assembly. The saints cry out in praise. The city descends. The dwelling of God is with humanity. These visions are not incidental ornaments at the edge of Christian doctrine. They reveal that liturgy belongs to the goal of history itself. The consummation is not a post-ritual spirituality of formless private experience. It is ordered presence, praise, communion, and participation before the throne of God and of the Lamb.

Revelation therefore confirms that covenant, presence, and liturgy are not temporary themes confined to Israel’s cult. They belong to the deepest structure of God’s communion with His people from first to last.

**Section Summary:** From Sinai and tabernacle to Leviticus, Psalms, Hebrews, and Revelation, Scripture presents covenant, presence, and liturgy as one continuous pattern of holy communion ordered toward Christ and consummation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing ritual, presence, and covenant as separate biblical strands → to recognizing a single scriptural pattern culminating in Christ and the heavenly assembly.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The patristic tradition consistently reads Scripture in ways that reinforce the inseparability of covenant, presence, and liturgy.

Irenaeus is especially useful because he views the divine economy as pedagogical and participatory. God forms humanity through historical covenants, sacrificial patterns, and ecclesial life in order to accustom humanity to communion. This helps explain why liturgy is necessary. It is not merely symbolic repetition but pedagogy in participation. The creature is trained to receive God’s presence through ordered forms.

Origen, with due caution, helps make visible the depth-structure of liturgical texts. He recognizes that the sanctuary, sacrifices, and festivals are not dead externals but signs of a deeper reality fulfilled in Christ and lived in the Church. While his allegorical tendencies must be disciplined by broader ecclesial doctrine, his instinct that liturgical realities bear spiritual and christological weight is sound.

Athanasius and Cyril of Alexandria, in their different ways, reinforce the christological center of worship. Because the incarnate Word is the true temple and mediator, worship is not abolished but fulfilled in Him. The Church’s liturgy becomes participation in the life He opens. This is not mere commemoration. It is entry into divine presence through the one who has united heaven and earth.

Maximus the Confessor’s *Mystagogy* is especially significant for this chapter. Maximus understands the Church’s liturgical life as the recapitulation of the cosmos in Christ, the harmonizing of visible and invisible, earthly and heavenly. For him, liturgy is not decorative but ontological and ecclesial: it expresses the ordered communion of creation with God. This perspective is deeply consonant with the present chapter’s thesis that worship is the covenantal form of communion.

The broader Orthodox tradition also preserves a profound instinct for the unity of presence and worship. The Church is not merely a teaching body or moral society; she is the place where heaven and earth meet, where God dwells with His people, where liturgy becomes the form of ecclesial participation in divine life. The sanctuary, iconography, chant, procession, and sacramental life are not ends in themselves. They are modes by which the covenant people inhabit presence.

The fathers therefore clarify several points. First, liturgy is pedagogical and participatory, not ornamental. Second, divine presence requires and gives form. Third, Christ fulfills and intensifies the sanctuary logic rather than rendering it meaningless. Fourth, the Church’s worship is already a share in heavenly communion.

**Section Summary:** Patristic theology shows that worship is the participatory and pedagogical form of divine-human communion, fulfilled in Christ and lived in the Church as a foretaste of heavenly presence.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing liturgy as secondary religious form → to understanding it as the churchly mode of inhabiting covenantal presence in Christ.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter can now be stated directly: covenant, divine presence, and liturgy form one biblical reality because God’s purpose is to dwell with His people in holy communion, and that dwelling requires an ordered covenantal relation enacted through worship. Covenant is the divine-human relation; presence is the reality and goal of that relation; liturgy is the embodied, communal, and responsive form in which that relation is lived.

This synthesis includes several interlocking claims.

First, covenant exists for communion. Its commands, promises, and sanctions are not ends in themselves. They serve belonging: “I will be your God, and you shall be my people.” Covenant therefore cannot be reduced to law, even though it includes law. It is the ordered relation of divine-human fellowship.

Second, presence exists for communion. God’s dwelling is not merely display of majesty or validation of sacred space. He dwells among His people to meet, speak, sanctify, and receive them. Divine presence is therefore relational and covenantal.

Third, liturgy exists for communion. It is not merely ritual formalization. It is the communal grammar of approaching, hearing, remembering, offering, and rejoicing before God. Liturgy is necessary because human beings are embodied creatures and, in their fallen condition, need divinely given forms of holy approach.

Fourth, worship is the covenantal form of communion. This is the heart of the chapter. Worship is not something added to communion from outside; it is what communion looks like when lived by a gathered, embodied, redeemed people. Praise, sacrifice, procession, blessing, table, feast, assembly, song, and prayer are not detachable “religious activities.” They are covenant life enacted.

Fifth, Christ fulfills rather than abolishes this fabric. In Him covenant is renewed and fulfilled, presence becomes incarnate and ascended, and liturgy becomes participation in heavenly access. Hebrews makes this unmistakable. Christ opens the true sanctuary, and the Church therefore enters more deeply into covenantal worship, not less.

Sixth, this fabric is eschatological. Revelation shows that the final state is not worshipless proximity but perfected liturgical communion before the throne. The end confirms the pattern rather than overturning it.

This doctrinal synthesis directly prepares for Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 as the theological center of Part II. God meets and speaks from the Mercy Seat because the whole sanctuary world exists to make holy communion possible. Meeting and speaking do not appear accidentally in the middle of ritual architecture. They are the reason for it. Covenant and liturgy are necessary because God’s goal is communion—and communion with the Holy One requires form, mediation, remembrance, holiness, and shared response.

**Section Summary:** The chapter resolves the key question by showing that covenant is relation, presence is goal, and liturgy is communal form, all ordered toward holy communion in Christ.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From asking whether liturgy and covenant are optional means → to seeing them as necessary modes of holy communion with the living God.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter carries substantial anthropological implications. It confirms that human beings are not disembodied souls seeking abstract spiritual nearness, nor autonomous individuals capable of inventing their own approach to God. They are embodied, communal, covenantal creatures who require formation. Liturgy matters because the human being is liturgical by structure: he remembers, repeats, offers, inhabits rhythms, and is shaped by patterns of bodily and communal life.

This is why communion requires form. A formless spirituality often appears liberating, but it frequently leaves the self untrained, vulnerable to projection, and captive to hidden liturgies supplied by culture, desire, or ego. The biblical God gives forms because He knows what kind of creatures we are. Sacred time, sacred speech, sacred action, and sacred assembly retrain perception and desire. They are not opposed to inwardness; they tutor it.

The chapter also clarifies why worship is so central to the formation of desire. What a people repeatedly enact before God becomes formative. The sanctuary trains reverence. Sacrifice trains self-offering. Feast trains joy in God. Psalms train longing and praise. Confession trains truthfulness. Blessing trains gratitude. Divine presence, when ritually inhabited, becomes the school of the person and the people.

This has direct bearing on humility. If communion is enacted liturgically, then humility is not merely an inward feeling of lowliness. It is learned through ordered participation in a life where God is the center and the self is decentered. Bowing, waiting, confessing, receiving, offering, kneeling, singing with others, hearing the word, and approaching holy things—these all school the person in creaturely truth. Worship, in this sense, is not only expressive but anthropological. It teaches us what we are.

The same is true of obedience. If covenant and liturgy are one fabric, then obedience is not external ethical supplement to worship. It is one of worship’s fruits. The liturgical life forms the kind of person and people for whom obedience becomes intelligible as covenantal fidelity. One learns to live before God in the sanctuary so as to live before Him in the world.

Spiritually, this chapter also challenges modern anti-form instincts. Many Christians imagine that form inhibits authenticity. Scripture and the Church’s tradition suggest the opposite: right form makes true participation possible. Of course forms can become empty; Scripture repeatedly warns of this. But emptiness is not cured by formlessness. It is cured by restored communion within true form.

**Section Summary:** Because humans are embodied and formable creatures, covenantal and liturgical forms are necessary to train desire, humility, memory, and obedience in the presence of God.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From imagining form as a hindrance to spirituality → to seeing liturgical form as the pedagogy of communion for embodied persons.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church is the new-covenant people in whom this fabric of covenant, presence, and liturgy is fulfilled and intensified in Christ. She is not a community that has outgrown liturgical form because Christ has come. Rather, because Christ has opened the true sanctuary and the Spirit has been given, the Church can now inhabit covenantal presence more deeply than ever. Her worship is not a nostalgic preservation of obsolete symbolism but the ecclesial participation in heavenly communion.

This is why the Church’s liturgical life cannot be dismissed as secondary. Scripture is proclaimed, prayers are offered, thanksgiving is given, the table is received, and the assembly is blessed because the covenant people live by holy response to divine presence. Word and sacrament belong together precisely because the God who dwells with His people also speaks with them. The Church is gathered to hear, praise, offer, receive, and be sent.

At the same time, this chapter warns against ecclesial self-enclosure. Church does not exist for church. Liturgy does not exist for liturgy. The people of God exist for communion with God and, in that communion, for the life of the world. When worship becomes self-referential, its covenantal truth is obscured. The Church must therefore always remember that her forms are ordered toward presence and participation.

The liturgical life of the Church also sustains ecclesial unity. A people is formed not only by beliefs held individually but by acts shared bodily and repeatedly. Gathering before God, confessing one faith, singing one praise, receiving one bread, hearing one word—these are not external markers only. They make a people. Thus liturgy is not only expressive of covenant identity; it is constitutive of it.

This ecclesial form is also eschatological. Hebrews and Revelation show that Christian worship is already oriented toward heavenly Zion. The Church’s liturgy is earthly participation in a heavenly reality. This does not flatten the distinction between present and future, but it does mean that worship is anticipatory communion. The Church becomes a pilgrim people by learning to dwell before God now in forms that anticipate the perfected liturgy of the age to come.

Thus the ecclesial and liturgical implications of the chapter are direct: the Church must understand herself as a covenant people of presence whose worship is the communal form of communion opened in Christ. This is the threshold from which the sanctuary theology of the following chapters becomes fully intelligible.

**Section Summary:** The Church fulfills the covenant-presence-liturgy fabric in Christ, living as a worshiping people whose word-and-sacrament life is already an anticipatory participation in heavenly Zion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing church worship as inherited custom → to understanding it as the present communal form of covenantal presence opened by Christ.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps pastors and teachers explain why worship matters beyond preference or tradition. Worship is the covenantal form of communion, not merely a style of religious expression.

Second, it deepens scriptural reading of Exodus and Leviticus. These books should not be treated as obsolete ritual archives but as pedagogies of holy presence ordered toward Christ.

Third, it strengthens liturgical catechesis. Christians should be taught how sacred time, sacred action, sacred speech, and sacred space form them for communion with God.

Fourth, it clarifies ecclesiology. The Church is not simply an audience, volunteer network, or ideological association; she is a covenant people gathered into divine presence through ordered worship.

Fifth, it offers a critique of modern formless spirituality. Believers should learn to see that form is not the enemy of authenticity but often its servant.

Sixth, it prepares readers for the theological center of Part II. Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 become luminous when one sees that the whole sanctuary order exists so that God may meet and speak with His people.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: If God’s goal is communion, why does He require so much ritual form?

**Reply:** Because communion with the holy God is not mere informal closeness. Human beings are embodied, communal, and fallen. They need divine pedagogy, holy order, and shared forms of approach. Ritual form serves communion; it does not replace it.

### Objection 2: Does this emphasis on liturgy risk reducing worship to external action?

**Reply:** Only if liturgy is severed from presence and covenant. The chapter explicitly rejects empty ritualism. Liturgy is meaningful precisely because it is the embodied response to divine presence in covenant communion.

### Objection 3: Isn’t the new covenant supposed to transcend the old sanctuary system?

**Reply:** It fulfills and intensifies it in Christ. Hebrews does not abolish the logic of access, holiness, priesthood, and worship; it relocates and perfects them in the true sanctuary opened by the Son.

### Objection 4: Does this view make private prayer or informal devotion less important?

**Reply:** No. It places them within a larger covenantal and ecclesial framework. Personal devotion is deepened when it is understood as participation in the liturgical and covenantal life of God’s people.

## Conclusion

This chapter has argued that covenant, divine presence, and liturgy are one biblical fabric. Covenant exists for communion. Presence exists for communion. Liturgy exists for communion. Worship is the covenantal form of communion. These claims do not compete but illuminate one another. God binds Himself to a people so that He may dwell among them. He dwells among them in holy presence. That holy presence is inhabited through ordered, communal, embodied response. Thus the forms of worship given in Scripture are not accidental ceremonial excess. They are part of the way God teaches a sinful people to live before Him.

This prepares directly for the sanctuary theology that follows. Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89 are not isolated proof texts but the concentrated center of an entire world: a world in which covenant, presence, holiness, sacrifice, priesthood, word, and worship converge so that God may meet and speak with His people. The Mercy Seat will soon emerge as one of the theological centers of the book, but it can only bear that weight because the whole prior fabric has been woven.

The chapter also clarifies why liturgy remains indispensable in the Church. Christ does not abolish covenantal worship; He fulfills it. He opens the true sanctuary and grants participation in heavenly communion. The Church therefore lives as a liturgical people, hearing the word, offering praise, receiving mercy, and approaching the throne through the Son in the Spirit. Her worship is not a side activity. It is one of the chief ways the covenant people inhabit divine presence now in anticipation of Zion.

If the purpose of redemption is communion, then form is not the enemy of intimacy but one of its servants. Covenant gives the relation its truth. Presence gives it its goal. Liturgy gives it its embodied communal life. Together they make possible a people who can hear, offer, obey, and rejoice before the living God. And only such a people can move toward the theological center ahead, where the promise stands at the heart of all sacred order: “There I will meet with you, and I will speak with you.”
