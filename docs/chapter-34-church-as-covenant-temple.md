# Chapter 34  
## Church as Covenant-Temple

### Thesis

The Church is a covenant-temple because in Christ and by the Spirit God forms a people in whom communion, presence, worship, and covenant renewal become a shared ecclesial reality. Ecclesiology therefore must flow from Christology → communion → presence rather than from institutional assumptions alone. The Church is not first a religious organization that later acquires sacred functions. She is the people gathered into Christ’s fulfilled sanctuary life, indwelt by the Spirit, renewed by divine speech, and formed in worship as the dwelling place of God. To ask who the Church is is therefore to ask what kind of communion Christ creates.

This chapter argues that worship forms God’s people precisely because the Church is covenant-temple. She is not merely a covenant community in legal or contractual terms, nor merely a temple in metaphorical or aesthetic terms. She is covenant-temple because God has bound Himself to a people through Christ’s blood and dwells among them by the Spirit. What the tabernacle and temple anticipated as meeting-place, dwelling-place, and speech-place is now fulfilled ecclesially. The Church becomes the communal form of restored presence. Worship is therefore not an activity the Church performs from outside herself; it is the act proper to what she is.

This matters because Chapters 34–37 collectively answer the question: how does God form a communion people? Chapter 34 addresses who we are. Chapter 35 will address where we participate. Chapter 36 will ask how the Church understood participation. Chapter 37 will ask how participation is enacted. The present chapter must therefore establish the deepest ecclesiological foundation. If the Church is covenant-temple, then worship is communion, formation, participation, and covenant renewal all at once. If that is not established, later chapters on heavenly liturgy, patristic witness, and the ordo will lack proper grounding.

The chapter therefore answers its key question directly: how does God use worship to form His people? He does so because the people He forms are themselves His covenant-temple. In worship, the Church lives from her identity as the place where Christ gathers His body into communion, where God dwells by the Spirit, where divine speech renews the people, and where covenant life is continually received and enacted. Worship forms the people because worship is what a covenant-temple is for.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 25:8–22; 2 Samuel 7:1–16; Psalm 84; Isaiah 2:1–5; Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:24–28; Ezekiel 37:26–28; John 1:14; John 2:19–21; 1 Corinthians 3:16–17; 1 Corinthians 6:19–20; 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1; Ephesians 2:11–22; 1 Peter 2:4–10; Hebrews 10:19–25; and Revelation 21:1–3, 22. These are read together with the governing passages of the whole project, especially Exodus 25:17–22; Numbers 7:89; Hebrews 12:22–24; and Revelation 21–22.

Exodus establishes the sanctuary as the place where God dwells among His people and speaks from above the Mercy Seat. The Davidic and prophetic texts deepen temple and covenant hope. John identifies Christ as the true tabernacle and temple. Paul and Peter explicitly describe the Church as God’s temple or spiritual house. Ephesians unites Christological peace, access in one Spirit, and corporate temple identity. Hebrews and Revelation place the Church within a trajectory from present access to final dwelling.

Together these texts establish that ecclesiology arises from Christ’s fulfilled temple reality and that the Church exists as a covenant people formed into God’s dwelling.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

After the interior and moral hinge of communion, divine speech, renewed nous, humility, priestly vocation, and embodied obedience, the argument of the book now turns decisively to ecclesial formation. The question is no longer only how the individual person is renewed, but how God forms a people. Chapters 34–37 together answer that question, and Chapter 34 must begin at the deepest level: who are we? The answer proposed here is that the Church is covenant-temple.

This is a decisive starting point because ecclesiology is often handled from the wrong direction. One begins with visible institution, governance, or sociological continuity and then asks how Christ and worship fit within that framework. But the prompt explicitly warns against institutional assumptions as the primary source of ecclesiology. The Church must instead be understood as flowing from Christology → communion → presence. Christ fulfills the temple. Christ opens access. Christ grants participation. Christ pours out the Spirit. Therefore the Church comes into being as the people gathered into that fulfilled presence.

The issue at stake is not simply whether the New Testament occasionally uses temple language for the Church. It is whether such language is central enough to define the Church’s identity. This chapter argues that it is. The Church is the people in whom God dwells, to whom He speaks, whom He sanctifies, and whom He gathers into worship. That identity is both covenantal and temple-shaped. Covenant names belonging, promise, and peoplehood. Temple names dwelling, presence, worship, holiness, and speech. The Church is covenant-temple because both realities meet in Christ and the Spirit.

This matters profoundly for the larger thesis of the book. If worship forms the people of God as pilgrims journeying toward Zion, then the Church cannot be a neutral container for worship. She must be a worship-constituted people whose very being is ordered to communion. Chapter 34 therefore lays the ontological and ecclesiological foundation for the formation chapters that follow.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks who the Church is and argues that she must be understood as covenant-temple, flowing from Christ’s fulfilled presence and not from institutional assumptions alone.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From defining the Church by structure first → to defining her by Christ-fulfilled communion, covenant, and divine dwelling.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate accounts of the Church obscure the significance of covenant-temple theology.

One inadequate account treats the Church primarily as institution. Governance, office, continuity, and social structure then become the first and governing features of ecclesiology. These realities are important, but when they are primary, the Church risks being interpreted from below rather than from Christ and communion. The result is an ecclesiology that may be orderly yet spiritually underdescribed.

A second inadequate account treats the Church primarily as voluntary association. On this reading believers gather around shared convictions or experiences, but the Church is not fundamentally the dwelling place of God or the site of covenant renewal. Such a model fits modern habits of thought more easily than the New Testament witness, but it cannot account adequately for temple language, Eucharistic communion, or worship as formative participation.

A third inadequate account spiritualizes temple language into vague divine nearness. The Church is said to be “God’s temple” in the sense that God blesses her or is generally among her, but the strong biblical themes of holiness, access, speech, worship, and covenant are muted. This weakens the theological force of the metaphor.

A fourth inadequate account isolates covenant from temple. The Church then becomes a covenant people in legal or promissory terms, but the dwelling, liturgical, and participatory dimensions are underdeveloped. Yet in Scripture covenant and presence belong together. God covenants in order to dwell with and among His people.

A fifth inadequate account isolates temple from covenant. The Church becomes sacred space or liturgical site, but her peoplehood, relational belonging, and covenantal speech are neglected. Temple without covenant risks becoming aestheticized sanctity rather than living communion.

These inadequate readings all fail because they do not sufficiently unite Christology, communion, presence, worship, and ecclesial identity. The chapter therefore seeks to recover the Church as the covenant-temple created by Christ’s fulfilled sanctuary work.

**Section Summary:** Institutionalist, associational, weakly spiritualized, covenant-only, and temple-only accounts all fail because they do not adequately describe the Church as the people in whom covenant and presence meet in Christ.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From partial or flattened ecclesiologies → to a Christological vision of the Church as covenant-temple.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### “Let Them Make Me a Sanctuary”

Exodus 25:8–22 provides the original covenant-temple logic. God commands Israel to make Him a sanctuary so that He may dwell among them. The Mercy Seat then becomes the place from which He meets and speaks. The sanctuary is therefore not first a symbol of human aspiration but the form of divine condescension and covenant presence. God dwells with a people and addresses them there.

This is the fundamental pattern later chapters and texts intensify. The Church as covenant-temple must be read as the fulfilled form of this same purpose: God dwelling among and speaking to His people.

### Temple and Covenant Hope in the Prophets

The prophetic texts deepen this logic rather than abandoning it. Jeremiah 31 promises a new covenant written on the heart. Ezekiel 36–37 promises cleansing, a new spirit, and the divine dwelling among the people forever: “My dwelling place shall be with them.” These are not two unrelated hopes—covenant and presence. They are one reality in two dimensions. God’s covenant renewal aims at restored dwelling.

This is crucial for the chapter’s thesis. The Church is covenant-temple because the prophetic hope of heart renewal and divine indwelling converges in her.

### Christ as the Fulfilled Temple

John 1 and 2 make the decisive Christological move. The Word tabernacles among us, and Jesus identifies His body as the true temple. Temple theology is therefore fulfilled personally before it is applied ecclesially. This is an indispensable safeguard. The Church is not temple in her own independent right. She is temple because she is joined to Christ the true Temple and indwelt by His Spirit.

This directly supports the prompt’s requirement that ecclesiology flow from Christology → communion → presence. The Church’s temple identity is derivative of Christ’s fulfilled temple identity.

### The Church as God’s Temple

Paul then makes the ecclesial claim explicit. In 1 Corinthians 3, the community is God’s temple and the Spirit dwells in it. In 1 Corinthians 6, the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit. In 2 Corinthians 6–7, the Church is the temple of the living God, and holiness therefore matters. These texts show both the corporate and personal dimensions of temple identity. The Church is not metaphorically “like” a temple only. She is the dwelling place of God by the Spirit.

This has immediate implications for worship and formation. Temple identity means holiness, speech, bodily consecration, and communal seriousness. The Church’s life is ordered by the presence she bears.

### Ephesians 2: Access and Dwelling

Ephesians 2 is perhaps the most important ecclesiological synthesis for this chapter. Through Christ, Jew and Gentile both have access in one Spirit to the Father. The dividing wall is broken down. The people are built together into a holy temple in the Lord, a dwelling place for God by the Spirit. This passage unites Christology, reconciliation, access, ecclesiology, and temple identity in one argument.

This is exactly what the chapter must show. The Church is covenant-temple because Christ grants access, the Spirit indwells, and a reconciled people is built together as God’s dwelling.

### 1 Peter 2 and the Spiritual House

First Peter 2 adds a closely related image: believers come to Christ the living stone and are themselves built up as a spiritual house and holy priesthood. Temple and priesthood are joined. Worship, holiness, offering, and peoplehood belong together. The Church’s temple identity is thus active, priestly, and communal.

### Hebrews and Revelation: Present Access, Final Dwelling

Hebrews 10 and 12 show that the Church already has access to the heavenly sanctuary and has come to heavenly Zion. Revelation 21:3 and 22 complete the movement: the dwelling of God is with humanity, and the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple. These texts together show that covenant-temple identity is already real and not yet consummated. The Church lives now as the people in whom God dwells and toward the day of unveiled dwelling.

**Section Summary:** Scripture presents a unified pattern in which God’s covenantal purpose is to dwell among His people, fulfilled first in Christ the true Temple and then ecclesially in the Spirit-indwelt Church.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From reading temple and covenant as separate biblical motifs → to seeing them converge in Christ and define the Church’s identity.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers often read the Church as temple in a way that strongly reinforces this chapter’s aims.

Irenaeus is a major witness because he sees the whole economy of salvation as God’s movement to dwell with humanity and draw humanity into communion. The Church, nourished by Eucharist and indwelt by the Spirit, becomes the communal site of this restored relation. His thought helps keep covenant and temple joined.

Cyril of Alexandria is especially helpful because of his deep temple Christology. For Cyril, Christ is the true Temple, and only through union with Him can the Church become the place of divine indwelling. This strongly supports the chapter’s insistence that ecclesiology must flow from Christology.

John Chrysostom’s preaching on Ephesians and Corinthians is also valuable. He handles temple language with moral and ecclesial seriousness, showing that if the Church is God’s temple then holiness, unity, and liturgical life are of the utmost importance.

Maximus the Confessor extends the insight by placing ecclesial worship within the wider participation of creation in God. The Church is temple not merely as an institutional space but as the liturgical body in which humanity’s vocation is restored and creation is offered back to God.

The Orthodox tradition more broadly powerfully sustains this understanding. The Church is repeatedly experienced and confessed as the place of divine indwelling, heavenly worship, covenantal life, and sanctifying presence. Temple language remains living, not antiquarian.

Thus the patristic witness confirms that covenant-temple is not an accidental image but a profound way of naming the Church’s identity in Christ.

**Section Summary:** Patristic theology consistently supports the Church’s identity as temple through union with Christ, indwelling by the Spirit, and life in covenantal worship.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating temple ecclesiology as a marginal metaphor → to recognizing it as a major patristic and ecclesial way of naming the Church.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that the Church is covenant-temple because in Christ and by the Spirit God has formed a reconciled people in whom He dwells, to whom He speaks, and whom He sanctifies in worship.

Several affirmations define this synthesis.

First, ecclesiology flows from Christology. Christ is the true Temple and the mediator of covenant access.

Second, communion is the mode of ecclesial existence. The Church is not merely organized around Christ; she participates in Him.

Third, presence is constitutive. The Church is the dwelling place of God by the Spirit.

Fourth, covenant and temple belong together. God binds Himself to a people in order to dwell among them and form them through worship.

Fifth, worship is covenant renewal and communal formation. The Church becomes what she is by living before God in praise, word, sacrament, and obedience.

Sixth, this identity is already real and not yet complete, reaching its consummation in Revelation 21–22.

This doctrinal synthesis answers the key question directly. How does God use worship to form His people? He does so because the people He is forming are His covenant-temple, and worship is the act proper to their indwelt, covenantal identity.

**Section Summary:** The Church is the covenant-temple created by Christ’s fulfilled presence and indwelt by the Spirit, and worship is the form by which this people is continually constituted and renewed.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From thinking of worship as something the Church merely does → to seeing it as the formative act of what the Church most deeply is.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter has major anthropological implications because it shows that redeemed humanity is not restored merely as a collection of individuals but as a dwelling-place people. Human beings are created for communion, and that communion is irreducibly communal. The person becomes most truly himself not in isolation but within the people among whom God dwells.

This means the renewed nous, transformed desire, and humility all take ecclesial form. Perception is not healed only for private peace, but for life in the temple-body. Desire is not reordered only toward private holiness, but toward shared worship and shared belonging. Humility is not merely interior modesty, but fitting life in a body whose presence is received, not self-made.

Spiritually, the chapter teaches that one cannot understand the Christian life without understanding the Church. The temple is not a backdrop to personal spirituality. It is the communal form of God’s indwelling purpose. Worship therefore forms persons by placing them within the people God is making.

This also means holiness must be communal and bodily. If the Church is covenant-temple, then speech, conduct, hospitality, reconciliation, shared prayer, and reverence all become temple-questions. The whole life of the body matters.

**Section Summary:** Redeemed humanity is formed as a communal dwelling-place of God, so spiritual transformation necessarily takes ecclesial, bodily, and shared form.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From privatizing communion and formation → to seeing human restoration as life within God’s covenant-temple people.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church’s liturgical life is the enactment of her identity as covenant-temple. In worship she hears the word of the covenant, offers praise, receives the gifts of Christ, participates in the Spirit, and is renewed as the dwelling place of God. Worship is therefore communion, formation, participation, and covenant renewal at once.

This means that ecclesial identity is not maintained by institutional continuity alone. It is maintained and deepened by the repeated inhabitation of worship. The Church becomes what she is by gathering before God, by hearing, by offering, by receiving, and by being built up in love. The temple is a living event, not only a doctrinal status.

The covenant-temple identity also grounds mission. The Church bears witness to the world not only by proclamation but by being a people among whom divine presence, reconciliation, holiness, and praise are visible. She is a foretaste of the dwelling of God with humanity.

Eschatologically, the Church’s worship points beyond itself. She is already God’s temple-body and yet still awaits the day when temple becomes immediate and all-encompassing in the new Jerusalem. Thus worship forms a pilgrim people ordered toward final dwelling.

**Section Summary:** The Church’s worship enacts her covenant-temple identity by gathering her into word, praise, communion, and renewal as God’s dwelling-place people.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing liturgy as one church activity among others → to understanding it as the enactment of the Church’s deepest identity.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps correct ecclesiologies driven mainly by institutional or sociological assumptions.

Second, it gives worship a much deeper ecclesiological meaning as the act proper to the Church’s identity.

Third, it unites covenant theology and temple theology into one ecclesial vision.

Fourth, it strengthens communal holiness by showing that the Church’s shared life is temple-life.

Fifth, it supports mission by presenting the Church as visible dwelling-place of God’s reconciling presence.

Sixth, it prepares directly for the following chapters on heavenly liturgy, patristic witness, and the ordo by grounding all of them in the Church’s identity as covenant-temple.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Does this make the Church too sacred and risk triumphalism?

**Reply:** Not if the already-and-not-yet is maintained. The Church is truly God’s dwelling-place now and still a pilgrim, sinful, repentant people awaiting consummation.

### Objection 2: Is temple language for the Church only metaphorical?

**Reply:** It is analogical, but not empty metaphor. The New Testament uses it to name a real indwelling, sanctifying, communal reality established by Christ and the Spirit.

### Objection 3: Why insist that ecclesiology flow from Christology?

**Reply:** Because Christ is the true Temple and covenant mediator. Without that grounding, ecclesiology becomes self-referential and loses its theological center.

### Objection 4: Does covenant-temple language diminish mission or ethics?

**Reply:** No. It intensifies both. The people among whom God dwells are formed in worship and then sent as a visible witness of reconciliation, holiness, and praise.

## Conclusion

The Church is a covenant-temple because in Christ and by the Spirit God has formed a people in whom He dwells, to whom He speaks, and whom He renews in worship. This is the chapter’s central claim and the necessary starting point for the formation chapters that follow. The Church cannot be understood adequately from institutional assumptions alone. She must be understood from Christ, communion, and presence.

This chapter has shown that covenant and temple are not separate biblical themes but deeply united. God covenants in order to dwell. He dwells in order to commune and speak. Christ fulfills temple and covenant together. The Spirit indwells. The people are built together as God’s house. Therefore worship is not an external duty added to church life. It is the act by which the Church most truly lives as what she is.

This matters profoundly for the book’s larger argument. Worship forms the people of God because the people of God are a worship-constituted people. Hearing, renewed nous, transformed desire, humility, obedience, and participation all take ecclesial shape because the Church is covenant-temple. Formation is not accidental to worship. It is inherent in the identity of the people gathered before God.

Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question can be stated plainly: God uses worship to form His people because His people are His covenant-temple. In worship He dwells, speaks, renews, and binds them to Himself. And in so doing He shapes them into a communion community journeying toward the day when the dwelling of God with humanity will be fully unveiled.
