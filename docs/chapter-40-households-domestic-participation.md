# Chapter 40  
## Households: Domestic Participation

### Thesis

Households are one of the primary places where communion becomes domestic participation. The home is not a secondary or spiritually indifferent zone lying outside the sphere of worship’s formation. Nor is it merely a private refuge from public discipleship. It is one of the most intense arenas in which communion → hearing → renewed perception → obedience → love must become durable and embodied. Domestic participation means that the life formed in worship enters the table, the bed, the conversation, the schedule, the conflicts, the care of children, the honoring of parents, the hospitality shown to guests, and the common burdens of household life.

This chapter argues that a life shaped by communion must take domestic form. The home becomes one of the chief sites where liturgical identity is tested, deepened, and made visible. If Chapter 38 described prayer as interior participation and Chapter 39 described daily life as ordinary participation, Chapter 40 now turns to the household as the domestic architecture of communion. The believer does not only pray privately and act faithfully in public. He also dwells with others—or in the memory, wounds, and hopes of household relation—and there communion must become patience, fidelity, forgiveness, order, tenderness, discipline, service, and hospitality.

This matters deeply for the book’s controlling thesis because worship forms the people of God not in abstraction from ordinary relational life but precisely for it. The renewed nous must learn to see the household not merely as a functional arrangement or emotional refuge, but as a place where love is practiced under the discipline of communion. The transformed desire that yields humility must show itself in shared life. The self-offering that yields obedience must be visible where people are most familiar, most vulnerable, and most difficult to love.

The chapter therefore answers its governing question directly: what does a life shaped by communion look like? It looks domestic. It looks like homes shaped by prayer, forgiveness, truthful speech, shared burdens, hospitality, chastity, honor, discipline, patience, and care. Domestic participation does not make the household a replacement for the Church. Rather, it makes the household one of the principal extensions of the Church’s formation into everyday life.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Deuteronomy 6:4–9; Psalm 128; Proverbs 31:10–31; Luke 10:38–42; Acts 10:1–8, 24–48; Acts 16:11–15, 25–34; Romans 12:9–21; Ephesians 5:21–6:4; Colossians 3:12–21; 1 Timothy 3:1–13; Titus 2:1–8; Hebrews 13:1–4; and 1 Peter 4:7–10. These are read together with the larger logic of communion and worship already established in the book.

Deuteronomy 6 shows the household as a site of hearing and covenant transmission. Psalm 128 links fear of the Lord, labor, table, spouse, and children. Acts shows households as receiving the gospel and becoming sites of ecclesial life. Ephesians, Colossians, Titus, and 1 Peter show that Christ-shaped obedience extends into marriage, parenting, hospitality, and relational conduct. Hebrews 13 places love, hospitality, marriage, and contentment within the practical continuation of worship.

Together these texts establish that the household is a privileged arena of ordinary and relational participation in the life of God.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

The “liturgy after the liturgy” section is moving from interior life to ordinary life to more specific social forms. Chapter 38 argued that prayer is interior participation. Chapter 39 argued that ordinary daily life is a field of participation shaped by communion. The next question is more specific and relational: what does this look like in the household? If communion is to become embodied fully, it must be visible where people live most closely together.

This question matters because the household is often one of the hardest places for Christian formation to remain visible. Public worship may be reverent. Private prayer may be sincere. Public ethics may be respectable. Yet in the home impatience, harshness, neglect, resentment, disorder, domination, indifference, and speech failures can quietly corrode the shape of communion. The household is therefore one of the most searching tests of whether worship truly forms a people.

The chapter must also avoid simplistic assumptions. Not every reader inhabits the same kind of household. Some are married, some single, some widowed, some in multigenerational homes, some in loneliness, some in fractured families, some in situations marked by grief or estrangement. “Household” must therefore be treated theologically and pastorally, not idealistically. It refers to the domestic sphere in which lives are shared, ordered, sustained, remembered, and often wounded.

Within the logic of the book, the key issue is whether communion → hearing → renewed perception → obedience → love continues here. The chapter argues that it does and must. Domestic life becomes participatory when the home is understood as a place where the Church’s liturgical formation is carried into proximity, repetition, and mutual burden-bearing.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks how communion becomes visible in the household and argues that domestic life is one of the chief places where worship’s formation is tested and embodied.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating the home as spiritually secondary or private-only → to seeing it as a major arena of communion-shaped participation and love.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate approaches distort a theological account of households.

One inadequate approach sentimentalizes the household. The home is then imagined as naturally harmonious, automatically nurturing, or spiritually wholesome simply because it is domestic. This ignores the distortions of sin and the deep need for redemption within ordinary relational life.

A second inadequate approach moralizes the household through external role-performance alone. One then focuses on family duties, structures, and expectations without sufficient attention to communion, hearing, renewed perception, humility, and love. This can produce order without tenderness, correctness without grace, and outward compliance without transformed desire.

A third inadequate approach privatizes the household entirely. The home becomes a self-enclosed private sphere detached from the Church’s worship and the kingdom’s claims. But the New Testament does not permit such insulation. Households hear the word, receive the gospel, practice hospitality, and become sites of ecclesial life and witness.

A fourth inadequate approach collapses the household into ideological conflict. Modern discourse often frames domestic relations chiefly through power, autonomy, or self-expression. While questions of justice and misuse of power matter profoundly, a theological account must go deeper. The Christian household is not first a negotiation of competing sovereignties, but a shared field of self-offering and mutual service under Christ.

A fifth inadequate approach treats domestic life as too ordinary to carry significant theological weight. Yet Scripture repeatedly places table, bed, parent-child relation, hospitality, and household discipline within the realm of covenantal faithfulness.

These distortions fail because they do not adequately show the home as a place where communion is either embodied or contradicted. The chapter therefore seeks to recover households as domestic participation shaped by worship and grace.

**Section Summary:** Sentimentalized, moralistic, privatized, ideological, and trivialized views of household life all fail because they do not adequately show the domestic sphere as a principal arena of communion-shaped formation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing households as either automatic or merely functional → to understanding them as places where communion must become visible in relation, order, and love.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### Hearing in the House

Deuteronomy 6 provides the fundamental biblical pattern for domestic participation. The words of God are to be spoken in the house, on the way, lying down, and rising up. The household is thus a primary site of hearing. Covenant does not remain at the sanctuary only. It enters domestic rhythms.

This is decisive for the chapter because it shows that the home is not merely where moral applications happen. It is where hearing itself continues. The household is to be a place under the word.

### The Table as Blessing and Formation

Psalm 128 presents the fear of the Lord, fruitful labor, spouse, children, and table fellowship as bound together in covenant blessing. The household table is not simply functional eating-space. It is a site of gratitude, shared life, and blessing received from God. This is highly significant for the larger theme of the liturgy after the liturgy. The table after the Table can still bear eucharistic memory and communal formation.

### Households in Acts

Acts repeatedly presents households as receiving the gospel and becoming sites of ecclesial participation. Cornelius’s household receives the Spirit. Lydia’s household is baptized. The Philippian jailer’s household hears the word and rejoices. These texts show that the gospel moves through domestic structures and that the home can become a place of hospitality, hearing, and transformation.

This supports the chapter’s claim that domestic life is not outside the Church’s formative action. The Church often comes to expression in the household.

### Domestic Love and Mutual Submission

Ephesians 5:21–6:4 and Colossians 3:12–21 are crucial and must be handled carefully. These texts are sometimes flattened into role enforcement, but within the argument of the book they must be read through communion and renewed perception. Mutual submission under Christ, love, gentleness, non-provocation, and nurture are central. The chapter should not treat these texts as mere social codes. They are practical expressions of Christ-shaped relation within the home.

The phrase “as to the Lord,” the command to love as Christ loved the Church, and the requirement not to embitter or provoke all point toward domestic participation under the logic of self-offering and love.

### Hospitality, Purity, and Fidelity

Hebrews 13 and 1 Peter 4 extend domestic participation outward. Brotherly love continues. Hospitality is offered without grumbling. Marriage is honored. The home thus becomes not a sealed private world but a porous site of faithfulness, chastity, mercy, and reception of others. Domestic participation includes both inward care and outward generosity.

### Titus and the Formation of Households

Titus 2 is especially important because it shows household life as catechetical and communal. Older and younger, male and female, public and domestic life all come under the formative discipline of grace. The household is a school of self-control, dignity, love, and good works. Again, this is not mere moralism. Grace trains.

### Domestic Participation as Continuation of Worship

Taken together, these texts show that the home becomes an arena where hearing continues, prayer can continue, love is practiced, burdens are shared, speech is disciplined, sexuality is sanctified, hospitality is extended, and patience is learned. The domestic sphere is not outside worship’s reach. It is one of the chief places where worship’s fruits are either confirmed or denied.

**Section Summary:** Scripture presents the household as a place of hearing, table-fellowship, mutual service, hospitality, chastity, and grace-trained love, showing that communion extends deeply into domestic life.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating household life as practical background only → to seeing it as a theologically charged arena where communion is carried into relation, table, and habit.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers frequently press Christian worship and virtue into domestic life, though often in ways requiring careful contextual reading.

John Chrysostom is especially significant here. He repeatedly exhorts households to become “little churches,” marked by prayer, scriptural hearing, mutual honor, care for the poor, and disciplined speech. His homilies on marriage and family life resist both romanticism and harsh moralism by insisting that domestic life must be ordered by Christ.

Basil the Great contributes helpfully through his broad sense that Christian virtue, simplicity, hospitality, and care for others extend into all of life, including domestic rhythms and the use of goods. He helps resist any view of the home as a purely private possession.

Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa, while not offering a full household theology in modern form, both witness to the sanctification of relational and familial bonds by grace and by the pursuit of holiness. Their reflections help show that household life is not extrinsic to communion with God.

Maximus the Confessor supports the chapter more indirectly but importantly. His account of healed desire and rightly ordered relation helps explain why domestic life matters so much. The household is where desire, self-love, patience, and service are repeatedly tested and reformed.

The Orthodox tradition also contributes by its sustained practices of icon corner prayer, table blessings, fasting cycles, feast-day observance, hospitality, and domestic prayer. These show that the home can indeed become a site where liturgical formation is extended and embodied.

Thus the patristic witness confirms that domestic life is one of the principal places where the Church’s worship either ripens into practice or fails to do so.

**Section Summary:** Patristic theology and practice support the household as an extension of ecclesial formation, where prayer, hospitality, mutual care, and scriptural life become domestic participation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From assuming patristic theology says little about home life → to recognizing its strong witness to domestically embodied communion.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that households are a primary sphere of domestic participation in which worship’s communion extends into sustained relational, moral, and embodied life.

Several affirmations shape this synthesis.

First, the household is not spiritually neutral. It is a field where communion is practiced or contradicted.

Second, domestic participation is grounded in hearing. The home must remain under divine speech.

Third, domestic participation is christological. Love, sacrifice, gentleness, fidelity, and service are shaped by Christ.

Fourth, domestic participation is noetic. Renewed perception changes how family, labor, sexuality, hospitality, and ordinary burdens are seen.

Fifth, domestic participation is ecclesially sourced. The Church forms households by worship, word, sacrament, and shared life.

Sixth, domestic participation is ordered toward love. The home is a school of patient, truthful, sacrificial charity.

This doctrinal synthesis answers the chapter’s key question. What does a life shaped by communion look like? It looks like a home in which hearing becomes relation, obedience becomes service, and love becomes habitual.

**Section Summary:** Households become domestic participation when worship’s formation enters hearing, love, fidelity, hospitality, and patient relational life under Christ.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing the home as merely natural structure → to understanding it as one of the central sites where communion becomes embodied love.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter has major anthropological significance because the household reveals how deeply human beings are relational. No one is formed in abstraction. The self is shaped in patterns of address, affection, authority, care, memory, and daily contact. Therefore if communion is to form persons, it must form households.

This means the renewed nous is tested not only in thought but in relational perception. How one sees spouse, child, parent, sibling, guest, or co-resident matters profoundly. The home becomes a proving ground for whether one truly sees others as gift, neighbor, burden to be borne, and person to be honored rather than as instrument, obstacle, or extension of the self.

Desire is also intensely present here. Domestic life reveals the disorder of appetite, anger, possessiveness, withdrawal, and self-love—but also becomes the school in which desire is retrained toward patience, fidelity, chastity, generosity, and tenderness.

Humility is unavoidable in the home. One cannot live closely with others without repeated opportunities for repentance, service, and relinquishment of self-importance. Domestic participation is therefore one of the most searching forms of the liturgy after the liturgy.

Spiritually, the chapter teaches that holiness must learn to dwell. The house is not simply where one returns from spiritual life. It is where spiritual life becomes ordinary, repetitive, tested, and real.

**Section Summary:** The household forms persons at the deepest relational level, making it a primary school of renewed perception, transformed desire, humility, and practiced love.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing formation as mostly individual → to recognizing the household as a central workshop of personhood under communion.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

Households do not replace the Church, but they extend the Church’s formation into domestic life. The home becomes an outworking of liturgical identity when it receives the word, practices prayer, shares meals with thanksgiving, offers hospitality, and learns patterns of forgiveness and truthfulness shaped by worship.

Ecclesially, this means the Church must care about domestic life not merely for moral regulation, but because homes are one of the chief places where her communion is enacted after the assembly disperses. A Church that worships beautifully but forms loveless homes has not yet carried communion deeply enough.

Liturgically, domestic participation can take concrete form in family prayer, blessing of meals, scriptural reading, observance of feasts and fasts, hospitality to the stranger, care for children and elderly members, and reverent treatment of time and speech. These do not turn the home into the sanctuary in a formal sense, but they allow the sanctuary’s formation to shape the home.

Eschatologically, domestic participation also bears witness that the household is not ultimate. Every Christian home is provisional and pilgrim, ordered toward the greater household of God in the kingdom. This saves the chapter from domestic idealism. The Christian home is not paradise restored, but a place of grace on the way to Zion.

**Section Summary:** The household becomes a domestic extension of ecclesial formation when the Church’s worship shapes its prayer, table, speech, hospitality, and shared life.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating the home as outside ecclesial concern → to seeing it as one of the Church’s most important spheres of formation after worship.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps extend liturgical theology into the home without collapsing church and household.

Second, it gives a strong basis for domestic prayer, shared Scripture, table fellowship, and hospitality.

Third, it resists both sentimental and moralistic views of family life.

Fourth, it helps pastors teach that worship should shape speech, patience, marriage, parenting, and care in the home.

Fifth, it provides a theological rationale for understanding household life as a primary school of humility and love.

Sixth, it prepares the way for Chapter 41 by moving from domestic participation to broader vocation and social mercy.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Does this chapter idealize households and ignore broken or painful homes?

**Reply:** It must not. The chapter explicitly understands households pastorally and theologically, including fractured, lonely, and difficult domestic realities. Participation here often includes repentance, endurance, grief, and patient rebuilding.

### Objection 2: Could this domestic focus become exclusionary for single persons?

**Reply:** No. “Household” is broader than the nuclear family and includes shared domestic life, extended kinship, hospitality, memory, and the domestic conditions under which all Christians live.

### Objection 3: Is there a risk of moralizing marriage and parenting texts?

**Reply:** Yes, and the chapter explicitly resists that by grounding them in communion, hearing, renewed perception, and Christ-shaped self-offering.

### Objection 4: Does this make the home a substitute for the Church?

**Reply:** No. The chapter insists the opposite: the home is formed by the Church and extends her communion into domestic life.

## Conclusion

Households are one of the primary places where communion becomes domestic participation. This is the central claim of the chapter. The home is not simply where one lives between liturgies. It is where liturgy’s fruits are tested, strengthened, and embodied in relationship, table, speech, chastity, hospitality, care, and forgiveness.

This means the Christian household must not be treated sentimentally or moralistically. It is neither naturally holy nor transformable by external roles alone. It must come under the same sequence that has governed the whole book: communion, hearing, renewed perception, humility, self-offering, obedience, and love. Only then can domestic life become participatory rather than merely functional.

The chapter also serves the larger architecture of the book by showing that worship truly forms a people when it reaches the home. Prayer continues the liturgy inwardly; domestic participation extends it relationally. The household becomes one of the clearest places where ordinary and hidden faithfulness manifests the durability of communion.

Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question may be stated plainly: a life shaped by communion looks like a home shaped by communion. It looks like hearing under the word, shared prayer, patient love, chastity, hospitality, repentance, mutual care, and ordinary faithfulness. Such households are not perfect sanctuaries, but they are real places where the liturgy after the liturgy takes flesh on the way to Zion.
