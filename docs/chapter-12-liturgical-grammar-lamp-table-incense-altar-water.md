# Chapter 12  
## Liturgical Grammar: Lamp, Table, Incense, Altar, Water

### Thesis

The sanctuary’s liturgical structures are not decorative details or ritual surplus. They are pedagogy. God teaches human beings to approach Him by giving them a grammar of worship through lamp, table, incense, altar, and water. These elements are not merely objects within sacred space; they are formative signs and practices through which God trains His people in perception, participation, holiness, and communion. The sanctuary therefore does not simply host worship. It instructs worship. Its forms shape the people who inhabit them.

This chapter argues that liturgical forms are one of God’s principal means of training a redeemed people for communion. The lamp teaches illumination and ordered sight. The table teaches fellowship, provision, thanksgiving, and covenant nearness. Incense teaches prayer, mediation, and ascent. The altar teaches sacrifice, consecration, and self-offering. Water teaches cleansing, transition, preparation, and holy approach. Taken together, these elements form a liturgical grammar—a structured language of approach through which God educates His people in the realities of His presence. The sanctuary is thus a pedagogy of communion.

This claim is crucial for the argument of Part II. Chapter 7 established that covenant, presence, and liturgy form one fabric. Chapter 8 showed that redemption is for communion. Chapter 9 demonstrated that God’s people are formed by hearing His voice. Chapter 10 explained why the Ten Words belong beneath the Mercy Seat. Chapter 11 identified meeting and speech as the theological center of sanctuary logic. The present chapter now asks: if God’s goal is that He may meet and speak with His people, how does He teach them to approach Him? The answer is that He gives them forms. Liturgical forms are not barriers placed between humanity and God, but pedagogical means by which a holy people learns how to draw near.

This chapter must therefore show that sanctuary elements are to be interpreted as pedagogy, approach, participation, and formation. They are pedagogical because they teach. They concern approach because they structure movement toward God. They are participatory because they involve embodied, communal, repeated acts of reception and response. And they are formative because they shape perception and desire. The people become what they repeatedly enact before God. The sanctuary’s liturgical grammar therefore belongs directly to the book’s broader sequence: communion → hearing → renewed nous → transformed desire → humility → self-offering → obedience → worship. God’s liturgical forms teach His people how to approach Him not merely externally, but inwardly, through the renewal of perception and the sanctification of desire.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 25:23–40; Exodus 27:1–8; Exodus 30:1–10, 17–21, 34–38; Leviticus 1–7; Leviticus 8–9; Leviticus 16; Numbers 8:1–4; Psalm 26; Psalm 43; Psalm 51; Psalm 141; John 6:32–35; John 8:12; John 13:1–17; Hebrews 9:1–14; Hebrews 10:19–22; and Revelation 1:12–20; 5:8; 8:3–4. These texts are read together with the broader sanctuary logic already established in Exodus 25:22 and Numbers 7:89.

Exodus provides the physical and cultic descriptions of lampstand, table, altar, incense altar, and laver. Leviticus explains the sacrificial and priestly use of these realities, especially in relation to approach and atonement. The Psalms reveal their spiritual and participatory significance, linking washing with innocence, incense with prayer, and sanctuary presence with longing. John’s Gospel and other New Testament texts show Christ fulfilling and recapitulating these realities: He is the light of the world, the bread from heaven, the one who washes His disciples, the self-offering sacrifice, and the mediator of access. Hebrews interprets the sanctuary and its furnishings in relation to Christ’s heavenly priesthood. Revelation gathers several of these symbols into the worship of the heavenly assembly.

These texts show that the sanctuary’s forms are not arbitrary or obsolete. They are revelatory structures that shape a people for communion and find their fulfillment in Christ and His Church.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

The previous chapter identified one of the sanctuary’s deepest truths: God’s purpose is to meet with His people and speak with them. That claim invites an immediate practical and theological question: how does a holy God teach a sinful, embodied people to approach Him? If the ultimate goal of redemption is communion, and if divine meeting and speech stand at the center of that communion, then the forms of approach cannot be accidental. They must themselves belong to the logic of redemption. The sanctuary’s liturgical structures are therefore not optional embellishments. They are part of the answer to the problem of approach.

This matters because modern readers often have difficulty seeing form as pedagogical. Ritual objects and prescribed actions can seem static, formal, or merely symbolic. The lamp may appear to be just furniture; the table merely cultic provision; incense an ancient sensory enhancement; the altar a sacrificial mechanism; water a hygienic necessity. But the sanctuary world does not permit such flattening. Every element is ordered. Every element teaches. Every element embodies a dimension of approach, remembrance, or transformation. The people of God are not simply told that He is holy and that they must approach rightly. They are trained into that reality through repeated liturgical grammar.

The chapter therefore stands in a necessary place within the argument of Part II. If Chapter 11 named the purpose of sanctuary—meeting and speech—Chapter 12 explains the practical and formational means by which a people is prepared for such communion. The issue is not only doctrinal coherence but anthropological realism. Human beings are embodied creatures. They learn by repetition, symbol, movement, memory, gesture, and communal enactment. Therefore if God will form them for communion, He will not form them only by propositions or by inward impressions. He will teach through liturgical life.

The key question named in the prompt is thus exactly right: how does God teach human beings to approach Him? This chapter argues that He does so through liturgical grammar. The sanctuary’s forms do not compete with divine presence or divine speech. They serve them. They shape a people capable of perceiving, desiring, responding, and dwelling before God in covenant faithfulness.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks how God teaches a redeemed people to approach Him and argues that the sanctuary’s liturgical forms function as a divinely given pedagogy of communion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating sanctuary elements as static ritual details → to seeing them as formative structures that train a people for holy approach.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate readings obscure the theological significance of the sanctuary’s liturgical grammar.

One inadequate reading treats sanctuary forms as merely ceremonial externals. In this view lamp, table, incense, altar, and water are primarily old covenant ritual apparatus with little formative or theological significance beyond historical interest. Such a reading often arises from impatience with cultic detail or from theological frameworks that sharply oppose outward form to inward spirituality. But it fails to explain why Scripture devotes such care to these elements, why they recur in poetic and prophetic imagination, and why the New Testament repeatedly retrieves them christologically.

A second inadequate reading reduces these elements to abstract symbols without pedagogy. Here the furnishings “represent” certain truths, but are not understood to train the people who use them. This is an improvement over bare externalism, but it remains too thin. The lamp does not merely symbolize light; it teaches the people to inhabit a world ordered by divine illumination. The altar does not merely represent sacrifice; it habituates a people to the logic of offering. The forms are participatory and formative, not only referential.

A third inadequate reading treats liturgical structures as spiritually unnecessary once divine presence is affirmed. Presence is then understood in such direct or inward terms that form becomes almost suspect. But biblical theology consistently resists this. God Himself gives forms of approach because He knows that presence without pedagogy would remain either presumptuous or unintelligible to fallen creatures. Holiness requires training.

A fourth inadequate reading absolutizes the forms themselves, as though correct liturgical action were the goal. This is the opposite distortion. It mistakes pedagogy for destination. Yet Chapter 11 has already shown that the destination is meeting and speech, that is, communion. The sanctuary’s forms matter because they lead, shape, and order; they do not replace the presence of God or become ends in themselves.

A fifth inadequate reading spiritualizes these elements in a christological way that bypasses their old covenant integrity. One may quickly say “Christ is the light,” “Christ is the bread,” “Christ is the sacrifice,” and stop there. These are true claims. Yet when made too quickly they can obscure the pedagogical logic by which Israel was formed to understand such realities in the first place. The sanctuary’s forms matter not only as foreshadowings but as actual means by which God schooled His people for communion.

These distortions all fail because they do not hold together form, formation, and fulfillment. The chapter must therefore recover the sanctuary elements as pedagogical forms of approach that shape perception and desire and find their fullness in Christ.

**Section Summary:** Externalist, merely symbolic, anti-form, over-formal, and overly hasty christological readings all fail to recognize the sanctuary as a formative pedagogy of approach.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing liturgical structures as either dispensable or self-justifying → to understanding them as formative means ordered toward communion with God.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### The Lamp: Illumination and Ordered Sight

The lampstand in Exodus 25:31–40 and Numbers 8:1–4 is not merely a source of light within a dark tent. It teaches a people that life before God requires illumination. To approach the holy God, one must see rightly. The sanctuary is not a place of self-generated vision but a space lit by divine provision. The lamp therefore participates in the theology of divine speech and renewed perception already developed in earlier chapters. God meets and speaks; therefore the people must be a seeing and hearing people. Light and word belong together.

The lamp also teaches constancy. Its tending is ordered, repeated, priestly, and communal. Illumination is not occasional inspiration but sustained formation. The sanctuary’s light suggests that God teaches His people to dwell in a world where reality is disclosed by His own holiness. The later identification of Christ as the light of the world, and Revelation’s vision of lampstands and heavenly radiance, do not cancel this old covenant meaning but fulfill it.

### The Table: Fellowship, Provision, and Presence

The table of the bread of the Presence in Exodus 25:23–30 places food within the sanctuary itself. This is already theologically significant. Bread in God’s presence indicates that communion is not merely visual or auditory, but also covenantal and table-oriented. The presence of God is hospitable, sustaining, and relational. The people live before Him not only as petitioners but as recipients of His provision.

The table teaches remembrance and fellowship. It locates sustenance within covenant relation. Human beings are trained to see life, bread, and daily provision not as autonomous possessions but as gifts received before God. The table thus shapes desire away from self-sufficiency and toward thanksgiving. In the broader biblical arc, this logic reaches Christological fulfillment in John 6 and ecclesial enactment in the Eucharistic life of the Church. Yet already in the tabernacle the table teaches that approach to God includes receiving sustenance in His presence.

### Incense: Prayer, Ascent, and Fragrant Approach

The incense altar of Exodus 30:1–10 and the incense imagery of Psalm 141 and Revelation 5:8; 8:3–4 reveal another dimension of liturgical grammar. Incense teaches that approach to God includes the offering of prayer and that prayer has ascentive, mediatory, and communal dimensions. The smoke rises; the people’s petitions are figured as fragrant offering. The act is repetitive and ordered, which means prayer itself is schooled by liturgical form.

This matters because it shows that participation in divine presence is not silent occupancy. It is responsive and intercessory. The sanctuary’s incense trains desire toward Godward ascent. It renders the inner life visible in liturgical action. Prayer becomes embodied, rhythmic, and communal rather than merely spontaneous interiority. In this way incense teaches the people how to approach God with hearts lifted and desires disciplined.

### The Altar: Sacrifice and Self-Offering

The altar in Exodus 27 and Leviticus 1–7 occupies a central place in the pedagogy of approach. It teaches that communion with God is costly because sin is real, holiness is burning, and self-offering is necessary. Yet the altar does not merely reveal danger. It also reveals divine provision. God gives the sacrificial system so that approach may be possible. The altar thus teaches both judgment and mercy, both consecration and access.

Theologically, the altar shapes a people in the logic of offering. The worshiper learns that life before God is not possession but presentation. This points forward naturally to Romans 12:1–2, where believers present their bodies as living sacrifice. The old covenant altar thus serves not only as site of slaughter but as school of self-offering. It trains the people to understand communion as involving consecrated life.

### Water: Cleansing and Transitional Holiness

The laver and the broader washing rites of Exodus 30:17–21 and Levitical texts teach yet another aspect of liturgical grammar: approach requires cleansing. Water marks transition from common movement to holy service. It teaches that God may not be approached casually. But it also teaches that cleansing is given. God provides means by which the people may pass from impurity toward service.

Water therefore belongs to the pedagogy of humility and preparation. The worshiper learns creaturely limitation, contamination, and dependence on divine provision. At the same time, water shapes desire for purity and disposes the self toward reverent nearness. In the wider canonical pattern, this anticipates baptismal and Christological meanings, but already at the sanctuary level it forms the people to understand approach as purification for communion.

### The Grammar as a Whole

Taken individually, lamp, table, incense, altar, and water each teach a specific aspect of holy approach. Taken together, they become a grammar. Illumination, sustenance, prayer, sacrifice, and cleansing are not disconnected liturgical themes. They form a sequence of life before God. The people are taught to see rightly, receive thankfully, pray responsively, offer themselves, and approach cleanly. This is why the sanctuary can rightly be called a pedagogy of approach and participation.

This grammar also shapes perception and desire. The people learn to desire light rather than darkness, provision as gift rather than possession, prayer rather than self-enclosure, offering rather than grasping, cleansing rather than presumption. The sanctuary trains not only conduct but longing. It forms the nous by habituating the people to the world as ordered by divine presence.

**Section Summary:** Lamp, table, incense, altar, and water each teach a distinct dimension of holy approach, and together they form a liturgical grammar that shapes perception, desire, and participation before God.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing sanctuary furnishings as isolated cultic objects → to understanding them as a coherent pedagogy of approach, formation, and communion.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers read the sanctuary’s forms with profound seriousness, refusing to treat them as either empty externals or disposable preliminaries.

Origen, for all the caution that must accompany his exegesis, is especially valuable in seeing that sanctuary structures possess pedagogical and spiritual depth. He does not read lamp, altar, and incense as inert pieces of old religious furniture but as realities pointing to the formation of the inner person and to Christ. His instinct is right even where his interpretive boldness sometimes needs restraint: liturgical forms shape souls.

Irenaeus contributes the broader framework of divine pedagogy. For him, God forms humanity gradually through material, historical, and ecclesial means. This helps explain why sanctuary forms are necessary. God does not merely dictate truths; He trains persons and peoples by embodied practices appropriate to their creaturely condition. The liturgical world of Israel is one mode of this formation.

Gregory of Nyssa’s treatment of Moses and the sanctuary world also suggests an ascentive pedagogy. The soul is led through stages, forms, and mediated participation into deeper divine life. This supports the chapter’s claim that the sanctuary teaches approach. The people are not instantly placed in the highest reality; they are schooled toward it.

Maximus the Confessor is especially important because he perceives liturgy as revelatory participation in the ordered relation between God, cosmos, and Church. In the *Mystagogy*, the Church’s liturgical life is not merely commemorative but ontological and formative. Such insight helps explain how sanctuary forms can shape perception and desire. They train the worshiper to inhabit a world ordered toward God.

The Orthodox liturgical tradition also offers a living confirmation of this chapter’s thesis. Light, table, incense, offering, washing, procession, chant, and blessing are all understood as pedagogical realities, not aesthetic extras. The faithful are trained by repeated embodied participation in the holiness, hospitality, and beauty of God. This is not a later addition to biblical religion, but a continuation in fulfilled form of the sanctuary grammar already visible in Exodus and Leviticus.

Thus the patristic witness confirms that liturgical forms are formative means of participation. They address the whole person—body, memory, desire, perception, and communal identity—and are properly understood within the movement toward communion with God.

**Section Summary:** Patristic and Orthodox tradition affirm that liturgical forms are not empty signs but formative, participatory means by which God educates His people in holy approach.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From reading sanctuary forms as archaic cultic details → to seeing them as enduringly revelatory pedagogies fulfilled in Christ and the Church.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that God teaches human beings to approach Him by giving them liturgical forms that function as pedagogy, approach, participation, and formation. Lamp, table, incense, altar, and water are not merely symbolic adjuncts to worship. They are divinely given structures through which redeemed humanity learns how to dwell before the Holy One.

This synthesis rests on several claims.

First, liturgical form belongs to redemption’s logic. God does not redeem a people and then leave them to invent approach. Because His goal is communion, He gives the means by which communion may be inhabited rightly. Liturgy is therefore not a secondary human response to divine initiative but part of divine initiative itself.

Second, the sanctuary elements are pedagogical. They teach vision, gratitude, prayer, self-offering, and cleansing. Their truth lies partly in their repeated enactment. The people become what they practice before God.

Third, these forms are modes of participation. They do not only point toward realities from afar; they draw the people into a shaped experience of those realities. The worshiper does not merely learn about sacrifice; he participates in sacrificial approach. He does not only think about cleansing; he undergoes liturgical preparation. He does not simply admire light; he stands within God-given illumination.

Fourth, liturgical forms are formative of perception and desire. This is especially important for the book’s broader thesis. Divine speech renews the nous, but it does so within a whole covenantal environment in which seeing, hearing, offering, washing, and feasting belong together. The sanctuary’s liturgical grammar trains the soul to desire rightly.

Fifth, these forms are fulfilled in Christ, not discarded. He is light, bread, sacrifice, purifier, and mediator of prayer. Yet His fulfillment does not abolish liturgical formation. Rather, it deepens and re-centers it in the life of the Church. The old grammar becomes fulfilled grammar.

Sixth, this doctrinal synthesis answers the prompt’s key question. How does God teach human beings to approach Him? He teaches through form. He teaches through repeated holy structures that shape a people for meeting, hearing, and communion.

**Section Summary:** God teaches approach through liturgical forms that are pedagogical, participatory, and formative, shaping a people whose perception and desire are ordered toward communion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From thinking of form as incidental to worship → to understanding liturgical structure as one of God’s appointed means of teaching communion.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter presses deeply into theological anthropology. Human beings are creatures who learn through habit, symbol, embodiment, repetition, and communal participation. If one ignores this, one will misread both the sanctuary and the human person. The liturgical grammar of Israel presupposes that persons do not become holy by information alone. They are formed by the worlds they inhabit and the gestures they repeat.

This has immediate relevance to the renewal of the nous. The nous is not renewed only through abstract reflection, but through a whole life ordered toward God. Light teaches what to see. Bread teaches what to receive. Incense teaches what to desire and where to direct longing. Altar teaches what it means to offer. Water teaches readiness and cleansing. Through these forms, divine truth descends into perception and desire. The sanctuary is therefore not merely an ethical system but a school of the transformed mind.

This also clarifies why liturgy shapes desire. Human beings always live liturgically in some sense. They are shaped by rituals of consumption, achievement, entertainment, politics, and memory even when they do not name them as such. The sanctuary offers a counter-formation. It teaches a different world and a different love. It trains the person to delight in God’s light, God’s provision, God’s holiness, God’s mercy, and God’s presence. Desire becomes covenantally educated.

Humility also emerges here. Liturgical forms decenter the self. One does not define the terms of approach. One receives them. One is taught how to stand, wash, offer, wait, eat, pray, and see. This receiving posture is not humiliation in a negative sense, but truthful creatureliness. The liturgical life teaches dependence, and dependence is one of the roots of humility.

Self-offering likewise becomes intelligible within this grammar. The altar prepares for Romans 12. Prayer, washing, bread, and light all prepare for a life given over to God. Worship becomes the embodied schooling of obedience. The spiritual life is therefore not merely inward intensity; it is the patient formation of a person through holy forms.

This chapter also offers a corrective to modern anti-form instincts. Many imagine that spontaneity is the mark of authenticity. But the sanctuary suggests that authenticity before God often requires training. The person does not naturally know how to approach the Holy One. He must be taught. Liturgy is one of God’s merciful ways of teaching.

**Section Summary:** The sanctuary’s liturgical grammar addresses the whole person, renewing perception, reshaping desire, teaching dependence, and forming the self for humble and worshipful communion with God.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From assuming people are transformed mainly by ideas or intentions → to seeing transformation as embodied, repeated, and liturgically schooled.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church receives the sanctuary’s pedagogical logic in fulfilled form through Christ. She does not replicate the old covenant furnishings unchanged, but neither does she outgrow the need for liturgical grammar. Word, table, light, incense, confession, offering, water, blessing, and sacramental participation remain central because the people of God are still being taught how to approach Him.

This means ecclesial worship must be understood as formation, not merely expression. The Church does not gather simply to “do worship” in a generic sense. She gathers to be taught again how to see, hear, pray, receive, confess, offer, and dwell. The liturgy shapes the Church’s perception of God, neighbor, body, time, and world. It becomes the communal school of communion.

The relation to Christ is decisive. Christ is the light that illumines the sanctuary’s meaning. He is the bread from heaven to which the table points. He is the fragrant mediator through whom prayer rises. He is the once-for-all sacrifice to which the altar bears witness. He is the cleanser who washes His people and grants baptismal incorporation. Thus the Church’s liturgical life is christological through and through. Yet precisely because it is christological, it remains liturgical. Fulfillment does not mean formlessness.

Ecclesially, this also means that worship shapes identity. The Church becomes herself by inhabiting this grammar together. Shared light, shared word, shared table, shared prayer, shared confession, shared cleansing, shared offering—these make a people. The Church is not formed only by doctrinal agreement or organizational loyalty, but by common approach to God.

The eschatological dimension is equally important. The sanctuary’s liturgical grammar is not merely retrospective or didactic. It anticipates Zion. Revelation’s heavenly worship is full of light, offering, table fellowship, incense, and adoration before the throne. The Church’s liturgical life is therefore a training in heavenly participation. She is schooled now in the forms that anticipate her consummation.

Thus the chapter concludes that the Church must understand worship as pedagogy of communion. The sanctuary’s grammar remains alive in fulfilled form because God is still teaching His people how to approach Him until hearing finally becomes seeing.

**Section Summary:** The Church inherits the sanctuary’s pedagogical logic in Christ, living a liturgical life that forms her identity, shapes her approach, and trains her for heavenly communion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing worship as expressive gathering only → to understanding it as the ecclesial school of communion and anticipation of Zion.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps pastors and teachers explain why liturgy matters as formation rather than mere tradition or preference.

Second, it deepens Christian understanding of Old Testament worship by showing that sanctuary elements teach approach, not just ritual compliance.

Third, it provides a theological basis for liturgical catechesis. Christians should be taught how light, table, prayer, offering, cleansing, and repetition shape them.

Fourth, it offers a powerful critique of secular formations of desire. If worship forms, then the Church must be intentional about how her practices train the heart.

Fifth, it strengthens the link between doctrine and worship. The truths of communion, speech, holiness, and redemption are not only taught propositionally but enacted liturgically.

Sixth, it prepares for the next chapter by clarifying that holy approach is learned through forms that make access, presence, and communion habitable.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Isn’t this reading too symbolic? Perhaps these items are just practical sanctuary necessities.

**Reply:** Their practical function is real, but Scripture’s sustained attention, placement, ritual use, and later biblical reuse show that they are more than practical necessities. They are pedagogically and theologically charged.

### Objection 2: Does emphasizing liturgical formation threaten the primacy of grace?

**Reply:** No. The chapter insists that the forms themselves are gifts of grace. God teaches His people how to approach Him because He desires communion. Formation is grace’s mode, not its competitor.

### Objection 3: Might this overstate the effect of ritual on the inner life?

**Reply:** Not if ritual is understood covenantally and scripturally. Human beings are formed by embodied and repeated practices everywhere else in life; Scripture simply reveals that God redeems and orders this reality toward holy communion.

### Objection 4: If Christ fulfills all these elements, why preserve liturgical structure in the Church?

**Reply:** Because fulfillment does not entail formlessness. Christ fulfills the realities to which these elements point, and the Church continues to live from those fulfilled realities through her own liturgical and sacramental grammar.

## Conclusion

The sanctuary teaches because God desires communion. Lamp, table, incense, altar, and water are not ritual leftovers from an obsolete cultus. They are liturgical grammar. Through them God trains a redeemed people in illumination, provision, prayer, self-offering, cleansing, and holy approach. The sanctuary thus becomes a pedagogy of approach and participation.

This means that liturgical forms are not accidental to the biblical story. They belong to the same movement as redemption, covenant, speech, and presence. God redeems for communion. He meets and speaks with His people. He then teaches them how to approach Him through ordered forms that shape perception and desire. The sanctuary’s furnishings become the means by which divine truth descends into habit, memory, longing, and communal identity.

The chapter therefore answers the prompt’s key question directly: God teaches human beings to approach Him by giving them liturgical structures that form them for His presence. The lesson is not only ancient. It remains vital for the Church. In Christ the forms are fulfilled, but the need for liturgical pedagogy remains. The Church still needs to be taught how to see, receive, pray, offer, wash, and dwell.

This prepares the way for the continuing argument of Part II. Holy approach is not natural to fallen humanity. It must be given, taught, enacted, and fulfilled. The sanctuary’s grammar reveals the wisdom and mercy of God in providing exactly such teaching. And because it does, it shows once again that God’s goal is not mere religious performance but communion—communion so serious, so holy, and so gracious that God Himself trains His people in how to draw near. In this way liturgy becomes not obstacle but mercy: the pedagogy by which hearing people are formed into worshiping people on the way to Zion.
