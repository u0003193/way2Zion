# Chapter 36  
## Patristic Witness to Participation

### Thesis

The fathers are witnesses to Scripture’s theology of participation, not authorities independent of Scripture, and their witness confirms that the Church understood worship as communion, formation, participation, and covenant renewal. This chapter argues that the patristic tradition does not introduce a foreign liturgical mysticism into the biblical text; rather, it receives, interprets, and extends the scriptural logic already traced in Exodus 25, Hebrews 12, Revelation, John, Paul, and the broader sanctuary-temple tradition. The fathers matter here because they show how the Church learned to inhabit what Scripture teaches: worship forms God’s people because worship is participation in the life of God through Christ in the Spirit.

This chapter stands at a crucial point in the ecclesial formation sequence of Chapters 34–37. Chapter 34 argued that the Church is covenant-temple: who we are. Chapter 35 argued that heavenly liturgy is where we participate. Chapter 36 now asks how the Church understood participation. The answer is not by setting the fathers over against Scripture, nor by treating them as speculative authorities detached from biblical foundations, but by receiving them as ecclesial readers of Scripture whose witness can help show how worship shapes a communion people.

This matters because participation can be misunderstood either as a later patristic embellishment or as a purely modern liturgical idea imposed retrospectively upon the Bible. The chapter argues the opposite. The fathers bear witness to a deep continuity: the God who met and spoke above the Mercy Seat, who fulfilled that pattern in Christ, who brought the Church to heavenly Zion, and who gathered the saints into the worship of the Lamb was understood by the Church to be forming His people through participation. The patristic witness therefore confirms, clarifies, and enriches the book’s central thesis.

The chapter therefore answers its key question directly: how does God use worship to form His people? He does so by bringing them into participatory communion with Himself in Christ, and the fathers testify that this participation is ecclesial, sacramental, noetic, transformative, and ordered toward deification rightly understood. The Church is formed because she does not merely remember salvation; she participates in it.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 25:17–22; Psalm 84; Isaiah 6:1–8; John 1:14; John 6:51–58; John 15:1–11; Romans 12:1–2; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 1 Corinthians 12:12–27; 2 Corinthians 3:17–18; Ephesians 2:18–22; Hebrews 10:19–25; Hebrews 12:22–24; and Revelation 4–5; 21–22. These texts are not merely background but the scriptural norm against which the patristic witness is received and interpreted.

Exodus 25 and Isaiah 6 establish presence, worship, and speech in the sphere of holy communion. John presents the Incarnation, Eucharistic life, and abiding as modes of participation. Paul and Hebrews explain ecclesial communion, access, transformation, and heavenly Zion. Revelation reveals the throne-room liturgy and consummated communion. These texts together form the canonical basis to which the fathers bear witness.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

The argument of Chapters 34–37 is ecclesial and formative: how does God form a communion people? Chapter 34 answered that the Church is covenant-temple. Chapter 35 showed that she participates in heavenly liturgy. Chapter 36 must now address a crucial historical and theological question: how did the Church understand this participation? The answer matters not only for historical interest but because the fathers can either illuminate or confuse the argument depending on how they are used.

The prompt wisely requires that the fathers be used as witnesses to Scripture rather than as authorities independent of Scripture. This is a crucial methodological safeguard. The chapter cannot merely collect patristic quotations about liturgy, Eucharist, deification, or temple and treat them as self-justifying proof. Nor can it ignore the fathers on the assumption that only direct biblical exegesis matters. Rather, the chapter must show how the fathers received and interpreted the biblical pattern already established: communion, presence, speech, participation, and formation.

This issue matters because the theological claims of this book—especially the claims about heavenly participation, divine speech, renewed nous, and embodied worship—may sound to some readers like later Orthodox or patristic developments rather than biblical theology. Chapter 36 can help answer that concern, but only if it is disciplined. The fathers should not be made to carry arguments Scripture itself has not already initiated. Instead, they should show how the early Church understood Scripture’s own logic.

This chapter therefore occupies a mediating role. It stands between the chapters that established participation biblically and the chapter that will describe how participation is enacted in the ordo. It must show that the Church’s liturgical self-understanding as participatory is neither modern invention nor historical accident. It is a deep ecclesial reception of Scripture.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks how the Church understood participation and argues that the fathers should be received as scriptural witnesses who help illuminate how worship forms God’s people.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating the fathers as either irrelevant or independent authorities → to receiving them as ecclesial readers of Scripture who testify to worship as participation.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate approaches to patristic theology and participation must be avoided.

One inadequate approach treats the fathers as authorities independent of Scripture. Their statements are then cited as though they could establish doctrine without or against biblical foundations. This is methodologically unsound and theologically dangerous. It also risks alienating readers who rightly expect the central claims of the book to be rooted in Scripture.

A second inadequate approach treats the fathers as merely historical curiosities. Their liturgical or sacramental language is acknowledged but regarded as secondary ornament or later development with little bearing on biblical theology. This fails to see that the fathers were living interpreters of the scriptural world and that their witness can illuminate how the Church came to inhabit participation.

A third inadequate approach selectively mines patristic material for isolated slogans such as “deification” or “the Eucharist” while detaching those terms from the fathers’ broader scriptural and ecclesial framework. This often generates distortion. Participation must be read in relation to Christology, ecclesiology, liturgy, holiness, and the whole economy of salvation.

A fourth inadequate approach uses the fathers polemically, either to justify one tradition against another or to force a later confessional synthesis back into every early text. Such use obscures rather than clarifies the formative and witness-bearing role the chapter needs.

A fifth inadequate approach flattens the diversity of the fathers into a single undifferentiated voice. While there is deep continuity, different fathers emphasize different aspects—Incarnation, Eucharist, Scripture, noetic transformation, liturgy, deification, pastoral formation. The chapter should use this diversity constructively.

These distortions fail because they do not allow the fathers to function as scriptural witnesses to the Church’s understanding of participation. The chapter therefore seeks a disciplined and ecclesial use of patristic sources.

**Section Summary:** Scripturally detached, dismissive, slogan-driven, polemical, and flattening uses of the fathers all fail because they do not let patristic witness illuminate biblical participation responsibly.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From misusing or underusing the fathers → to hearing them as faithful witnesses to the Church’s scriptural understanding of worship and participation.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim as Received by the Fathers

### Irenaeus: Participation as Recapitulated Communion

Irenaeus is one of the earliest and most important witnesses because he reads salvation as recapitulation in Christ. Humanity is gathered into the incarnate Son so that communion with God may be restored. This is not a departure from Scripture but an extension of John’s Incarnation theology, Paul’s in-Christ language, and the sanctuary logic of divine indwelling. The Eucharistic dimensions of Irenaeus also reinforce that participation is bodily, ecclesial, and cosmic.

Irenaeus therefore bears witness to a Church that understood worship not merely as remembering salvation but as living within it. The Church is formed by participating in Christ’s restored humanity and offering thanksgiving to God.

### Athanasius: Incarnation and Deifying Participation

Athanasius receives scriptural temple-Christology and participation by insisting that the Word became human so that humans might share the life of God by grace. This well-known formula must be read in its scriptural setting: John 1, union with Christ, the destruction of death, and renewal of the image. Athanasius does not replace biblical categories with philosophical speculation. He intensifies the scriptural claim that salvation is communion and participation.

In relation to worship, Athanasius’s significance lies in showing that liturgical and sacramental life are not external appendices to salvation. They are modes of inhabiting what Christ has accomplished.

### Cyril of Alexandria: Christological Participation

Cyril is especially important because he ties participation tightly to Christology. The Church participates because Christ is the true Temple, the incarnate Word, and the one in whom divine and human realities meet without confusion. Cyril’s sacramental and ecclesial thought, especially in relation to John, bears strong witness to the fact that participation is christological before it is mystical. Worship forms the people because in worship they participate in Christ Himself.

This is especially valuable for the chapter’s methodological concern. Cyril shows how the fathers interpret Scripture not by moving away from Christ but by pressing more deeply into Him.

### John Chrysostom: Liturgy and the Formed People

John Chrysostom repeatedly depicts the Church’s liturgy as heavenly, moral, and transformative. Worship is not static observance. It is standing with angels, receiving the word, approaching the mysteries, and being formed for mercy, generosity, almsgiving, and holiness. This makes him a particularly strong witness for the chapter’s larger question: how does worship form God’s people? Chrysostom’s answer is thoroughly scriptural and pastoral—by participation in the realities proclaimed and offered in the assembly.

### Maximus the Confessor: Noetic and Cosmic Participation

Maximus is indispensable because he integrates many of the book’s major themes: renewed nous, transformed desire, liturgical participation, deification, and ecclesial formation. His *Mystagogy* especially shows how the Church’s worship is an entry into the divine economy that restores human perception and reorders creation toward God. Maximus is not inventing participation out of speculative metaphysics; he is drawing together Hebrews, Revelation, Pauline union language, and the sacramental life of the Church.

He is especially important for this project because he bears witness to the claim that worship forms the person inwardly and communally. The renewed nous is not a private achievement. It is shaped in the liturgical participation of the Church.

### Patristic Reception of Heavenly Liturgy

Across the fathers, heavenly worship is not treated as merely future or merely figurative. The Church’s liturgy is repeatedly described as participation in the heavenly worship of angels and saints before God. This reception clearly rests on Hebrews 12 and Revelation. The fathers therefore confirm the biblical argument of Chapters 26, 27, and 35: worship is communion and participation, not observation alone.

### Patristic Witness as Scriptural Continuity

The cumulative point is not that the fathers agree in every accent, but that they witness to a continuous reception of Scripture in which worship is participatory, sacramental, transformative, ecclesial, and eschatological. This witness strengthens rather than supplants the book’s biblical argument.

**Section Summary:** The fathers receive Scripture as teaching participatory communion in Christ, and their witness confirms that the Church understood worship as sacramental, ecclesial, noetic, and transformative participation.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From suspecting participation is a later addition → to seeing it as a deeply patristic reception of Scripture’s own logic.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers offer several specific insights that help this chapter’s larger argument.

First, they consistently refuse to separate worship from formation. Liturgy is not ornamental. It transforms the Church because it brings the Church into the reality of Christ’s life.

Second, they read participation christologically. The Church participates not in a vague sacred atmosphere but in Christ Himself—the incarnate, crucified, risen, and ascended Son.

Third, they read participation ecclesially. No father of significance imagines worship as merely individualistic inward piety. The Church is built, nourished, corrected, illumined, and sanctified as a people.

Fourth, they read participation sacramentally. Word and mystery belong together. The Church hears and receives, is addressed and fed, is taught and united.

Fifth, they read participation eschatologically. The liturgy is already real participation in heavenly things and a foretaste of consummation. This is especially relevant to the book’s movement toward Revelation 21–22.

These insights do not replace Scripture’s claims. They show how the Church learned to dwell inside them. That is the great value of the patristic witness for this chapter.

**Section Summary:** The fathers clarify that Christian participation is christological, ecclesial, sacramental, formative, and eschatological, thereby showing how the Church inhabits Scripture’s worship theology.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From using the fathers as quotation sources only → to receiving their deeper theological insights into how worship actually forms the Church.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that the patristic witness confirms the book’s central ecclesial claim: God forms His people through worship because worship is participation in Christ’s life before the Father in the Spirit, and the fathers received this as a scriptural reality rather than an independent speculative doctrine.

Several affirmations shape this synthesis.

First, the fathers must be used as witnesses to Scripture, not authorities above it.

Second, their witness is consistent and significant in presenting worship as participatory rather than merely symbolic or didactic.

Third, this participation is christological and ecclesial. It occurs in Christ and in His body.

Fourth, participation is transformative. It forms perception, desire, humility, holiness, and communal life.

Fifth, participation is sacramental and liturgical. The Church is formed by the concrete practices of worship.

Sixth, participation is eschatological. The Church enters now what will one day be seen unveiled.

This doctrinal synthesis directly answers the chapter’s key question. How does God use worship to form His people? He does so by drawing them into participatory communion in Christ, and the fathers bear unified witness that this has been the Church’s understanding from early on.

**Section Summary:** The fathers confirm, as scriptural witnesses, that worship forms the Church by making her participate in Christ’s life, heavenly worship, and sacramental communion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating patristic theology as optional supplement → to seeing it as a powerful ecclesial witness to Scripture’s participatory vision of worship.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter has important anthropological implications because the patristic witness shows that participation is not an external ecclesial theory but a claim about what human beings are for. The person is formed for communion, and worship is one of the primary modes by which that communion becomes real, perceptible, and transformative.

The fathers especially help reinforce the book’s noetic and formative claims. The human person is not changed only by command or information, but by entering into divine life in Christ. Perception is healed, desire is re-ordered, humility is deepened, and the person becomes more truly human. Patristic theology thus strongly supports the book’s movement from divine speech to renewed nous to humility.

Spiritually, the chapter shows that worship forms because it re-situates the person within truth. The Church does not gather simply to think about God. She gathers to participate in the life God gives. The fathers understood that such participation shapes the soul at its deepest level.

This also protects the Christian life from moralism and voluntarism. If formation is participatory, then the believer’s growth depends not first on self-exertion but on inhabiting worship faithfully, hearing rightly, and receiving what God gives in Christ.

**Section Summary:** The patristic witness supports a deeply participatory anthropology in which worship heals perception, reorders desire, and forms the human person for communion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From imagining Christian formation as primarily moral or intellectual effort → to seeing it as participation in the life of God that reshapes the person from within.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church’s liturgical life is the context in which the patristic witness becomes concrete. The fathers did not formulate participation in abstraction from worship. They preached, catechized, baptized, celebrated Eucharist, sang psalms, kept feasts and fasts, taught Scripture, and led people into the mysteries. Their theology arose from and returned to liturgical life.

This means the chapter’s argument is not merely historical. It is ecclesial. The Church today is still formed as the fathers describe when she hears Scripture, participates in sacramental worship, joins heavenly praise, and is built together as Christ’s body. The patristic witness therefore serves not only as retrospective testimony but as living guidance for understanding what worship does.

It also helps prepare the way for Chapter 37 on the ordo. If the fathers understood participation as enacted through liturgical order, then the Church’s worship patterns are not accidental. They are one of the chief means by which formation becomes durable.

**Section Summary:** The fathers’ theology of participation arose from lived liturgical practice, and their witness therefore prepares the Church to understand how worship’s concrete order forms a communion people.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing patristic theology as merely historical data → to recognizing it as liturgically embodied witness relevant to the Church’s ongoing formation.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps readers receive patristic theology as a witness to Scripture rather than as alien authority.

Second, it strengthens the biblical case for worship as participation by showing the Church’s consistent historical reception of that claim.

Third, it deepens ecclesial confidence that liturgy forms because it truly mediates participation in Christ.

Fourth, it supports catechesis by connecting biblical theology, patristic reflection, and liturgical practice.

Fifth, it reinforces the book’s claims about noetic renewal, transformed desire, and humility by showing that the fathers linked these to worship.

Sixth, it prepares the way for the ordo chapter by showing that the concrete structure of worship matters because participation is enacted, not merely believed.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Does this chapter give the fathers too much authority?

**Reply:** No. It explicitly uses them as witnesses to Scripture, not as sources independent of it.

### Objection 2: Could “participation” in the fathers mean something too different from the biblical categories used in the book?

**Reply:** Differences of vocabulary and emphasis certainly exist, but the chapter argues that the central logic—communion in Christ, ecclesial worship, sacramental life, transformation, heavenly participation—shows deep continuity.

### Objection 3: Why not rely on Scripture alone and avoid patristic material?

**Reply:** Because the chapter’s aim includes showing how the Church understood participation. The fathers help demonstrate that the book’s claims are not novel inventions but ecclesially received readings of Scripture.

### Objection 4: Is patristic witness mainly an Orthodox concern here?

**Reply:** The chapter makes strong use of Orthodox-friendly themes, but its primary claim is broader: the fathers across the early Church bear witness to worship as participation and formation.

## Conclusion

The fathers are witnesses to Scripture’s theology of participation, and their witness confirms that the Church understood worship as communion, formation, participation, and covenant renewal. This is the central claim of the chapter. The patristic tradition does not impose a foreign liturgical mysticism upon the Bible. It inhabits the biblical vision: God dwells, speaks, gathers, feeds, sanctifies, and transforms His people in Christ.

This means the Church’s understanding of worship as participatory is neither late invention nor optional devotional style. It is a deeply received ecclesial conviction grounded in Scripture and borne forward by the fathers. Irenaeus, Athanasius, Cyril, Chrysostom, Maximus, and others all bear witness that salvation is not only declared but participated in, not only remembered but inhabited.

The chapter therefore strengthens the larger argument of the book in a crucial way. Worship forms the people because worship is participation. Divine speech renews the nous because the Church receives it within communion. Humility and obedience arise because the whole person is being shaped in worship by the life of God in Christ. The fathers did not merely teach doctrines about these realities. They lived and interpreted them in the Church.

Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question can be stated plainly: God uses worship to form His people by bringing them into participatory communion in Christ, and the fathers testify—on scriptural grounds—that this is how the Church has understood worship from early on. Their witness therefore stands not alongside Scripture as a rival authority, but behind the Church as a chorus of readers who help us hear Scripture’s own liturgical and formative voice more clearly.
