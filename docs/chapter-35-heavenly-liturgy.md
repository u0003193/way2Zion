# Chapter 35  
## Heavenly Liturgy

### Thesis

Heavenly liturgy is the sphere in which the Church participates, and it is one of the primary means by which God forms His people as a communion community. Worship is not merely earthly ceremony, pious symbolism, or devotional expression arising from below. It is communion, formation, participation, and covenant renewal because in Christ the Church is brought into the heavenly worship already taking place before the throne of God and the Lamb. The Church is shaped by where she truly stands. She becomes a communion people because she is gathered into heaven’s liturgy.

This chapter argues that heavenly worship must be explicitly linked to Hebrews 12, Revelation, and Exodus 25. Hebrews 12 declares that the Church has come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, festal assembly, church of the firstborn, God the judge of all, Jesus the mediator, and the sprinkled blood. Revelation unveils the throne-room liturgy of heaven centered on the Lamb. Exodus 25 reveals the older covenant pattern of meeting and speech from above the Mercy Seat. Together these texts show that Christian worship is participation in the heavenly counterpart and fulfillment of the sanctuary pattern. God uses worship to form His people because worship brings them into the presence, speech, order, and praise of heaven itself.

This chapter follows directly from Chapter 34’s claim that the Church is covenant-temple. If the Church is the dwelling-place people of God, the question now becomes: where does that people participate? The answer is heavenly liturgy. The Church is not merely a temple on earth with self-contained sacred acts. She is a people whose liturgical life is opened upward into Christ’s heavenly priesthood and throne-room worship. To speak of the Church as communion community therefore requires that her worship be understood as participation in heaven, not just remembrance of heaven.

The chapter therefore answers its key question directly: how does God use worship to form His people? He does so by gathering them into heavenly liturgy, where communion, presence, divine speech, praise, covenant renewal, and eschatological hope converge. Worship forms the people because heavenly participation forms the people. The Church becomes what she worships in Christ before the throne.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 25:17–22; Isaiah 6:1–8; Daniel 7:9–14; Hebrews 8:1–6; Hebrews 10:19–25; Hebrews 12:18–29; Revelation 4–5; Revelation 7:9–17; Revelation 8:1–5; Revelation 14:1–5; and Revelation 21–22. These are read together with Luke 24:30–35; Acts 2:42–47; and 1 Corinthians 10:16–17.

Exodus 25 establishes the Mercy Seat as the place of meeting and speech. Isaiah 6 and Daniel 7 disclose heavenly throne-room worship. Hebrews presents Christ as heavenly minister and the Church as already come to Zion. Revelation offers the most developed biblical portrait of heavenly liturgy centered on the Lamb. Luke 24, Acts 2, and 1 Corinthians 10 show the earthly Church’s sacramental and communal life as participation rather than mere memorial observation.

Together these texts establish that heavenly liturgy is real, present, participatory, christological, and formative for the Church.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

Chapters 34–37 collectively ask how God forms a communion people. Chapter 34 answered that the Church is covenant-temple: who we are. Chapter 35 must now answer where we participate. If the Church is the people in whom God dwells and whom He forms through worship, what is the sphere of that worship? The answer is heavenly liturgy.

This question matters because worship can easily be conceived too narrowly. It may be treated as gathered religious practice enclosed within earthly space, or as a symbolic reminder of truths located elsewhere, or as a communal spiritual experience generated by the people themselves. But the argument of the book has already pushed strongly in another direction. Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat, opens access, brings the Church into heavenly worship, speaks within communion, renews the nous, and forms a people. If this is true, then ecclesial worship cannot be interpreted from below alone. Its true locus is heavenly.

The prompt explicitly asks whether heavenly worship is adequately linked to Hebrews 12, Revelation, and Exodus 25. This chapter must therefore draw together the sanctuary pattern, the heavenly throne-room, and the present ecclesial participation of the Church. It is not enough to affirm that heaven inspires worship or that worship resembles heaven. The claim must be stronger: in Christ, the Church actually participates in heaven’s liturgy, and it is that participation that forms her.

This chapter also advances the central movement of the whole project. The people are formed because in worship they enter the sphere where communion, hearing, renewal, and covenantal identity converge. The heavenly liturgy is not an appendix to ecclesiology; it is the place where ecclesiology lives.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks where the Church truly participates in worship and argues that she is gathered into the heavenly liturgy of God and the Lamb, which forms her as a communion people.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From imagining worship as enclosed earthly activity → to understanding it as participation in heaven’s liturgy through Christ.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several inadequate answers weaken the doctrine of heavenly liturgy.

One inadequate answer treats worship as symbolic-only. The Church’s acts then refer to heavenly realities without any real participation in them. Such an account may preserve reverence or theological memory, but it cannot do justice to Hebrews 12’s present-tense approach to Zion or Revelation 8’s integration of the prayers of the saints into heavenly worship.

A second inadequate answer reduces worship to religious psychology. Worship becomes chiefly inward experience—elevation, awe, consolation, or communal energy. While interior participation matters, this reading makes heaven largely subjective. It is the people’s experience of transcendence rather than their true entry into the liturgy of the Lamb.

A third inadequate answer makes heavenly liturgy purely future. Heaven’s worship belongs to the age to come, but present Christian worship remains on earth only. This fails to reckon sufficiently with the already-and-not-yet logic of Hebrews and Revelation. The Church awaits full vision, yet she has already come to Zion.

A fourth inadequate answer isolates heavenly worship from the sanctuary pattern of Exodus 25 and the wider temple tradition. In that case Revelation becomes a disconnected spectacle rather than the unveiling of what the Mercy Seat, cherubic throne, priestly mediation, and divine speech anticipated. The chapter must resist that severing and show deep continuity of purpose.

A fifth inadequate answer presents heavenly liturgy as detached from formation. It may be doctrinally affirmed yet remain abstract, doing little practical work in how the Church understands itself. But the whole thrust of this chapter is that heaven’s liturgy forms earth’s people. If participation is real, formation follows.

These inadequate readings fail because they do not adequately account for the Church’s present participation, christological access, or liturgical formation. The chapter therefore seeks to show heavenly liturgy as the active sphere in which God forms His people through communion and covenant renewal.

**Section Summary:** Symbolic-only, psychological, future-only, disconnected, and abstract accounts fail because they do not adequately present worship as present participation in heaven’s liturgy that forms the Church.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From reducing worship to reminder, mood, or future hope → to seeing it as real present heavenly participation shaping God’s people.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### Exodus 25 and the Sanctuary Pattern

Exodus 25:17–22 establishes the fundamental older covenant pattern: God meets and speaks from above the Mercy Seat between the cherubim. This is not simply a detail of tabernacle furniture. It is the grammar of sacred presence, covenantal access, and liturgical approach. The sanctuary is the place where God dwells among His people and where divine speech proceeds.

This older pattern is indispensable for understanding heavenly liturgy. Without it, Revelation’s throne-room and Hebrews’ sanctuary language risk being treated as generic heavenly symbolism. With it, they appear as fulfillment and unveiling.

### Heavenly Worship in the Prophets

Isaiah 6 and Daniel 7 reveal that the true center of worship is heavenly. Isaiah sees the Lord enthroned, the seraphim crying “Holy, holy, holy,” and receives purification and commission. Daniel sees the Ancient of Days enthroned and the Son of Man receiving dominion. These are not yet Christian liturgies, but they disclose the heavenly worship toward which earthly sanctuary life points.

This supports the chapter’s central argument: the liturgy in which the Church participates is not invented by the Church. It precedes her. It is God’s own heavenly worship.

### Hebrews: Present Access to Heavenly Zion

Hebrews 8 and 10 insist that Christ is minister in the true tent and that believers now have confidence to enter the holy places by His blood. Hebrews 12 then names the reality more fully: the Church has come to heavenly Zion. The cumulative force is strong. Christian worship is not merely analogous to sanctuary life. It is access to the heavenly sanctuary through the High Priest.

This is perhaps the chapter’s most direct answer to the prompt. Heavenly worship is linked to Hebrews 12 because Hebrews 12 plainly states present participation in the heavenly assembly. It is also linked to Exodus 25 because the same access, presence, and speech logic has now been opened into heaven.

### Revelation: The Liturgy of the Throne and the Lamb

Revelation 4–5 provides the fullest biblical depiction of heavenly liturgy. The throne, elders, living creatures, hymns, and above all the Lamb standing as slain form the heart of the Church’s worshiping reality. The decisive point is not simply that John sees heaven, but that heaven is liturgical, christological, and communal. It is worship structured around the worthiness of the Lamb.

Revelation 7 expands the vision to include the redeemed multitude. Revelation 8 shows the prayers of the saints ascending in relation to heavenly altar and incense. Revelation 14 portrays the undefiled firstfruits standing with the Lamb. Revelation 21–22 shows final dwelling, the throne of God and the Lamb, and servants worshiping Him. The whole book witnesses that the people of God are formed by the heavenly liturgy that centers on Christ.

### Heavenly Worship as Participation, Not Observation

The key theological move must be explicit: heavenly liturgy is participatory rather than merely visionary. Hebrews says the Church has come. Revelation 8 says the prayers of the saints rise there. First Corinthians 10 says the Eucharistic cup and bread are communion. Luke 24 and Acts 2 show the earthly Church gathered around word, table, and prayer in ways consonant with heavenly participation. The Church is not a spectator before heaven’s worship. She is caught up into it.

### Worship as Covenant Renewal

Heavenly liturgy is also covenant renewal. The blood that speaks, the mediator of the new covenant, the Lamb slain, the people gathered, and the praise offered all belong to covenantal communion. Worship forms the people because in it God binds them to Himself again and again through word, praise, table, and presence.

### Formation Through Participation

Because worship is participation in heaven’s liturgy, it forms the Church’s identity, memory, desire, speech, hope, and obedience. The Church learns who she is by where she stands: before the throne, through the Lamb, among saints and angels, under the word, and on the way to final sight.

**Section Summary:** Scripture presents heavenly liturgy as the fulfilled sanctuary reality in which the Church already participates through Christ, especially in Hebrews 12 and Revelation’s throne-room visions.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing heaven as the backdrop of worship only → to understanding the Church’s worship as real participation in the liturgy of the throne and the Lamb.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers frequently speak as though heavenly liturgy were not an optional devotional image but the true environment of Christian worship.

John Chrysostom is especially important. His liturgical imagination is saturated with the conviction that the Church worships with angels and stands before the heavenly throne. He does not present this as rhetorical embellishment but as the theological truth of Christian liturgy.

Cyril of Jerusalem’s mystagogical teaching also assumes that the faithful, in sacramental worship, are participating in realities beyond ordinary sight. Earthly rites are not empty rehearsals. They are entrances into the mystery of Christ’s heavenly life.

Maximus the Confessor is perhaps the single most important patristic witness for this chapter. In the *Mystagogy*, he understands liturgy as the uniting of heaven and earth, visible and invisible, present and future. Worship forms the people because it places them in that very participatory order. This resonates profoundly with the chapter’s burden.

Pseudo-Dionysius, with due discernment, also reinforces the sense that the Church’s worship participates in heavenly order and praise. The symbolic forms of worship are not barriers to reality but participatory mediations of it.

The Orthodox liturgical tradition as a whole preserves this instinct vividly. Worship is entrance into the heavenly sanctuary, standing with angels, beholding the Lamb, and participating in the kingdom. This makes the chapter’s claims not only exegetically plausible but ecclesially lived.

**Section Summary:** Patristic and liturgical theology strongly support the claim that the Church’s worship participates in heavenly liturgy and is thereby formed by the order of heaven.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From thinking heavenly worship language is devotional excess → to seeing it as the Church’s normal theological grammar for liturgical participation.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that heavenly liturgy is the participatory sphere in which God forms His covenant-temple people. Worship is communion, formation, participation, and covenant renewal because the Church is gathered through Christ into heaven’s worship.

Several affirmations give this synthesis clarity.

First, heavenly liturgy is christological. The Lamb is at its center, and all access is through Him.

Second, heavenly liturgy fulfills the Mercy Seat pattern. What Exodus 25 anticipated in earthly sanctuary form is unveiled in throne-room worship.

Third, heavenly liturgy is present participation. Hebrews 12 and Revelation together resist reducing it to future hope or symbolic recollection only.

Fourth, heavenly liturgy is covenantal. The mediator, the speaking blood, the gathered people, and the worshiping assembly all testify to covenant renewal.

Fifth, heavenly liturgy is formative. The Church becomes the communion people of God by participating there.

Sixth, heavenly liturgy is already and not yet. It is real now and awaits unveiled consummation in Revelation 21–22.

This synthesis directly answers the chapter’s key question. How does God use worship to form His people? He forms them by bringing them into the heavenly liturgy of Christ, where presence, speech, praise, covenant, and hope converge.

**Section Summary:** Heavenly liturgy is the christological, covenantal, participatory sphere in which the Church is formed as God’s communion community.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing worship as human action addressed upward → to seeing it as God-given participation in heaven’s worship that shapes His people.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter has strong anthropological implications because it reveals that human beings are not formed only by ideas or ethics, but by participation in ordered worship. People become like the world they inhabit. If the Church inhabits heaven’s liturgy in Christ, then the people of God are shaped by holiness, praise, reverence, joy, truth, and hope.

This means the renewed nous and transformed desire are sustained ecclesially through heavenly participation. The mind learns to see from above. Desire learns to love what heaven loves. The person is decentered from self and re-centered on God and the Lamb. Humility grows because the worshiper stands within a reality he did not create and cannot control.

Spiritually, heavenly liturgy protects the believer from both immanentism and activism. Worship is not merely motivational prelude to work; it is formative participation in the true order of reality. The people leave worship shaped by what they have entered.

This also gives serious weight to regular, gathered worship. If heavenly liturgy forms the people, then absence from worship is not only absence from a communal duty. It is deprivation from the environment in which God shapes His communion people.

**Section Summary:** Heavenly participation forms human perception, desire, humility, and communal identity because people are shaped by the worshiping reality they inhabit before God.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From assuming formation happens mainly by moral instruction → to seeing worship itself as a primary means of divine formation.

---

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church’s earthly liturgy is the place where heavenly participation is enacted. Word, Eucharist, prayer, praise, confession, thanksgiving, and blessing are not arbitrary religious gestures. They are the ecclesial modes by which the people of God enter heaven’s worship through Christ.

Ecclesially, this means the Church’s identity is inseparable from her gathered liturgy. She is not merely a community that happens to worship; she is the community that becomes itself in worship. Her covenant-temple identity from Chapter 34 is enacted and deepened here.

The heavenly liturgy also explains why worship is covenant renewal. The people hear again the divine word, are gathered again by the blood of the covenant, give thanks again, offer praise again, and are sent again as those formed by heaven’s order. Worship is not repetitive in a barren sense. It is the repeated inhabitation of the reality that makes the Church who she is.

Eschatologically, the Church’s liturgy remains a foretaste. She participates truly yet not fully. She worships in hope, knowing that one day the heavenly liturgy in which she now participates sacramentally and ecclesially will become unveiled sight in the new Jerusalem.

**Section Summary:** The Church’s gathered worship enacts heavenly participation and thereby renews her covenant life, forms her identity, and trains her hope toward final sight.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating liturgy as routine repetition → to understanding it as repeated participation in the heavenly worship that forms the Church.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps restore a rich theology of worship as participation rather than performance.

Second, it strengthens the link between Hebrews 12, Revelation, and Exodus 25 in ecclesial formation.

Third, it gives worship a stronger role in spiritual formation, beyond mere instruction or inspiration.

Fourth, it deepens sacramental theology by locating Eucharistic life within heavenly participation.

Fifth, it helps pastors teach the Church why regular liturgy matters so profoundly for identity and holiness.

Sixth, it prepares the way for the following chapters on patristic witness and the ordo by clarifying what the Church is actually doing in worship.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Does this overstate the present reality of heavenly liturgy?

**Reply:** Not if the already-and-not-yet is maintained. The chapter claims real present participation, not full unveiled possession.

### Objection 2: Could this turn worship into escapism from earthly responsibility?

**Reply:** No. Participation in heaven’s worship is precisely what forms the Church for faithful embodied life on earth.

### Objection 3: Is this too dependent on apocalyptic imagery?

**Reply:** The imagery is canonical and theologically interpreted through Hebrews, Exodus, and Christ’s priestly work. It is not decorative excess but revelation of worship’s true sphere.

### Objection 4: Why insist that heavenly liturgy is formative?

**Reply:** Because the Church is shaped by where she stands, what she beholds, what she hears, and in whom she participates. Worship is not neutral.

## Conclusion

Heavenly liturgy is where the Church participates, and it is one of the principal ways God forms His people. This is the central claim of the chapter. Worship is not merely earthly religious action aimed upward toward an absent heaven. In Christ, the Church is brought into heaven’s own worship—the throne-room liturgy centered on the Lamb, anticipated in Exodus, unveiled in Revelation, and declared present in Hebrews 12.

This means the Church’s worship is communion, formation, participation, and covenant renewal all at once. She is formed by what she does because what she does is not self-enclosed human activity. It is participation in the order of heaven. The Lamb’s worthiness, the blood that speaks, the saints’ prayers, the angelic praise, and the presence of God all shape the people who stand there.

The chapter therefore advances the larger argument of the book in a decisive ecclesial direction. If worship forms the people of God, that formation cannot be explained merely by atmosphere, pedagogy, or repetition. It must be explained by participation in the heavenly liturgy itself. The Church becomes a communion people because she worships within heaven’s communion.

Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question may be stated plainly: God uses worship to form His people by gathering them into the heavenly liturgy of Christ. There He renews covenant life, shapes desire, restores perception, deepens humility, and forms the Church into the people who will one day move from sacramental participation to unveiled sight in the presence of God and the Lamb.
