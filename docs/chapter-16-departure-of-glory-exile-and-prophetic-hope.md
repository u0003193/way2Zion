# Chapter 16  
## Departure of Glory, Exile, and Prophetic Hope

### Thesis

Jeremiah 50:5 matters because it names the crisis and the longing at the heart of the biblical story after Eden and after Zion: God’s people have lost the way to ordered communion and must ask for it again. The departure of glory, the reality of exile, and the prophetic hope of return are not merely political or historical themes. They are the scriptural grammar of communion lost and communion longed for. Exile is not only displacement from land. It is loss of presence, loss of worship, loss of divine speech, and loss of ordered covenant life before God. To ask again for the way to Zion is therefore to ask for the restoration of what humanity was created for and what sin repeatedly forfeits.

This chapter argues that the prophetic witness must be read in continuity with Eden, sanctuary, and Zion. Chapter 14 showed that Eden was the original sanctuary of communion. Chapter 15 showed that Zion and the ark became the historical and theological concentration of ordered divine dwelling, enthronement, speech, and pilgrimage. Chapter 16 now shows what happens when that communion is broken at the level of national and covenant history: glory departs, worship is disrupted, the people are scattered, and Zion becomes object of longing rather than lived reality. The exile is thus not merely punishment in the abstract. It is sanctuary loss in historical form.

This is why Jeremiah 50:5 becomes programmatic for the whole project: “They shall ask the way to Zion, with faces turned toward it.” This is not simply travel language. It is theological yearning. The people must ask for the way because the way has been lost. They must turn their faces because desire must be reoriented. They must seek Zion because communion has been ruptured. In one verse, exile, memory, repentance, pilgrimage, and hope are gathered together. Jeremiah 50:5 therefore functions as a concise summary of the problem the whole book addresses.

The chapter’s central claim is that the departure of glory and the experience of exile reveal the true depth of sin and the true meaning of salvation. What was lost was not merely secure territory or national continuity. What was lost was God’s manifest dwelling, liturgical order, covenant hearing, and holy fellowship. The prophets therefore long not simply for political restoration, but for renewed presence, renewed speech, renewed worship, and renewed Zion. Their hope reaches forward beyond postexilic reconstruction to the deeper restoration fulfilled only in Christ and consummated only in Revelation 21–22. Exile is the historical repetition of Eden’s expulsion. Prophetic hope is the refusal to let that exile have the final word.

### Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

The primary biblical texts for this chapter include 1 Samuel 4; Psalm 74; Psalm 79; Psalm 137; Isaiah 40; Isaiah 52; Jeremiah 3:12–18; Jeremiah 31:31–34; Jeremiah 50:4–5; Ezekiel 8–11; Ezekiel 36:24–28; Ezekiel 37:24–28; and Zechariah 8. These are read together with the wider canonical horizon of Genesis 3, Exodus 25:17–22, Numbers 7:89, Psalm 99, Hebrews 12:22–24, and Revelation 21–22.

1 Samuel 4 provides an early warning sign in the loss of the ark and the cry, “The glory has departed.” Psalm 74, Psalm 79, and Psalm 137 give liturgical and emotional voice to the devastation of sanctuary ruin and exile. Ezekiel 8–11 narrates the departure of glory from the temple with extraordinary theological force. Jeremiah 3, 31, and especially 50:5 reveal exile not only as judgment but as the occasion for renewed seeking of Zion and covenant renewal. Isaiah 40 and 52 proclaim comfort, return, and the re-enthronement of God as redemptive hope. Ezekiel 36–37 promises cleansing, new heart, new spirit, renewed Davidic shepherding, and divine dwelling in the midst of the people once more. Zechariah further sustains the hope of restored presence. Hebrews and Revelation show that this hope reaches its final truth in heavenly Zion and the New Jerusalem.

Together these texts establish that exile is loss of communion and prophetic hope is longing for restored presence and speech.

## I. Context and Question at Issue

Part III of the book is tracing the loss and promise of communion. Chapter 14 looked back to Eden as proto-temple and asked what was lost in the Fall. Chapter 15 showed that Zion and the ark represent ordered communion in the historical life of Israel: God’s dwelling, throne, speaking place, and the destination of covenant pilgrimage. The present chapter now asks what it means when even Zion is lost, when glory departs, and when the covenant people are cast into exile. If Eden was the first sanctuary lost and Zion the concentrated place of restored historical communion, what does exile signify? And why must the people ask for the way to Zion again?

These are not merely historical questions. They cut to the heart of the book’s controlling thesis. If God created humanity for communion, if sin disrupted communion, and if God’s redemptive work serves access to communion, then exile must be understood as a radical manifestation of that same disruption. Exile is not only disciplinary relocation. It is covenantal disorientation. It is what happens when a people called to dwell before God loses the place, order, speech, and life of that dwelling.

This is why Jeremiah 50:5 is so important. The verse captures a moment in which return is desired but not yet simply possessed. The people must ask the way to Zion because communion has become estranged enough that its path must be sought again. Zion remains the destination, but not the present habitation. The verse also implies that the heart can be turned back toward that destination. Exile therefore produces not only judgment but longing. It becomes the place where desire is exposed and, potentially, redirected.

The chapter must therefore explain why the loss of temple, land, glory, speech, and liturgical order matters theologically. Modern readings can be tempted to flatten exile into political catastrophe or identity crisis. Those dimensions are real, but biblical theology presses further. The real crisis is loss of presence. The prophetic hope is therefore not satisfied with mere resettlement. It seeks renewed divine dwelling, renewed covenant, renewed holiness, renewed hearing, and renewed Zion. This chapter must make that clear if the book’s use of Jeremiah 50:5 is to be justified.

**Section Summary:** The chapter asks what exile means when read through the book’s communion theology and argues that Jeremiah 50:5 names the spiritual crisis of a people who have lost the way to God’s dwelling.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing exile mainly as political catastrophe → to recognizing it as the loss of sanctuary communion and the reawakening of desire for Zion.

## II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

Several common readings of exile and prophetic hope fail to reach the depth required by the biblical witness.

One inadequate reading reduces exile chiefly to political punishment. On this view Israel loses land, monarchy, autonomy, and security because of national disobedience. This is true as far as it goes, but it does not go far enough. The destruction of temple, interruption of liturgical life, departure of glory, and the silence or distortion of covenant hearing reveal that the catastrophe is more than political. It is the loss of ordered communion with God.

A second inadequate reading moralizes exile as simply the consequence of bad behavior. Sin is indeed central, and prophetic texts insist on covenant infidelity. Yet if exile is read merely as deserved discipline, the positive content of what is lost and what is hoped for may be obscured. The issue is not only that punishment has fallen. It is that worship has been broken, presence has withdrawn, speech has become distant, and Zion has become absence. The prophets grieve not merely suffering but lost nearness.

A third inadequate reading spiritualizes Zion and exile too quickly. It moves immediately to inward alienation or existential homelessness, bypassing temple, land, ark, city, and covenant history. Again, there is a legitimate spiritual dimension. But if one abandons the historical and liturgical realities too soon, the canonical logic becomes thin. The prophets mourn a real sanctuary, a real city, a real people, and a real withdrawal of glory. Only on that basis can the spiritual and christological dimensions be rightly perceived.

A fourth inadequate reading construes prophetic hope mainly as national restoration. Such a reading may rightly emphasize return from Babylon, rebuilt walls, and revived identity. But prophetic hope consistently outstrips mere restoration of political structure. Jeremiah promises new covenant; Ezekiel promises new heart, new spirit, renewed Davidic shepherd, and everlasting dwelling; Isaiah speaks of the nations, comfort, and the renewed reign of God. The hope is ultimately communion restored, not simply political recovery.

A fifth inadequate reading disconnects exile from the earlier sanctuary and Edenic pattern. But Scripture itself suggests continuity. Eastward expulsion from Eden, departure of glory from temple, and the people’s displacement from Zion all participate in one deeper reality: sanctuary loss. Exile is the historical repetition of humanity’s original estrangement.

These inadequate approaches fail because they do not fully integrate presence, speech, worship, and communion into the meaning of exile. The chapter therefore seeks to recover exile as loss of divine dwelling and prophetic hope as longing for restored Zionic communion.

**Section Summary:** Political, moralistic, overly spiritualized, merely national, and non-canonical readings of exile all fail to account for the loss of presence, worship, and divine speech.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating exile as punishment or displacement alone → to seeing it as the historical rupture of sanctuary communion and the stimulus to renewed longing.

## III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

### “The Glory Has Departed”

Though the Babylonian exile is the chapter’s main focus, the cry “The glory has departed” in 1 Samuel 4 provides an important anticipatory signal. The capture of the ark and the naming of Ichabod reveal that Israel early learned the horror of losing the sign of divine presence. Even before the later temple theology is fully developed, the loss of the ark is experienced as the loss of glory. This anticipates the later prophetic drama and shows that presence, not merely military fortune, is the heart of the crisis.

This matters because it helps establish a pattern. The true disaster for God’s people is not simply defeat, but the withdrawal or loss of the reality by which they are a people at all: divine dwelling among them.

### Ezekiel and the Departure of Glory

Ezekiel 8–11 is among the most devastating and theologically significant passages in all of Scripture for this chapter’s argument. The prophet is shown abominations in the temple, and then the glory of the Lord departs in stages from the sanctuary, the threshold, the city, and finally the mountain east of it. This is not merely symbolic commentary on political judgment. It is the enactment of sanctuary rupture. The temple can no longer function as secure sign of communion because the people have desecrated the relationship it was meant to embody.

The departure of glory makes visible what exile truly is. God’s people lose not just place but presence. The liturgical center is hollowed out. Worship is judged. The speaking and dwelling God withdraws. The eastward movement of glory even echoes the eastward expulsions and movements already embedded in Genesis, reinforcing the connection between Eden lost and Zion/temple lost. Exile is sanctuary loss in historical form.

### The Psalms of Ruin and Silence

Psalm 74, Psalm 79, and Psalm 137 give emotional, liturgical, and theological voice to this disaster. The enemies burn the sanctuary. The holy places are defiled. The songs of Zion can scarcely be sung in a foreign land. The grief is not merely patriotic. It is liturgical. The people lament the destruction of the place where God’s name dwelt and where His praise was ordered. They feel the loss of worship as the loss of identity.

This is critical for the present chapter because it shows that exile involves loss of worship and, by implication, loss of the communal forms by which divine presence and speech were inhabited. The people are not only homeless. They are disoriented in relation to God’s dwelling. Their very songs become sites of pain.

### Jeremiah 50:5 and the Recovery of Desire

Jeremiah 50:4–5 stands at the center of this chapter’s argument: “In those days and in that time, declares the Lord, the people of Israel and the people of Judah shall come together, weeping as they come, and they shall seek the Lord their God. They shall ask the way to Zion, with faces turned toward it, saying, ‘Come, let us join ourselves to the Lord in an everlasting covenant that will never be forgotten.’”

This text is extraordinarily rich. First, it presents return as penitential and communal. The people come weeping, not triumphantly. Second, they seek the Lord, not merely territory. Third, they ask the way to Zion. This implies that exile is deep enough to require renewed orientation. Fourth, they desire everlasting covenant. Return is not conceived as mere reversal of circumstances but as renewed belonging to God. Jeremiah 50:5 therefore concentrates exile, longing, covenant renewal, and Zion pilgrimage into one verse.

This is why the verse matters so much for the book. It names the spiritual condition of God’s people after communion has been disrupted. They must ask for the way again because they have lost not just geographical bearings but covenantal orientation. Their faces must be turned because desire itself must be reoriented toward the place of God’s dwelling.

### Prophetic Hope: Cleansing, New Heart, New Dwelling

Jeremiah and Ezekiel both insist that restoration must exceed mere return. Jeremiah 31 promises a new covenant in which God’s law is written on the heart. Ezekiel 36 promises cleansing, new heart, and new spirit. Ezekiel 37 promises one shepherd, peace, and everlasting dwelling: “My dwelling place shall be with them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.” These texts show that prophetic hope is fundamentally about restored communion. The people need more than return to land. They need inward renewal and renewed divine indwelling.

Isaiah 40 and 52 further deepen this by proclaiming comfort, return, and the reign of God. The point is not only that captives will come home, but that God Himself will reign again in Zion and His people will behold His salvation. Zion thus becomes the object of purified hope because it is the place where divine presence, covenant, and worship are restored.

### Christological and Eschatological Completion

The New Testament does not erase this prophetic horizon; it fulfills it. Hebrews 12 identifies the Church’s access with heavenly Zion. The people of God have come to the city of the living God, Jesus the mediator, and sprinkled blood. Revelation 21–22 reveals the final answer to exile: the dwelling of God with humanity, the removal of the curse, and the restoration of worship, light, and presence. Jeremiah 50:5 is thus not merely about post-Babylonian return. It reaches toward the final restoration of communion in Christ.

The scriptural claim is therefore clear: exile means loss of presence, worship, and divine speech, while prophetic hope means renewed desire for Zion and restored communion with God.

**Section Summary:** Scripture presents exile as the departure of glory, the loss of worship and speaking presence, and prophetic hope as the reorientation of a people seeking again the way to Zion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From seeing prophetic hope as simple return-from-exile → to understanding it as renewed desire for restored communion with God.

## IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

The fathers repeatedly read exile not only as historical disaster but as revelation of humanity’s estrangement from God and of the soul’s longing for restored communion.

Irenaeus helps frame exile within the broader arc of divine pedagogy. The people’s scattering and longing are not meaningless interruptions but moments within the larger divine purpose of drawing humanity back to Himself. The prophetic promises of renewed covenant and dwelling therefore take on pedagogical depth: God teaches His people through loss what they had ceased to value in presence.

Origen, though to be approached critically, is important for seeing the exilic texts as concerning both Israel and the soul. When disciplined by the literal and historical sense, his insight can be fruitful: exile manifests disordered love, and Zion names the return to divine order. He is strongest when he lets the historical grief sharpen the spiritual longing rather than replace it.

Athanasius’s participatory theology helps again by making clear that salvation’s purpose is restored life with God. Exile can thus be read as the historical and communal counterpart to the corruption and estrangement he sees in Adamic humanity. The hope of restoration is always the hope of divine communion.

Gregory of Nyssa’s theology of desire is especially useful for Jeremiah 50:5. To ask the way to Zion is to have desire redirected toward its true end. Longing itself becomes theological. The prophets do not simply predict return; they retrain the heart to want the right city, the right presence, the right communion.

Maximus the Confessor provides conceptual strength here too. When the nous is darkened and desire disordered, exile becomes not only historical but anthropological. Restoration requires reorientation toward God. Zion thus functions as one of the great biblical names for the end of that reorientation: the ordered gathering of the many in the one communion of God.

Orthodox hymnography and liturgical theology often preserve this exilic sense strongly. The Church sings of Babylon, Jerusalem, paradise, and Zion not as disconnected images but as realities within the drama of loss and restoration. This tradition reinforces the chapter’s insistence that exile is sanctuary loss and prophetic hope is longing for restored presence.

**Section Summary:** Patristic theology reads exile as estrangement from divine life and Zion as the rightly desired return to ordered communion with God.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From treating prophetic texts as mere historical reportage → to hearing them as theological and anthropological witness to communion lost and desired again.

## V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that exile must be understood as the loss of ordered communion with God, and prophetic hope as the recovery of desire and direction toward restored Zionic presence. Jeremiah 50:5 matters because it names in condensed form the people’s need to seek again the way to communion after it has been forfeited.

Several affirmations converge here.

First, communion is the central good lost in exile. Land, monarchy, and stability matter precisely because they were dimensions of a deeper reality: life before God in His dwelling.

Second, exile is loss of presence. The departure of glory in Ezekiel makes explicit what the destruction of temple and city implies. The crisis is that God’s manifest dwelling has withdrawn.

Third, exile is loss of worship. The sanctuary is ruined, songs are disrupted, and liturgical life is fractured. A people formed for ordered worship becomes a people of dislocation.

Fourth, exile is loss of divine speech in its covenantal immediacy. The people are no longer living in the ordered environment where the word of God structures communal life from Zion outward. The prophetic word remains, but often now as word of judgment, distance, or promised future rather than as settled liturgical ordering.

Fifth, prophetic hope is longing for Zion because Zion represents renewed dwelling, renewed speech, renewed worship, and renewed covenant. The people ask for the way because the way has been lost in history and desire alike.

Sixth, Christ fulfills this hope. In Him the new covenant is enacted, the way to Zion is opened, the dwelling of God returns, and the pilgrim people are gathered toward the final city. Exile thus finds its answer only in the one who restores communion completely.

This doctrinal synthesis directly supports the book’s controlling movement. Communion lost produces the crisis of hearing lost, desire disordered, humility absent, worship broken, and pilgrimage interrupted. Prophetic hope begins to reverse that by turning the face toward Zion once more.

**Section Summary:** Exile is the rupture of presence, worship, and covenantal orientation, while prophetic hope is the reawakening of longing for restored communion in Zion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From defining exile mainly by punishment or displacement → to understanding it as the loss of God’s dwelling and the reeducation of desire toward return.

## VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

This chapter has deep anthropological implications because it shows what happens to human beings and communities when communion with God is lost historically and existentially. Exile is not merely something that happens to a nation; it manifests what becomes of humanity when presence is withdrawn, worship is disordered, and speech is no longer rightly heard. The human being becomes displaced in the deepest sense.

This displacement affects desire immediately. The people in Jeremiah 50:5 must turn their faces toward Zion because exile has bent them elsewhere. Longing has become confused. The chapter therefore helps explain why the book places so much emphasis on desire. Exile is not only loss of location; it is misdirection of love. To ask the way to Zion again is already to begin recovering rightly ordered desire.

The chapter also illuminates the role of the nous. In exile, hearing is disrupted. The liturgical and covenantal environment that sustained divine address is fractured. This does not mean God ceases to speak, but it means the people’s life is no longer stably ordered by His presence and word. Hence prophetic hope must include not only return but renewed covenant and renewed heart. The nous must be restored if communion is to be regained.

Humility appears here as well. Exile shatters presumption. The people must ask the way. They do not possess it automatically. Such asking is already an act of humbled desire. They come weeping, not self-assured. This is profoundly important for the book’s larger argument. Humility is not merely a private virtue; it emerges historically and communally when illusions of secure possession are broken and the people must seek God again.

Spiritually, the chapter teaches that repentance is not only turning from sin in the abstract, but turning the face again toward Zion. It is reorientation toward the place of God’s communion. The Church’s own spiritual life often repeats this pattern. She must continually ask again for the way when worship becomes hollow, speech becomes muted, and desire becomes scattered.

**Section Summary:** Exile reveals humanity as spiritually displaced and desire as disoriented, while repentance begins when the heart again asks for and turns toward Zion.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From viewing exile as external misfortune → to recognizing it as the disordering of presence, hearing, desire, and communal identity before God.

## VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

The Church reads exile and prophetic hope not as relics of Israel’s past but as realities fulfilled and interpreted in Christ. She knows herself as a pilgrim people who have come to heavenly Zion in one sense and yet still long for the full dwelling of God in another. This gives her worship a distinctive tension: access already granted, consummation still awaited.

Ecclesially, the chapter teaches that the Church must guard against forms of exile within her own life. Worship may continue outwardly while desire drifts, hearing dulls, and the felt reality of God’s presence is neglected. The prophetic texts therefore remain searching words for the Church. They call the people of God not only to remember ancient disaster but to ask again for the way to Zion whenever communion is functionally lost.

Liturgically, the Church embodies prophetic hope by gathering as those whose faces are turned toward Zion. Her worship is already a refusal of exile’s finality. In word and sacrament, prayer and praise, confession and blessing, she practices reorientation. She does not generate Zion, but she is drawn toward it and begins to inhabit it by anticipation through Christ.

This also clarifies the Church’s eschatological identity. Revelation 21–22 is not merely future scenery. It is the end toward which exilic longing moves. The Church becomes a people of hope because she knows what was lost, what is promised, and what has begun to be restored in Christ. She sings not only of paradise lost but of Zion found and new Jerusalem coming down from God.

Thus the ecclesial significance of Jeremiah 50:5 remains great. The people of God are always those who ask the way to Zion, with faces turned toward it. The Church’s deepest identity is not settled self-possession, but pilgrim longing under promise.

**Section Summary:** The Church lives as an exilic yet hopeful people, gathering in worship as those already oriented toward heavenly Zion and awaiting the full restoration of divine dwelling.

**Before → After Frame Shift:** From reading exile as Israel’s past only → to understanding the Church’s liturgy and pilgrimage as shaped by the longing for restored Zion.

## Practical Outcomes

First, this chapter helps readers grasp why Jeremiah 50:5 can function as an apt epigraph for the whole book: it names the people’s lost orientation and renewed longing for communion.

Second, it deepens preaching on exile by moving beyond political displacement toward the loss of presence, worship, and divine speech.

Third, it clarifies repentance as reorientation toward Zion rather than mere moral regret.

Fourth, it equips the Church to diagnose spiritual exile within her own life when worship becomes hollow and desire drifts.

Fifth, it strengthens eschatological hope by showing that Zion-longing reaches its completion in Revelation 21–22.

Sixth, it provides a bridge from Old Testament prophetic sorrow to New Testament access in Christ and the Church’s pilgrim worship.

## Objections & Replies

### Objection 1: Isn’t this chapter making exile too “spiritual” and not sufficiently historical?

**Reply:** No. The chapter insists on the historical reality of temple destruction, displacement, and political judgment, but argues that Scripture itself interprets these as theological losses of presence and worship.

### Objection 2: Does emphasizing loss of speech imply God ceased speaking in exile?

**Reply:** Not absolutely. The prophetic word continues, often intensely. The point is that the settled liturgical and covenantal ordering of communal life by divine presence and speech is disrupted. Speech becomes more promissory and judgment-laden than stably inhabitable.

### Objection 3: Why make Jeremiah 50:5 so central?

**Reply:** Because it uniquely gathers return, desire, covenant renewal, communal weeping, and Zion-directed pilgrimage into a single compact statement that mirrors the book’s whole argument.

### Objection 4: If Hebrews says believers have already come to Zion, is the language of longing still appropriate?

**Reply:** Yes. The New Testament holds together inaugurated access and eschatological consummation. The Church already participates in heavenly Zion and still longs for its unveiled fullness.

## Conclusion

Jeremiah 50:5 matters because it names the human and covenantal condition after communion has been lost: God’s people must ask the way to Zion again. That single verse gathers the whole drama of exile into an act of reoriented longing. It assumes that Zion is the place of God’s dwelling, speech, and ordered worship. It assumes that the way has been lost through sin and judgment. It assumes that return is possible only through renewed seeking of the Lord. And it assumes that the people’s faces must be turned again toward the place of communion.

This is precisely why exile cannot be reduced to political displacement. Exile is loss of presence, loss of worship, loss of divine speech in its settled covenantal form, and loss of the city of ordered communion. The departure of glory in Ezekiel reveals the heart of the disaster. The laments of the Psalms give its grief a voice. The prophets then turn that grief into hope by promising renewed covenant, renewed cleansing, renewed heart, renewed dwelling, and renewed Zion.

This chapter therefore serves as the necessary completion of Part III’s opening movement. Eden showed what communion originally was. Zion showed the historical and liturgical concentration of that communion. Exile shows how deeply that communion can be lost in history. Prophetic hope shows that loss does not have the final word. God’s people may ask for the way again, and God Himself will provide it.

In Christ, that way is opened. He restores access to the true Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, and leads His people toward the day when the dwelling of God is fully with humanity once more. The Church now lives within that tension: already turned toward Zion, already tasting its life, yet still longing for its unveiled fullness. In that sense Jeremiah 50:5 remains the proper prayer of every pilgrim people. To ask the way to Zion is to ask again for the restoration of presence, hearing, worship, and communion—the very realities for which humanity was made from the beginning.
