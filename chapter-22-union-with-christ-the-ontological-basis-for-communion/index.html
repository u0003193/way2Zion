    Chapter 22

    Union with Christ: The Ontological Basis for Communion

    Thesis

    Communion with God is actually possible because believers are united to Christ. Union with Christ is not a decorative theological abstraction added to Christian doctrine after the real work of salvation has been explained elsewhere. Nor is it merely a metaphor for moral agreement, religious affiliation, or covenant membership. It is the ontological basis of communion: the real participation by which human beings share in the life of the incarnate, crucified, risen, and ascended Son by the Spirit. Without union with Christ, access would remain external, forgiveness would remain juridically isolated, and communion would remain aspirational. With union, what Christ has accomplished becomes participable. Believers do not merely admire His life from a distance; they share in it.

    This chapter argues that union with Christ must be presented primarily as participation, communion, and sharing in Christ’s life. It is the answer to the book’s question: how is communion actually possible? Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat, opens access to the heavenly sanctuary, and grants participation in communion with God, but the category that explains how His accomplished work becomes ours is union. The believer is in Christ, and Christ is in the believer. Through this union, His death becomes our death to sin, His resurrection our new life, His sonship the ground of adoption, His access the basis of our access, His worship the pattern and sphere of our worship, and His communion with the Father the source of our communion with God.

    This means union with Christ must not be reduced to legal imputation, ecclesial location, mystical language, or subjective religious feeling taken separately. All of these may be related, but none exhausts the reality. The New Testament presents union as comprehensive and participatory. Believers are crucified with Christ, raised with Christ, seated with Christ, indwelt by Christ, and incorporated into His body. Such language does not describe mere metaphorical solidarity. It names the real mode by which salvation is shared.

    This chapter therefore serves as the ontological hinge of Part V. The previous chapters showed how Christ fulfills the old covenant institutions and continues as the living mediator of communion. Chapter 25 will later show how communion is sustained through abiding. But in between those themes, this chapter must establish the deep basis of participation itself. Why can the believer truly share in Christ’s life, hear His word, be renewed by His presence, and be formed toward worship? Because the believer is united to Him. Union is not the whole of communion, but communion is impossible without it. It is the ontological bridge by which redemption becomes access and access becomes shared life in God.

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

    The primary biblical texts for this chapter include John 14:20; John 15:1–11; John 17:20–26; Romans 6:1–11; Romans 8:1–17; 1 Corinthians 1:30; 1 Corinthians 6:15–20; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 1 Corinthians 12:12–27; Galatians 2:19–20; Galatians 3:26–28; Ephesians 1:3–14; Ephesians 2:4–10; Ephesians 5:30–32; Colossians 2:9–15; Colossians 3:1–4; and 2 Peter 1:3–4. These are read in relation to the wider sanctuary and communion theology of Hebrews 10:19–22; Hebrews 12:22–24; and Revelation 21–22.

    John 14–17 establishes mutual indwelling and participation in the life and love shared by Father and Son. Romans 6 and Galatians 2 show that believers are united to Christ in death and resurrection. Romans 8 reveals the Spirit as the mode of this participation. First Corinthians emphasizes incorporation into Christ’s body and Eucharistic communion. Ephesians and Colossians speak of believers being blessed, raised, seated, and hidden in Christ. Second Peter’s language of participation in the divine nature must be read carefully, but it strongly confirms that salvation is participatory rather than merely external. These texts together show that union with Christ is the ontological ground of Christian communion.

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    Part V of the book turns explicitly to communion and participation. The argument has already shown that God created humanity for communion, that Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat, opens access, and gathers His people into worship, and that the ascended Christ remains the living mediator of communion. But all of this raises a further and unavoidable question: by what deep theological logic does Christ’s life become ours? How is communion actually possible rather than merely declared? The answer is union with Christ.

    This question matters because Christian theology can easily say many true things without yet reaching the level of ontological participation. One may say that Christ died for sinners, that access is opened, that the Spirit is given, that the Church worships, and that believers are justified. All of this is true. Yet one may still ask: how does the believer actually share in what Christ is and has accomplished? What is the mode by which the divine-human life of the incarnate Son becomes the life of His people? Union with Christ names that mode.

    The issue must be pressed because the phrase “union with Christ” can become too abstract. It may sound like a theological summary term rather than a living biblical reality. But the New Testament refuses abstraction here. It speaks of being in Christ, Christ in us, being buried with Him, raised with Him, one body in Him, branches in the vine, members of His flesh, and partakers of His life. These are not accidental flourishes. They are the grammar by which the apostles explain how salvation is shared rather than merely observed.

    This chapter must therefore show that union is not less than participation, not less than communion, and not less than sharing in Christ’s life. It is the ontological basis for the entire sequence the book traces later: hearing, noetic renewal, transformed desire, humility, self-offering, obedience, and worship. None of these can be sustained if Christ remains merely external. The believer must be joined to Him. Thus the chapter stands at a strategic place in the book: it provides the theological depth beneath the ongoing life of communion.

    Section Summary: The chapter asks how Christ’s life and work become truly ours and argues that union with Christ is the ontological basis by which communion is actually possible.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From speaking of salvation as external benefit only → to seeing it as real participation in the life of Christ.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Several common treatments of union with Christ fail to capture its full significance.

    One inadequate approach reduces union to legal representation alone. In this view the believer is “in Christ” primarily because Christ acts as covenant head or legal substitute, and the benefits of His work are credited to the believer. This dimension is deeply important and biblical, especially in Paul. Yet if union is exhausted by representation, participation recedes. The believer remains only juridically related to Christ rather than sharing in His life. But the New Testament speaks more deeply than this.

    A second inadequate approach reduces union to mystical feeling or inward religious awareness. One “feels close” to Christ, experiences His presence, or senses existential connectedness. Such experiences may indeed accompany union, but they cannot constitute its foundation. Union is real even when feeling fluctuates. It is grounded in Christ, Spirit, baptismal incorporation, and covenantal participation, not in emotional intensity alone.

    A third inadequate approach treats union as merely ecclesial belonging. To belong to the Church is, in a sense, to belong to Christ’s body. Yet if the language stops there, the believer’s direct participation in Christ’s death, life, and sonship may be obscured. Ecclesial incorporation is essential, but it is not the whole of union. The Church is what she is because she participates in Christ.

    A fourth inadequate approach makes union a kind of devotional shorthand for sanctification. One is “united to Christ” meaning simply that one should imitate Him or obey Him. But this reverses the logic. Sanctification and imitation flow from union; they do not define it. The branch bears fruit because it abides in the vine, not the reverse.

    A fifth inadequate approach, especially in some modern theology, fears ontological language altogether. Union is then spoken of so cautiously that it becomes almost indistinguishable from moral influence or covenant inclusion. But the New Testament’s language is bolder. Believers are in Christ, Christ lives in them, they die and rise with Him, and they partake of His body and blood. Theological caution must not flatten apostolic realism.

    These inadequate readings all fail because they separate what Scripture holds together: legal, ecclesial, participatory, sacramental, and transformative dimensions belong within one reality of union. The chapter therefore seeks to recover that integrated account.

    Section Summary: Legal-only, emotional, merely ecclesial, moralistic, and anti-ontological readings all fail because they do not adequately account for the believer’s real participation in Christ’s life.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From shrinking union into one dimension of salvation → to recovering it as the comprehensive basis of participation and communion.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    “In That Day You Will Know”

    John 14:20 gives one of the clearest christological and trinitarian formulations of union: “In that day you will know that I am in my Father, and you in me, and I in you.” This is a breathtaking statement. Believers are drawn into a relational structure that has its source in the Son’s own relation to the Father. The union is not one of confusion or absorption, but of real participation. Christ remains distinct from believers, and yet believers are truly “in” Him and He in them.

    This text is decisive for the chapter because it makes communion and union inseparable. One does not commune with Christ only from outside. The believer participates in a life of indwelling. The possibility of communion therefore depends on this ontological relation.

    Vine and Branches

    John 15 extends the same logic. The branch does not merely receive instructions from the vine; it receives life from the vine. The life of the vine is shared with the branch, and only on that basis can fruit be borne. The branch’s dependence is total. This means union with Christ is not static location but living participation.

    Theologically, this image is indispensable because it reveals that the believer’s sanctification, fruit-bearing, and sustained communion all arise from union. Participation precedes and grounds transformation. The branch is not related to the vine by moral resemblance first, but by shared life.

    “That They May Be One, Even as We Are One”

    John 17 adds a further dimension. Christ prays that believers may be one in the Father and the Son, and that the love with which the Father loves the Son may be in them. Here union becomes explicitly ecclesial and doxological. It is not a private mystical reality. It forms a people. Their communion with one another is grounded in participation in the life of God through Christ.

    This is especially important for the book’s larger argument because covenant worship, pilgrimage, and ecclesial formation all depend on a communion that is corporate as well as personal. Union with Christ thus becomes the ontological ground not only of personal salvation but of the Church’s life as one body.

    Death and Resurrection with Christ

    Romans 6 and Galatians 2 are central Pauline witnesses. Believers have been baptized into Christ’s death, buried with Him, and raised to walk in newness of life. Paul can even say, “I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me.” This is strong participatory language. The believer does not merely benefit from Christ’s death at a distance. He shares in it. The old man is judged in Christ, and new life arises in union with Him.

    This is ontological precisely because it concerns existence, not merely status. The believer’s relation to sin, life, identity, and God has changed at the root. Communion is possible because the believer now exists in Christ.

    The Spirit as the Mode of Union

    Romans 8 is essential because it shows that union with Christ is actualized and lived by the Spirit. To have the Spirit of Christ is to belong to Him. The Spirit indwells believers, gives life, bears witness to adoption, and conforms them to Christ. This means union is not mechanical or impersonal. It is pneumatological and filial. The Spirit is not a substitute for Christ’s presence but the mode of His present indwelling life.

    This is a major answer to the prompt’s requirement that union be shown as participation rather than abstraction. Participation in Christ is living because it is effected by the Spirit.

    One Body in Christ

    First Corinthians 12 makes the ecclesial implication explicit: believers are one body in Christ, with many members and one Spirit. The Church is not externally organized around Christ; she is organically incorporated into Him. Ephesians 5 deepens this in nuptial and bodily terms, speaking of the Church as members of Christ’s body and linking the mystery of marriage to Christ and the Church.

    Such language confirms that union is not merely vertical relation. It is also the ontological basis of ecclesial communion. The Church shares one life because she shares one Christ.

    Hidden with Christ in God

    Colossians 3 speaks of believers as having died and their life being hidden with Christ in God. This is both participatory and eschatological. The believer already shares Christ’s life and yet awaits its full manifestation. Thus union with Christ also supports the already-and-not-yet structure explored earlier. The communion is real now and will be unveiled then.

    Participation in the Divine Nature

    Second Peter 1:4 must be handled with care, but it is too important to ignore. Believers become “partakers of the divine nature” by escaping corruption through God’s promises. Whatever caveats are necessary, this text reinforces the book’s participatory logic. Communion with God is not merely external favor. It involves real sharing in the life God gives, without collapsing Creator and creature.

    Section Summary: Scripture presents union with Christ as mutual indwelling, shared life, death-and-resurrection participation, Spirit-mediated incorporation, ecclesial unity, and real sharing in Christ’s life before God.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From seeing Christ as only external savior → to recognizing that salvation is shared through real participation in His life.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The fathers are especially rich on union because they consistently refuse to separate salvation from participation in Christ.

    Irenaeus is foundational. His doctrine of recapitulation means that Christ assumes human life to restore and gather it into Himself. Humanity is saved because it is taken up into the incarnate Son. This provides a major patristic foundation for speaking of union as ontological basis rather than secondary metaphor.

    Athanasius is equally indispensable. His famous insistence that the Son became human so that humans might be made divine must be read carefully, but it unmistakably emphasizes participation. Salvation is not only acquittal but sharing in the life of God through the incarnate Word. The believer’s communion is possible because Christ unites humanity to Himself.

    Cyril of Alexandria offers especially strong support for John 14–17 and John 15. He reads union with Christ in deeply participatory terms, often in relation to Eucharistic communion and the Spirit’s indwelling. For Cyril, the believer’s participation is real, though never absorptive. This makes him one of the strongest patristic allies for the chapter’s thesis.

    Augustine, despite differences in idiom, helps reinforce the ecclesial and Christological dimensions. Christ and His body belong together. The Church’s life is life in Christ, and love is both sign and fruit of this union.

    Maximus the Confessor integrates all of this with extraordinary depth. For him, participation in Christ heals division, restores the nous, and redirects desire toward God. Union is therefore not static metaphysical bond but transformative participation that grounds the whole ascetical and liturgical life.

    The Orthodox tradition broadly preserves this participatory instinct. Union with Christ is not treated as optional refinement but as the very heart of salvation, realized sacramentally, ecclesially, and spiritually. Theosis, when properly understood, is precisely the outworking of this union in communion.

    Section Summary: Patristic theology strongly confirms that salvation is participatory, that union with Christ is real and transformative, and that communion with God depends on sharing in Christ’s life.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From treating union as abstract doctrine → to seeing it as the fathers’ central account of how salvation becomes lived communion.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that union with Christ is the ontological basis for communion because by the Spirit believers are truly joined to the incarnate, crucified, risen, and ascended Son, and therefore share in His life, death, resurrection, access, sonship, and worship.

    Several affirmations structure this synthesis.

    First, union with Christ is participatory. It is not mere external relation or legal association, though it includes covenantal and forensic dimensions.

    Second, union with Christ is communion. To be in Christ is already to stand within the sphere of divine-human fellowship opened in Him.

    Third, union is Spirit-mediated. The Spirit is the mode by which Christ indwells and believers participate in Him.

    Fourth, union is ecclesial. The one Christ unites the many into one body, so communion with Christ cannot be severed from communion with His people.

    Fifth, union is transformative. Because believers share in Christ’s life, their minds are renewed, desires reordered, humility deepened, and obedience made possible.

    Sixth, union is eschatological. The believer’s life is already hidden with Christ in God and awaits full manifestation in glory.

    This synthesis answers the prompt’s key question directly. How is communion actually possible? It is possible because believers are not merely told about Christ, forgiven by Christ, or inspired by Christ. They are united to Him. The ontological distance between Christ’s accomplishment and the believer’s life is bridged by union. Thus participation becomes real.

    Section Summary: Union with Christ is the Spirit-mediated, ecclesial, transformative participation by which believers truly share in Christ’s life and thus enter communion with God.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From asking how Christ’s work becomes ours → to answering that it becomes ours through real union with Him.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    This chapter reshapes theological anthropology profoundly. Human beings are not saved as isolated selves receiving external divine favor only. They are reconstituted in relation to Christ. The deepest truth of Christian identity is not autonomous selfhood but participatory personhood: life in Christ.

    This means the renewed nous is not merely improved cognition. It is the mind of one whose life is in Christ. The believer thinks, perceives, and interprets reality from within a new ontological location. Likewise, desire is not transformed by moral effort alone, but because the self’s life-source has changed. What the believer loves begins to be re-ordered because he shares in the life of the Son.

    Humility is likewise grounded here. Union excludes both pride and despair. Pride is excluded because the life is not self-generated. Despair is excluded because the believer’s life is hidden with Christ. Self-offering becomes possible because the believer participates in Christ’s own self-giving life. Obedience becomes the fruit of a shared life rather than the project of a detached moral subject.

    Spiritually, the chapter teaches that prayer, worship, Scripture, and sacrament are not techniques for creating union but means of inhabiting and deepening a union already given in Christ. This gives the whole Christian life both security and dynamism.

    Section Summary: Union with Christ redefines human identity as participatory rather than autonomous, grounding renewed perception, reordered desire, humility, self-offering, and obedient life.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From seeing salvation as external improvement of the self → to seeing the self reconstituted through participation in Christ.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church exists as the body of those united to Christ. This means ecclesiology is unintelligible apart from union. The Church is not merely the gathering of those who happen to believe similar truths about Jesus. She is the communion of those who share in His life.

    Liturgically, this means the Church’s worship is the expression and deepening of union. In baptism believers are incorporated into Christ’s death and resurrection. In the Eucharist they participate in His body and blood. In the proclaimed word they hear the voice of the one in whom they live. In prayer they address the Father in the Son by the Spirit. Every major act of worship presupposes and enacts union.

    Ecclesial ethics are likewise rooted here. Because the many are one in Christ, divisions, pride, and indifference are contradictions of what the Church is. Communion among believers flows from union with Christ, and union with Christ is displayed in communion among believers.

    The eschatological dimension also matters. The Church’s union with Christ is already real and not yet fully unveiled. She shares His life now and awaits glory with Him. Thus ecclesial life is both present communion and pilgrim anticipation.

    Section Summary: The Church is the body of those united to Christ, and her liturgical, sacramental, ethical, and communal life flows from that shared participation in His life.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From treating the Church as association around Christ → to understanding her as communion in Christ.

    Patristic Witness (Top 6, SBL)

    1. Irenaeus of Lyons. Against Heresies.
      Foundational for recapitulation and participation in Christ as the mode of salvation.

    2. Athanasius. On the Incarnation.
      Essential for the participatory and transformative union made possible by the Word made flesh.

    3. Cyril of Alexandria. Commentary on John.
      Especially valuable for Spirit-mediated union and Eucharistic participation in Christ.

    4. Augustine. Tractates on the Gospel of John.
      Helpful for ecclesial union, love, and Christ-body language.

    5. Maximus the Confessor. Ambigua and Questions to Thalassius.
      Rich for transformation, participation, and the restoration of the person in Christ.

    6. Nicholas Cabasilas. The Life in Christ.
      Strong for sacramental and ecclesial participation in the life of Christ.

    Practical Outcomes

    First, this chapter helps prevent union with Christ from being treated as abstract doctrinal surplus.

    Second, it gives pastors and teachers a way to explain how salvation becomes participable and personal without collapsing into subjectivism.

    Third, it strengthens sacramental theology by grounding baptism and Eucharist in real participation in Christ.

    Fourth, it deepens ecclesiology by showing that Church unity and communion arise from shared life in Christ.

    Fifth, it supports spiritual formation by locating transformation in ontological participation rather than moralism alone.

    Sixth, it provides the theological basis for the ongoing mode of communion explored further in Chapter 25.

    Objections & Replies

    Objection 1: Does speaking of union ontologically risk confusing Creator and creature?

    Reply: No. Christian theology has always insisted that participation is real without being absorptive or dissolving distinction. Believers share in Christ’s life by grace, not by nature in a pantheistic sense.

    Objection 2: Is union just another way of talking about justification?

    Reply: Justification is one crucial aspect of salvation in Christ, but union is broader. It is the participatory reality in which justification, sanctification, adoption, and communion are all shared.

    Objection 3: Does this make subjective experience the proof of union?

    Reply: No. Union is grounded objectively in Christ and mediated by the Spirit in the Church’s life. Experience may reflect it, but does not establish it.

    Objection 4: Why insist so strongly on participation language?

    Reply: Because the biblical and patristic witness does. Without participation, communion risks becoming external and abstract rather than real and life-giving.

    Conclusion

    Communion is actually possible because believers are united to Christ. This is the chapter’s central claim and one of the most indispensable truths for the whole book. If Christ remained external to the believer, then redemption would remain a report, access a legal provision, and communion a distant hope. But because believers are in Christ and Christ is in them, what He has accomplished becomes participable. His death becomes theirs, His life becomes theirs, His access becomes theirs, and His relation to the Father becomes the ground of their communion with God.

    This means union with Christ must be understood not as theological ornament but as the ontological basis of Christian life. The believer is not simply forgiven and left outside. He is incorporated into Christ. The Church is not merely assembled around Him. She is His body. Worship is not merely directed toward Him. It is life in Him, through Him, and by His Spirit before the Father.

    The chapter also clarifies why the rest of the book’s sequence is possible. Divine speech renews the nous because believers are in the one who is the Word. Desire is transformed because believers share in His life. Humility arises because this life is received, not self-generated. Self-offering becomes possible because Christ’s self-offering life becomes normative from within. Obedience becomes communion-shaped rather than moralistically external. Worship becomes the natural fruit of life shared in Christ.

    Thus the answer to the prompt’s key question can be stated simply: communion is possible because believers are united to Christ. This union is participation, communion, and sharing in His life. It is the bridge between the accomplished work of Christ and the lived life of the Church. Everything that follows in Christian formation depends upon it. And because of it, the people of God do not merely hope that one day they might somehow draw near. In Christ, they have already begun to live the life for which they were created.
