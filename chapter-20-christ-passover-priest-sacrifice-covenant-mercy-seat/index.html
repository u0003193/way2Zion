    Chapter 20

    Christ: Passover, Priest, Sacrifice, Covenant, Mercy Seat

    Thesis

    Christ accomplishes everything necessary for communion with God because in His one person and work He fulfills what the Old Testament’s central institutions could only prefigure in differentiated and partial form. He is Passover, Priest, Sacrifice, Covenant, Temple, and Mercy Seat—not as a loose cluster of devotional images, but as one coherent Christological reality. What Exodus, Leviticus, temple theology, covenant history, priestly mediation, and sanctuary access all served in pieces, Christ accomplishes in fullness. The point of each of these institutions was never itself. Their ultimate purpose was always to bring God’s people into communion with the living God. In Christ that purpose is accomplished.

    This chapter therefore serves as a major christological synthesis within Part IV. Chapter 17 showed that the Incarnation is God’s decisive act of drawing near, making Christ the true tabernacle and temple. Chapter 18 demonstrated that Eucharistic anamnesis is the Church’s covenantal participation in His accomplished work. Chapter 19 argued that the torn veil signifies opened access into God’s presence through His sacrifice. Chapter 20 now gathers these lines into one concentrated claim: Christ fulfills in Himself the entire old covenant architecture of redemption-for-communion. He is not merely related to these realities by analogy. He is their telos and truth.

    This synthesis must be carefully handled. It must not become a list of disconnected titles for Jesus. If Christ is called Passover in one section, priest in another, sacrifice in a third, covenant in a fourth, temple in a fifth, and Mercy Seat in a sixth, yet those themes are not integrated, the chapter will fail its deepest purpose. The theological burden here is to show that these realities belong together because they all served one divine intention: access to God in communion. Passover redeems from bondage for God. Priesthood mediates holy approach. Sacrifice serves atonement and access. Covenant binds the redeemed to God in belonging and speech. Temple concentrates divine dwelling. The Mercy Seat becomes the place of meeting and divine address. In Christ these are no longer parallel strands but one fulfilled reality.

    The chapter therefore answers the prompt’s key question directly: how does Christ accomplish everything necessary for communion with God? He does so by being in His own person and work the fulfillment of every old covenant institution whose purpose was to draw humanity into divine presence. Redemption → access → communion reaches its christological center here. Christ does not merely forgive. He redeems, brings near, reconciles, speaks, nourishes, mediates, indwells, and gathers into worship. In Him the logic of the whole book becomes personal, accomplished, and open to participation.

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

    The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Exodus 12; Exodus 24; Exodus 25:17–22; Leviticus 16; Psalm 110; John 1:14; John 2:19–21; John 6:32–58; Romans 3:21–26; Romans 5:1–11; 1 Corinthians 5:7–8; 2 Corinthians 5:17–21; Ephesians 2:11–22; Hebrews 7–10; and 1 John 2:1–2. These texts are read together with the book’s wider governing passages in Numbers 7:89, Luke 24:45, Hebrews 12:22–24, and Revelation 21–22.

    Exodus 12 supplies the Passover background fulfilled in Christ. Exodus 24 establishes covenant through blood. Exodus 25:17–22 identifies the Mercy Seat as the place of meeting and speech. Leviticus 16 provides the priestly and atoning pattern fulfilled in Christ’s once-for-all work. Psalm 110 grounds priestly and royal messianic categories. John 1 and 2 identify Christ as tabernacle and temple. Romans 3 uses language that many interpreters rightly connect to hilastērion, the Mercy Seat, while Romans 5 and 2 Corinthians 5 deepen the logic of reconciliation and peace. Ephesians 2 emphasizes reconciled access. Hebrews integrates priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, sanctuary, and access. Revelation reveals the consummation of this entire movement in the dwelling of God with humanity.

    Together these texts establish that Christ is not a supplement to old covenant institutions but their personal and climactic fulfillment.

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    Part IV has been unfolding the claim that Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat reality. Yet such a claim cannot remain at the level of one isolated typological connection. The Old Testament’s sanctuary world was never constituted by one object or one rite alone. It was an integrated economy of redemption, covenant, priesthood, sacrifice, temple, and divine speech. If Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat, then He must also be shown to fulfill the wider economy in which the Mercy Seat had meaning. This chapter exists to make that synthesis explicit.

    The question at issue is therefore not whether Scripture sometimes uses these categories of Christ, but whether they can and should be held together in one coherent Christological vision. The answer must be yes if the book’s central argument is to stand. The whole structure from Part II onward has insisted that old covenant institutions served communion with God. If that is true, then Christ’s saving work cannot be explained adequately by one image only—say sacrifice or covenant or access in isolation. Rather, Christ must be shown to gather into Himself all that was necessary for communion.

    This is why the chapter is so important. Without it, the preceding chapters might be read as a series of successive theological studies rather than as elements of one deeply unified christological claim. One chapter might stress Incarnation, another Eucharist, another veil, another priesthood, and so forth. But the Christian confession is not that God gives multiple independent solutions to the human problem. It is that in His Son He gives one complete and integrated salvation. Christ fulfills all these realities because He Himself is the one in whom God’s purpose to dwell with humanity reaches accomplishment.

    The prompt rightly asks whether all Old Testament institutions ultimately exist to bring God’s people into communion. This chapter must answer that question not by theoretical assertion alone, but by showing how Christ personally fulfills each institution in a way ordered toward access and participation. The chapter therefore stands near the center of the whole project. It is one of the book’s strongest opportunities to demonstrate canonical, christological, and doctrinal coherence.

    Section Summary: The chapter asks whether the Old Testament’s central institutions can be integrated into one christological fulfillment and argues that Christ is the unified accomplishment of what each served in part.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From treating biblical types as separate christological images → to seeing them converge in one coherent fulfillment in Christ for communion.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Several inadequate approaches prevent a full christological synthesis.

    One inadequate approach isolates typologies. Christ is called Passover in one sermon, high priest in another, temple in another, and covenant mediator elsewhere, but these are handled independently, almost as separate devotional labels. Such treatment may be edifying at a basic level, but it lacks theological force. If the connections among these themes are not shown, the reader may admire each title but miss the integrated nature of Christ’s saving work.

    A second inadequate approach reduces salvation to one dominant image, most often penal or forensic categories alone. Such language is deeply biblical and must be preserved, especially in texts like Romans 3 and 2 Corinthians 5. Yet if it becomes the whole account, priestly access, temple presence, covenant belonging, and Eucharistic participation are marginalized. The result is a truncated soteriology that struggles to explain the book’s larger communion theology.

    A third inadequate approach overreacts by emphasizing participation or presence while underplaying sacrifice and atonement. This too becomes imbalanced. Communion with God cannot be spoken of as though no barrier had to be removed. The whole logic of Exodus, Leviticus, Hebrews, and the cross insists otherwise. Christ fulfills communion precisely by accomplishing atonement, not by bypassing it.

    A fourth inadequate approach treats temple and sanctuary categories as merely old covenant symbolism rendered mostly obsolete by the New Testament. But the New Testament repeatedly retrieves and intensifies them. John identifies Christ’s body as temple. Hebrews reads Christ’s work in sanctuary terms. Ephesians uses access language. Revelation culminates in fulfilled dwelling. The categories remain central because they name the very realities Christ brings to completion.

    A fifth inadequate approach speaks of communion but neglects ecclesial and worshipful participation. If Christ’s work is complete yet remains externally “true” without being liturgically or sacramentally inhabited by the Church, the movement from redemption to communion remains underdeveloped. The biblical witness insists that the Church is brought into participation, not merely informed of it.

    These inadequate readings all fail because they either isolate, reduce, or imbalance the multiple biblical strands that converge in Christ. The chapter must therefore demonstrate their deep coherence in one christological accomplishment.

    Section Summary: Isolated typologies, single-image reductions, participation without atonement, obsolete-sanctuary assumptions, and non-participatory accounts all fail to show the integrated fulfillment of God’s communion-purpose in Christ.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From partial or competing images of salvation → to a unified christological vision in which all old covenant institutions converge.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    Christ as Passover

    Passover is fulfilled in Christ because He is the Lamb through whose self-giving the people of God are redeemed from bondage, judgment, and death for communion with God. Paul’s declaration, “Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed” (1 Cor. 5:7), is not a passing metaphor. It gathers into Christ the whole exodus logic traced earlier in the book: deliverance by blood, formation of a people, memorial feast, and movement toward worshipful belonging.

    Yet Christ does not fulfill Passover narrowly. He does not simply avert judgment. He opens the new exodus that leads to access, table fellowship, and participation. In Him the people of God are brought out in order to be brought near. Thus Passover reaches its truth in the one who redeems for communion.

    Christ as Priest

    Hebrews develops with incomparable force the claim that Christ is the great high priest. But His priesthood must be understood teleologically. He is priest not simply because priesthood is a convenient biblical analogy, but because priesthood exists to mediate holy approach. Christ fulfills priesthood by representing humanity before God, entering the true sanctuary, and securing access once for all.

    This priesthood is inseparable from His person. He is not a priest functioning externally to His identity. He is the Son made flesh, the one who can mediate because He is both from God and with us. Thus priesthood in Christ becomes the personal form of reconciled access.

    Christ as Sacrifice

    Sacrifice is fulfilled in Christ because He offers Himself. Hebrews insists on the once-for-all character of this act. The blood of bulls and goats could not perfect the worshiper, but Christ’s self-offering cleanses the conscience and opens the way into the holy place. Romans 3 and 5 likewise insist that peace with God comes through His blood and death.

    Yet again, sacrifice must be read teleologically. The point is not sacrifice for its own sake. As Chapters 11 and 13 argued, sacrifice serves access, and access serves communion. Christ’s sacrifice therefore cannot be reduced to juridical transaction alone. It is the sacrifice that opens the way to God.

    Christ as Covenant

    Christ fulfills covenant because in His blood the new covenant is established. At the Last Supper He does not merely invoke covenant language decoratively; He announces that His self-offering is covenant-forming. In Him God binds His people to Himself with a permanence and inwardness Jeremiah had anticipated. The law is written on the heart, sins are remembered no more, and a people is gathered in living relation to God.

    This is why covenant cannot be separated from sacrifice or participation. The covenant is not merely declared; it is sealed in Christ’s blood and inhabited in the Church’s remembrance, worship, and access. Christ is thus both mediator and substance of the new covenant.

    Christ as Temple

    John 1 and 2 show that Christ fulfills temple theology because divine presence dwells bodily in Him. He is the true tabernacle among humanity and the true temple raised in three days. The temple existed because God desired to dwell with His people. In Christ this dwelling becomes personal and incarnate. Sacred space is not abolished into abstraction; it is concentrated in the body of the Son.

    This means temple fulfillment is integral to communion theology. If God’s goal is to dwell with humanity, then Christ must be understood as the one in whom that dwelling truly occurs. The Church then participates in temple life by union with Him.

    Christ as Mercy Seat

    Romans 3’s use of hilastērion and the whole movement of Hebrews together justify speaking of Christ as fulfillment of the Mercy Seat. The Mercy Seat was the place of meeting, speech, atonement, and holy presence. In Christ God presents the definitive atoning and meeting place. The blood is no longer brought merely before the Mercy Seat; the Son Himself becomes the one in whom atonement and access are accomplished.

    This is the climactic synthesis. Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, temple, and Mercy Seat all converge here. The whole sanctuary world aimed toward a place where God and humanity might meet in peace. Christ is that place in person.

    One Coherent Vision

    The force of the biblical witness lies precisely in this coherence. Passover without priesthood would lack sustained holy access. Priesthood without sacrifice would lack atoning entry. Sacrifice without covenant would lack communal belonging. Covenant without temple would lack concentrated divine dwelling. Temple without Mercy Seat would lack the place of reconciling meeting and speech. Christ fulfills all because He accomplishes all that is necessary for communion with God.

    Section Summary: Scripture presents Christ as the unified fulfillment of Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, temple, and Mercy Seat, each aspect serving one movement toward communion with God.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From reading these themes as parallel symbols → to seeing them integrated in Christ’s one complete and communion-opening work.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The fathers of the Church often differ in emphasis, but they are remarkably united in refusing to divide Christ’s work into disconnected functions.

    Irenaeus is especially important because his theology of recapitulation naturally gathers biblical institutions into Christ. He sees in the Son not a partial answer to one aspect of the human problem, but the one in whom humanity, covenant history, and divine purpose are summed up. This makes him an excellent patristic witness for the kind of synthesis this chapter requires.

    Athanasius also supports the chapter strongly. For him, the Incarnation, cross, victory over death, and restoration of humanity are inseparable. He does not compartmentalize Christ into isolated roles. The one Word made flesh accomplishes reconciliation, life, and restored communion in a unified work.

    Cyril of Alexandria deepens the temple and incarnational dimensions. Christ’s body is the place of divine dwelling, and through union with Him believers participate in divine life. This helps prevent the priesthood and sacrifice themes from being treated abstractly. The one who offers and mediates is also the one in whom God dwells bodily.

    John Chrysostom, especially in his preaching on Hebrews and 1 Corinthians, sustains the relation among sacrifice, priesthood, Eucharistic participation, and bold approach. He is especially useful for showing that the Church’s life flows from the unified work of Christ rather than from disconnected images.

    Maximus the Confessor offers perhaps the richest synthetic framework of all. For him Christ is the one in whom all divisions are overcome, all realities are recapitulated, and all liturgical and cosmic purposes find coherence. Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, and temple all belong in the one Logos who reconciles and unifies.

    The Orthodox liturgical tradition also bears witness to this synthesis continuously. In hymn and sacrament Christ is hailed as Lamb, Priest, Altar, Temple, Offering, and King. This is not liturgical excess. It reflects the Church’s instinct that the whole economy converges in Him.

    Section Summary: Patristic theology confirms that Christ’s saving work is one coherent fulfillment in which the many old covenant institutions are recapitulated and accomplished for communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From using multiple christological titles devotionally but separately → to confessing one integrated saving work in the incarnate Son.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that Christ fulfills Passover, Priesthood, Sacrifice, Covenant, Temple, and Mercy Seat because all of these institutions existed to accomplish what humanity could not achieve on its own: communion with God. In Christ, therefore, the entire old covenant architecture of access is not merely continued, but fulfilled in personal and definitive form.

    Several affirmations define this synthesis.

    First, Christ is Passover because He redeems from bondage and judgment for God.

    Second, Christ is Priest because He mediates holy approach and represents humanity before the Father.

    Third, Christ is Sacrifice because He offers Himself once for all to remove the barrier of sin and open the way.

    Fourth, Christ is Covenant because in His blood God binds His people to Himself in permanent belonging and renewed heart-life.

    Fifth, Christ is Temple because the fullness of deity dwells bodily in Him and in Him God is with us.

    Sixth, Christ is Mercy Seat because in Him atonement, meeting, access, and divine speech are fulfilled together.

    These are not six unrelated accomplishments. They are six dimensions of one complete work. Christ accomplishes everything necessary for communion by being in Himself the one who redeems, mediates, offers, binds, dwells, and reconciles. This is why the chapter serves as a major christological synthesis. It shows that the Old Testament institutions are teleologically ordered toward communion and christologically fulfilled in one person.

    Section Summary: The chapter resolves its central question by showing that all the old covenant institutions served communion and are personally and definitively fulfilled in Christ.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From multiple incomplete institutions → to one complete Savior in whom all necessary realities for communion are accomplished.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    This chapter clarifies the human problem and the human destiny in a particularly comprehensive way. Humanity is not merely guilty, though it is guilty. It is also enslaved, estranged, impure, alienated, disordered, and unable to sustain divine communion on its own. This is why no single image of salvation can suffice by itself. Human need is too deep and too many-sided. Christ fulfills every institution necessary because human beings need everything those institutions served.

    This means the human vocation to communion is restored in Christ comprehensively. He does not merely pardon sinners and leave them outside. He redeems them like Passover, brings them near like priesthood, cleanses them like sacrifice, binds them like covenant, indwells them like temple, and reconciles them like the Mercy Seat. The anthropology of the book is therefore strengthened here: humanity is restored not by partial repair but by total reorientation toward life in God.

    The nous is renewed because Christ is not only sacrifice but divine speech and temple presence. Desire is transformed because the heart is given a new object of love and a new environment of communion. Humility is formed because every aspect of access is received, not produced. Self-offering becomes intelligible because the believer participates in the self-offering of Christ. Obedience becomes grateful rather than legalistic because it arises within covenantal belonging and opened access.

    Spiritually, this chapter teaches that the Christian life is irreducibly christological and participatory. No one comes to God by extracting spiritual benefits from Christ while remaining outside Him. One must be gathered into Him, the one in whom all access is accomplished. The life of communion is therefore life in Christ, from Christ, and through Christ.

    Section Summary: Christ’s comprehensive fulfillment shows that humanity needs full restoration into communion, and that the renewed life of mind, desire, humility, and worship depends entirely on participation in Him.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From imagining the human problem as one-dimensional → to seeing Christ’s many-sided fulfillment as answering humanity’s full need for communion.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church exists as the people gathered into Christ’s comprehensive fulfillment of the old covenant institutions. This means ecclesial life is not built around one isolated benefit of the gospel, but around the whole christological reality of redemption, access, covenant, worship, and communion.

    In worship the Church participates in Christ as Passover, remembering and receiving the redemption that brought her out for God. She participates in His covenant through Eucharistic anamnesis. She approaches the Father through His priestly mediation. She stands within His temple reality as His body. She draws near through His sacrifice. She lives from the Mercy Seat fulfilled in Him. Christian liturgy is therefore a dense participation in Christ’s total saving identity.

    This also deepens ecclesiology. The Church is not merely an association of the pardoned. She is the temple-people, covenant-people, Eucharistic people, priestly people, and pilgrim people of the new exodus gathered into the Son. Her worship is not a mere reminder of absent truths but participation in the accomplished work of the present Christ.

    The eschatological dimension remains essential. The Church participates now in what will one day be unveiled in full. Revelation 21–22 shows the destination: no temple because God and the Lamb are its temple; no curse because atonement’s work has reached consummation; full dwelling because communion is complete. The Church’s worship is thus both remembrance and anticipation, grounded in Christ’s accomplished fulfillment and stretched toward Zion consummated.

    Section Summary: The Church lives from Christ’s total fulfillment of Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, temple, and Mercy Seat by participating liturgically and communally in His accomplished work.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From defining the Church by one aspect of salvation only → to seeing her as the community gathered into Christ’s complete fulfillment of all that is necessary for communion.

    2. Athanasius. On the Incarnation.
      Essential for the unified redemptive significance of the Incarnation, cross, and restored life.

    3. Cyril of Alexandria. Commentary on John and On the Unity of Christ.
      Valuable for temple Christology, incarnation, and participation.

    4. John Chrysostom. Homilies on Hebrews and Homilies on 1 Corinthians.

    5. Maximus the Confessor. Ambigua and Mystagogy.
      A profound source for the integrative and participatory vision of Christ’s fulfillment.

    6. Nicholas Cabasilas. The Life in Christ.
      Helpful for how the Church actually participates in Christ’s fulfilled work sacramentally and ecclesially.

    Practical Outcomes

    First, this chapter helps prevent truncated presentations of the gospel by showing that Christ’s work cannot be reduced to one image alone.

    Second, it provides pastors and teachers with a coherent framework for reading Old Testament institutions christologically.

    Third, it deepens sacramental and liturgical theology by locating worship within Christ’s complete fulfillment of access.

    Fourth, it strengthens ecclesiology by framing the Church as the community gathered into all that Christ has accomplished.

    Fifth, it clarifies discipleship by showing that obedience and worship arise within participation in Christ rather than from external moral demand alone.

    Sixth, it provides a strong conceptual bridge to the following chapter on resurrection, ascension, heavenly enthronement, and Lord’s Day worship.

    Objections & Replies

    Objection 1: Does this synthesis flatten important distinctions among the old covenant institutions?

    Reply: No. It preserves their distinct functions while showing that those functions served one divine purpose and therefore converge naturally in Christ.

    Objection 2: Might calling Christ all these things at once produce theological overload?

    Reply: Only if the themes are treated independently. The point of the chapter is precisely that they belong together because communion with God requires all that they served.

    Objection 3: Does this overemphasize continuity with the Old Testament?

    Reply: The continuity is biblical and christologically governed. Fulfillment is not repetition but completion. Christ transcends precisely by fulfilling.

    Objection 4: If Christ fulfills everything, what role remains for the Church?

    Reply: The Church does not add to Christ’s accomplishment. She participates in it, receives it, proclaims it, and is formed by it in worship and life.

    Conclusion

    Christ accomplishes everything necessary for communion with God because in Him all the institutions of access find their fulfillment. Passover, priesthood, sacrifice, covenant, temple, and Mercy Seat were never ends in themselves. They existed because humanity needed redemption, mediation, atonement, belonging, divine dwelling, and reconciled meeting with God. In the old covenant these realities appeared in differentiated forms. In Christ they become one accomplished reality.

    This is why the chapter must be read as a christological synthesis rather than a catalogue. Christ is not simply compared to many old covenant realities. He is the one in whom their common purpose is fulfilled. He redeems like Passover, mediates like priesthood, offers Himself as sacrifice, seals the covenant in His blood, embodies the temple in His flesh, and becomes the true place of atoning meeting and divine speech as the fulfilled Mercy Seat. The many become one in Him because God’s purpose was always one: communion.

    This synthesis also clarifies the whole book’s theological movement. Redemption → access → communion is not a chain of abstract stages. It is the living work of Christ. He does not merely remove one problem and leave others unaddressed. He accomplishes all that is necessary for humanity to be brought near to God. The Church therefore does not live from a partial gospel. She lives from a complete Christ.

    For worship, theology, and spiritual formation this is decisive. The Eucharist, prayer, obedience, humility, renewed desire, and communal life of the Church all depend on the fact that Christ has fulfilled every old covenant institution necessary for communion. The believer need not seek another Passover, another priest, another sacrifice, another covenant, another temple, or another Mercy Seat. In Christ, God has provided all.

    Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question may be stated plainly: Christ accomplishes everything necessary for communion with God by being Himself the complete fulfillment of every institution God once gave to open the way to His presence. In Him the whole architecture of Scripture becomes personal, unified, and accessible. Everything before led here. Everything after proceeds from here. And because of Him, the people of God may now live not merely as the forgiven, but as the redeemed, covenanted, priestly, worshiping communion of the living God.
