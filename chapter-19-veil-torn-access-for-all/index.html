    Chapter 19

    Veil Torn: Access for All

    Thesis

    The tearing of the veil at Christ’s death signifies the opening of access to God’s presence through the once-for-all sacrifice of the Son. It is not a dramatic ornament attached to the crucifixion narrative, nor simply a symbolic statement that “religion has changed,” nor merely the removal of a ceremonial barrier. It is the decisive revelation that the barrier between sinful humanity and the holy presence of God has been overcome in Christ, and that the purpose of this opened access is communion. The veil is torn not so that access might stand alone as an abstract theological achievement, but so that redeemed humanity may draw near to God in reconciled fellowship, priestly confidence, and worshipful participation.

    This chapter argues that Matthew 27:51, Hebrews 9–10, and Ephesians 2 together reveal the heart of Christ’s priestly accomplishment: sacrifice serves access, access serves communion. The barrier Christ removes is not only legal guilt in isolation, though guilt is real; nor only ritual distance, though ritual distance is involved; nor only Jew-Gentile division, though that too is addressed. The barrier is the whole complex of sin, uncleanness, alienation, hostility, and sanctuary distance that prevents humanity from dwelling before God. Christ removes this barrier by His sacrificial death, priestly mediation, and ascended entrance into the heavenly sanctuary.

    This matters enormously for the book’s central movement. Earlier chapters established that the sanctuary existed because God desired to meet and speak with His people. The Day of Atonement showed that sacrifice and priesthood served access to that meeting place. Chapters 17 and 18 then argued that in Christ God has drawn near in person and given the Church Eucharistic participation in His accomplished work. Chapter 19 now presses the question of opened access explicitly. What barrier did Christ remove? He removed the barrier that kept humanity outside the Holy of Holies of communion. He did so not as an end in itself, but so that communion might become possible again in fullness.

    This chapter must therefore avoid two distortions. It must not reduce access to forgiveness alone, as though the Christian life stopped at acquittal. Nor must it treat access as if it were already the final good. Access matters because God is there. The torn veil means the living God is now approached through Christ. The heavenly sanctuary is not empty sacred space; it is the sphere of divine presence, priestly mediation, and covenant communion. Christ opens the way so that humanity may draw near, hear, worship, and participate in the life of God. In that sense, the torn veil is one of the clearest new covenant manifestations of the whole book’s governing claim: redemption is ordered toward communion.

    Biblical Ground (Primary Texts)

    The primary biblical texts for this chapter include Matthew 27:45–54; Mark 15:33–39; Luke 23:44–49; Hebrews 4:14–16; Hebrews 6:19–20; Hebrews 9:1–15, 24–28; Hebrews 10:19–25; Ephesians 2:11–22; Leviticus 16; Exodus 26:31–35; and Revelation 21:22–27. These texts are read together with the whole sanctuary pattern already traced through Exodus 25:17–22, Numbers 7:89, and the preceding chapters of the book.

    Matthew 27:51 provides the key narrative sign: the veil of the temple is torn from top to bottom at the death of Jesus. Hebrews gives the essential theological interpretation, identifying Christ as the great high priest who enters the true sanctuary and opens “a new and living way” through the veil, that is, through His flesh. Ephesians 2 adds the ecclesial and reconciliatory dimension, showing that Christ abolishes hostility and grants access in one Spirit to the Father. Leviticus 16 provides the old covenant background of restricted priestly access into the Most Holy Place. Revelation 21 then reveals the consummation toward which this opened access tends: the city where there is no temple because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple.

    These texts together establish that Christ’s sacrificial death removes the barrier to God’s presence and opens access for communion.

    I. Context and Question at Issue

    Part IV of the book is showing how Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat reality. Chapter 17 argued that the Incarnation is God’s decisive act of drawing near, making Christ the true tabernacle, temple, Bethel, and Emmanuel. Chapter 18 argued that Eucharistic anamnesis is the Church’s covenantal participation in Christ’s accomplished work. The present chapter now asks how access itself is opened. If Christ is the true dwelling and the Church participates in His work, what exactly has changed in the relation between humanity and the divine presence? The answer is concentrated in the image and theology of the torn veil.

    This question is indispensable because sanctuary theology reaches one of its sharpest points at the veil. The veil marked the boundary of the Most Holy Place, the barrier protecting sinful humanity from presumptuous nearness to divine glory. Under the old covenant, access was real but restricted, holy, priestly, and carefully mediated. The very existence of the veil declared both truths at once: God desired to dwell among His people, and sinful humanity could not simply enter on its own terms. Thus the veil embodied the tension between communion and holiness.

    The tearing of that veil at Christ’s death must therefore be read with great theological seriousness. It is not enough to say generically that “the old system ended” or that “now everyone can come to God.” Both claims are true in some sense, but they are too thin if left unqualified. What ended was not holiness. What changed was access. What opened was not mere religious freedom. What opened was the way into God’s presence through the sacrifice and priesthood of Christ. The barrier removed was whatever kept humanity outside the sphere of communion.

    The key question of the prompt is thus exactly right: what barrier did Christ remove? The answer must be given in relation to sanctuary, priesthood, sin, hostility, alienation, and divine presence. The chapter must also make clear that access is not self-justifying. It serves communion. To be granted entrance to the Holy of Holies is to be granted entrance to the place where God is, where speech is heard, where worship is offered, and where a people is formed in fellowship with Him.

    Section Summary: The chapter asks what changed when the veil was torn and argues that Christ removed the sanctuary barrier that kept humanity from holy communion with God.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From speaking vaguely of “new access” → to understanding the torn veil as the decisive opening of the way into God’s presence through Christ.

    II. Prior Answers and Their Inadequacy

    Several inadequate readings of the torn veil and opened access obscure the full force of the biblical witness.

    One inadequate reading reduces the torn veil to a bare symbol of religious change. The old order ends, the new begins, and the interpreter is satisfied. Yet such a reading says too little. The veil was not arbitrary religious décor. It was the boundary of the Most Holy Place, charged with the meaning of holiness, danger, priestly restriction, and sanctuary access. Its tearing therefore signals something far more precise and profound than generic change.

    A second inadequate reading collapses access into forgiveness. Certainly Christ’s death brings forgiveness, and forgiveness is indispensable. But if access is treated as only another way of describing acquittal, the sanctuary logic is lost. Hebrews insists on more. The point is not merely that sins are pardoned, but that believers draw near, enter with confidence, and approach the heavenly holy place through Christ. Forgiveness matters because it opens communion.

    A third inadequate reading treats access as an end in itself. Here one may say truly that Christ opened the veil, yet stop at the doorway. But access to what? The answer is the presence of God. If access is not ordered toward communion, the chapter’s theological movement stalls. Scripture never presents holy access as empty privilege. It is always access to God.

    A fourth inadequate reading interprets Ephesians 2 only in sociological or horizontal terms. Certainly the breaking down of the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile is central. But Paul does not stop there. He says that through Christ both have access in one Spirit to the Father. The vertical and horizontal belong together. Christ removes hostility because He brings a reconciled people into the presence of God.

    A fifth inadequate reading spiritualizes heavenly sanctuary language such that priestly access becomes a figure for inward comfort only. While assurance is important, Hebrews speaks concretely about Christ’s priesthood, heavenly holy place, and the Church’s bold approach. The sanctuary logic must not be dissolved into subjectivity. Christ’s mediatorial work really opens communion.

    These inadequate readings all fail because they stop too soon or narrow the meaning of the texts. The chapter therefore seeks to recover the full biblical pattern: sacrifice → access → communion.

    Section Summary: Symbolic-only, forgiveness-only, access-as-end, horizontal-only, and over-spiritualized readings fail because they do not account fully for Christ’s opening of the way into God’s presence.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From reducing the torn veil to generic religious symbolism → to reading it as the climactic opening of sanctuary communion in Christ.

    III. The Scriptural Event or Claim

    The Veil Torn at the Death of Jesus

    Matthew 27:51 states with arresting simplicity: “And behold, the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom.” Mark and Luke preserve the same event. Several details are crucial. The tearing occurs at the death of Jesus. It moves from top to bottom, indicating divine initiative. And it concerns the temple veil, the boundary of the holy place. The event therefore interprets the cross. Christ’s death is not simply martyrdom or moral example. It opens what had been closed.

    Theologically, this means that the death of Christ is priestly and sanctuary-oriented. The Gospel narrative itself gives a sign that the barrier surrounding the Holy of Holies has been breached by divine action. This is not because holiness no longer matters, but because the way to holiness is being opened in and through the sacrifice of Christ.

    Leviticus 16 and Restricted Access

    Leviticus 16 supplies the essential old covenant background. The high priest enters the Most Holy Place only once a year, with blood, and only under strict divine command. The veil protects holy presence from casual approach. The sanctuary’s very structure teaches that communion is both desired by God and impossible without mediation. The annual entry dramatizes the unfinished and provisional character of old covenant access.

    This is vital for understanding what Christ removes. He does not remove the reality of divine holiness. He removes the barrier that sin and old covenant incompletion imposed between humanity and holy presence. The veil represents restricted access because of sin; its tearing proclaims access opened because of Christ.

    Hebrews 9–10: Christ in the Heavenly Sanctuary

    Hebrews provides the definitive theological account. Christ is the high priest of the good things to come, who enters the greater and more perfect tent not made with hands. He enters once for all into the holy places by His own blood. Hebrews then states that believers have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, “by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh.”

    This language is astonishingly rich. Christ is sacrifice, priest, and opened way all at once. The veil is interpreted in relation to His flesh. His self-offering opens access to the heavenly sanctuary. The once-for-all sacrifice does not merely conclude guilt; it opens communion. The Church is therefore summoned to draw near with true hearts in full assurance of faith.

    This is the clearest scriptural statement of the pattern at the heart of the chapter: sacrifice → access → communion. The logic is priestly, sanctuary-centered, and profoundly relational.

    Ephesians 2: Access and Reconciliation

    Ephesians 2 adds a complementary and indispensable dimension. Christ is our peace. He has broken down the dividing wall of hostility and reconciled both Jew and Gentile in one body through the cross. But Paul’s climax is not merely social unity. “Through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father.” This sentence makes clear that access is trinitarian, ecclesial, and communion-oriented.

    The barrier Christ removes therefore includes both vertical alienation from God and horizontal hostility within humanity. The torn veil and broken wall belong together. Christ creates one new humanity by bringing the many into shared access to the Father. Thus access serves communion not only individually but corporately.

    Christ as Fulfillment of Day of Atonement

    The Day of Atonement logic from Leviticus 16 is not abolished but fulfilled in Christ. He does not repeat the high priest’s annual entry; He fulfills it once for all. He does not bring the blood of another; He offers Himself. He does not enter the copy only; He enters the heavenly reality. This means Christ is the final mediator of holy approach.

    The relevance to the prompt is direct. The chapter must show Christ as fulfillment of the Day of Atonement, and Hebrews does exactly that. The barrier removed is the restricted, provisional, sin-conditioned distance between humanity and God, addressed annually in shadow and now finally overcome in Christ’s priestly sacrifice.

    Access to What?

    It is essential to ask again: access to what? Hebrews answers: to the holy places, to the presence of God. Ephesians answers: to the Father. The point is not empty sacred entry. It is communion. The Church enters because God is there, because Christ mediates there, and because divine presence is the final good. Access has no independent meaning apart from the God whose presence it opens.

    Eschatological Fulfillment

    Revelation 21 sharpens the final perspective. In the new Jerusalem there is no temple, for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple. This does not mean temple theology has vanished into irrelevance. It means the access for which the temple existed has reached fulfillment. The torn veil points forward to the day when nothing of curse, impurity, or exclusion remains. Access becomes unveiled communion.

    Section Summary: Scripture shows that Christ’s death tears the veil, fulfills the Day of Atonement, removes hostility, and opens the way into God’s presence for reconciled communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From understanding the cross mainly as forgiveness event → to seeing it as the priestly opening of the Holy of Holies for the people of God.

    IV. Patristic Reading and Insight

    The fathers read the tearing of the veil and Christ’s priestly work in ways that strongly reinforce the chapter’s central argument.

    Athanasius understands Christ’s death and resurrection not merely as legal resolution but as the decisive overthrow of death and corruption for the sake of restored life with God. Though he does not frame everything in the exact language of Hebrews, his participatory vision helps clarify why opened access matters: the Son restores humanity to the divine life from which it had fallen.

    Cyril of Alexandria is especially helpful because of his temple and Incarnation theology. For Cyril, Christ’s flesh is not incidental to redemption but the very mode in which divine life becomes communicable. This supports Hebrews’ statement that access is opened through the veil, that is, His flesh. The Incarnate One opens the way in and through the humanity He has assumed.

    John Chrysostom’s homilies on Hebrews are particularly important. He is attentive both to the once-for-allness of Christ’s offering and to the boldness granted believers in approaching God. He refuses both a flattening of priesthood and a reduction of access to abstraction. His reading supports the claim that Christ’s priestly work opens real communion, not merely legal possibility.

    Gregory of Nyssa’s theology of ascent also remains relevant. For him, purification and approach serve deeper participation in God. This helps interpret veil tearing not as collapse of holiness but as opened ascent through the crucified and risen Christ.

    Maximus the Confessor integrates all of this within a cosmic and participatory framework. Christ’s work reconciles divisions and opens creation to its true end in God. Access is therefore not merely juridical admission, but the restoration of humanity’s path into divine communion. The barrier removed is whatever holds creation in alienation from its telos.

    Orthodox liturgical tradition likewise sustains a vivid sense that Christ has opened the way to the Holy of Holies. The Church prays and worships as those admitted into heavenly things through the High Priest. The liturgy is full of access language because communion, not bare acquittal, is its aim.

    Section Summary: Patristic theology confirms that Christ’s death opens real priestly access to divine life and presence, preserving holiness while making communion possible.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From seeing access as a doctrinal abstraction → to understanding it as living entry into the presence of God through the incarnate and priestly Christ.

    V. Doctrinal Synthesis (Resolution)

    The doctrinal resolution of this chapter is that Christ’s cross opens access to God’s presence by removing the sanctuary barrier created by sin, hostility, and old covenant restriction, and that this access exists for the sake of communion. Sacrifice serves access. Access serves communion.

    This synthesis includes several affirmations.

    First, the barrier removed by Christ is real and multi-dimensional. It includes sin’s guilt, impurity, alienation, exclusion from holy presence, and the hostility that fractures humanity. The veil and the dividing wall together help name this reality.

    Second, Christ removes this barrier through sacrifice. His death is priestly and atoning, not merely exemplary. The torn veil interprets the cross as access-opening sacrifice.

    Third, Christ is mediator. He does not merely create a principle of access; He Himself is the way into the Father’s presence. Hebrews’ veil-through-his-flesh language makes this explicit.

    Fourth, access is heavenly sanctuary access. The new and living way is not merely psychological reassurance. It is entry into the true holy place where Christ ministers.

    Fifth, access is not the final end. It serves communion with the Father, shared by the one body of Christ in the Spirit. This guards against turning access into self-enclosed religious privilege.

    Sixth, Christ fulfills the Day of Atonement once for all. The annual provisional access of the old covenant is surpassed in His definitive entrance and opened way.

    This doctrinal synthesis is essential to the book’s broader movement. If Christ fulfills the Mercy Seat, then His work must not stop at canceled debt. It must reach holy presence, divine speech, worship, and participation. The torn veil is therefore one of the central signs that redemption has become access and access is meant to become communion.

    Section Summary: Christ’s cross removes the real barrier of sin-conditioned exclusion from God and opens priestly, heavenly, and communal access that exists for communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From saying only that Christ forgives sinners → to confessing that He opens the Holy of Holies for shared life with God.

    VI. Anthropological and Spiritual Implications

    This chapter has profound anthropological implications because it shows what human beings most deeply need. They need more than pardon. They need restored access to God. Sin leaves humanity not only guilty but outside, estranged, and incapable of inhabiting divine presence. The torn veil speaks directly to this condition. What Christ removes is not merely moral stain as isolated defect, but the whole exclusionary condition of fallen humanity before the Holy One.

    This clarifies why noetic renewal and transformed desire depend upon access. Divine speech renews the nous, but only those brought near can dwell under that speech in its covenantal fullness. Access is therefore the condition for the deeper interior transformations the book later explores. The person must first be brought into God’s presence if he is to hear rightly, desire rightly, and worship rightly.

    The chapter also clarifies humility. Access is given, not taken. The believer approaches with boldness, but not with presumption. The torn veil therefore forms a paradoxical humility: confidence without arrogance, nearness without self-authorization, reverence without despair. One stands before God only because Christ has opened the way.

    Desire is also reshaped by this doctrine. The human heart often imagines that what it needs is autonomy, vindication, or internal peace on its own terms. The torn veil declares that what humanity truly needs is God Himself. Access teaches desire to seek presence rather than self-possession. It reorients longing from spiritual independence to communion.

    Spiritually, the chapter warns against treating Christian life as though its highest benefit were merely being “forgiven and left alone.” Forgiveness matters because it restores entrance into fellowship. Prayer, worship, Eucharist, and ecclesial life all presuppose this opened access. The Christian life is therefore life before God, not merely life after acquittal.

    Section Summary: The torn veil reveals that human beings need restored entrance into God’s presence, and this given access forms humility, reorients desire, and grounds all deeper renewal.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From thinking salvation gives only pardon → to understanding it as opened life before the living God.

    VII. Ecclesial and Liturgical Location

    The Church lives as the community of opened access. She gathers not as those standing outside the sanctuary hoping to be admitted, but as those brought near by the blood of Christ. This gives worship its distinctive new covenant boldness. The Church prays, praises, intercedes, confesses, and communes because the veil has been torn and the High Priest has entered on her behalf.

    This means liturgy is an enactment of access. The Church’s worship is not merely memory of a past saving event but present entry into the communion Christ has opened. The Eucharist, prayer, proclamation, confession, and blessing all belong to a people who have access in one Spirit to the Father. Chapter 18 showed how anamnesis participates in Christ’s accomplished work; Chapter 19 now clarifies that this participation is possible because access has truly been opened.

    Ecclesially, the Church is also the reconciled people of Ephesians 2. Jew and Gentile, once divided, are brought near and made into one new humanity. Access is therefore corporate. The Church is one because it approaches one Father through one Christ in one Spirit.

    Liturgically and sacramentally, the Church’s life remains priestly in a derivative and participatory sense. She does not replace Christ’s priesthood; she shares in its fruits. Her worship is bold because His access is secure. Her holiness is real because His mediation is living. Her communion is possible because the barrier is removed.

    Eschatologically, the Church’s life of access anticipates the unveiled presence of Revelation 21–22. The temple is no longer needed because the Lord God and the Lamb are its temple. The torn veil points forward to that end: not endless entry rites, but communion without exclusion. The Church worships now on the way to that consummation.

    Section Summary: The Church is the community of opened access, gathered in Christ’s priestly nearness to the Father and formed liturgically as one reconciled body on the way to unveiled communion.

    Before → After Frame Shift: From treating worship as approach from outside → to seeing the Church as already admitted into God’s presence through Christ.

    Practical Outcomes

    First, this chapter helps preachers and teachers explain why forgiveness is not the end of salvation but the beginning of restored access to God.

    Second, it clarifies temple and veil imagery in the passion narratives, preventing superficial readings.

    Third, it strengthens Eucharistic and liturgical theology by grounding worship in opened priestly access.

    Fourth, it deepens ecclesiology by showing that the Church is the reconciled and admitted people of God.

    Fifth, it teaches believers to approach God with reverent boldness rather than either presumption or despair.

    Sixth, it prepares for the following chapters on priesthood, heavenly worship, and participation by making clear that communion depends upon Christ’s opened way.

    Objections & Replies

    Objection 1: Doesn’t this chapter risk making access more important than communion?

    Reply: No. The chapter explicitly argues that access serves communion. Access has no independent end; it matters because it opens the way to God Himself.

    Objection 2: Is the torn veil too slender a narrative sign to bear this much theological weight?

    Reply: Not when read in light of Leviticus 16 and Hebrews 9–10. The New Testament itself interprets sanctuary and veil language with profound theological seriousness.

    Objection 3: Does emphasizing heavenly sanctuary theology make salvation too remote or abstract?

    Reply: No. Hebrews uses heavenly sanctuary language precisely to ground the Church’s present confidence, gathered worship, and real access to the Father through Christ.

    Objection 4: If Christ removed the barrier, why does holiness still matter?

    Reply: Because the barrier removed is exclusion from holy presence, not the holiness of God itself. Christ opens the way into holiness; He does not abolish it.

    Conclusion

    The veil was torn because the barrier to God’s presence had been decisively addressed in Christ. This is the chapter’s central claim and one of the Gospel’s most profound announcements. At the cross, Christ did not merely suffer, forgive, or inspire. He opened the way. The old covenant sign of restricted access was ruptured from top to bottom by divine initiative, declaring that the Holy of Holies was no longer closed to the people of God through the priestly sacrifice of the Son.

    Yet the opening of access is not the final end. It serves communion. This is why Hebrews moves so quickly from Christ’s sacrifice to confidence in entering the holy places and to the summons, “let us draw near.” Access matters because God is there. The Father is there. The living Christ ministers there. Worship rises there. The Church is gathered there. The barrier removed is therefore whatever kept humanity outside the life for which it was made: sin, impurity, hostility, exclusion, and sanctuary distance.

    This chapter advances the book’s larger argument by clarifying the relation between sacrifice, access, and communion. The cross is not reducible to forgiveness alone. It opens the sanctuary. The torn veil is not a dramatic flourish. It is a theological sign that redemption has become access and access is meant to become communion. Ephesians confirms this by showing that the one new humanity has access in one Spirit to the Father. Hebrews confirms it by showing that the once-for-all sacrifice opens a new and living way through Christ’s flesh. Revelation confirms it by pointing toward the city where no temple is needed because communion is finally unbroken.

    Thus the answer to the chapter’s key question may be stated simply: Christ removed the barrier that kept humanity from God’s holy presence. He removed it not so that humanity might simply stand at the threshold admiring an opened door, but so that humanity might enter, draw near, worship, hear, and live in communion with the living God. The torn veil is therefore one of the clearest signs that the Mercy Seat reality is fulfilled in Christ. What was once guarded, restricted, and annually approached in shadow is now opened in Him for the Church and, in the end, for the new creation itself.
